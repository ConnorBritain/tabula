Role of the ballot (ROB) and role of the judge

7 min read · Advanced

A role of the ballot, or ROB, is an argument about what the judge's vote should reward.

  • What the ballot is: At the end of every round, the judge fills out a ballot that names one team as the winner.
  • How teams use it: One team might say the ballot goes to the team with the better policy. The other might say it goes to the team that best challenges a harmful idea.
  • Role of the judge: A related argument says what kind of decision-maker the judge should be.
  • Where it comes up: These claims come up most in kritik and framework debates.

What a ROB claim says

A ROB claim usually has one sentence and a reason. The sentence often starts: "The role of the ballot is to vote for the team that best..." Some common shapes:

ROB (generic)What it rewardsOften run by
"...the team with the best policy option"The plan or counterplan with the best resultsPolicy affs, against a kritik
"...the team that best challenges harmful ways of thinking"The better critique of ideasNegs running a kritik, or kritikal affs
"...the team that does the most to make debate a better space"Effects on the activity and the people in itTeams arguing about debate itself
"...the team with the better argument, judged by the flow"Technical skill on the flowTeams who want a neutral test

Each one tells the judge what matters most. Under the first, a kritik counts only if it shows the plan is a bad policy. Under the second, the plan's results may count for less than the ideas behind it.

Role of the judge

The role of the judge (ROJ) is a claim about what kind of decision-maker the judge should be. Common examples:

  • Policymaker: the judge acts like a lawmaker, choosing the best policy.
  • Educator: the judge rewards what teaches the most.
  • Critic of argument: the judge rewards the best-argued position, whatever kind it is.
  • Intellectual or scholar: the judge weighs ideas the way a careful reader would.

A team's ROJ claim is not the same as the judge's own paradigm, the judge's written statement of how they decide rounds. A paradigm says what the judge does by default. A ROJ claim asks the judge to take a certain role in this round. See Judge paradigms.

How ROB relates to framework

Framework is the debate about how the judge should decide. See Framework. The ROB and framework are closely tied, and judges see the link in different ways.

  • Many judges see the ROB as part of framework: framework sets the method, and the ROB names the goal.
  • Some judges treat the ROB as a separate question to decide first, before anything else.
  • Others see the ROB as framework under a different name, and judge it the same way.

Whatever the label, a team should explain how its ROB changes what the judge weighs. A ROB that changes nothing in the decision does little work.

What makes a ROB argument strong

  • It is clear. It fits in one sentence, and the judge can apply it.
  • It has reasons. It says why this is the right goal for the round: fairness, education, or the effect of the judge's decision.
  • It connects to the round. It tells the judge which arguments count, and how much.
  • It can be met by both sides. This is a common point of debate.

A frequent objection is that a ROB is self-serving: only the team that wrote it could ever win under it. The team that offered the ROB often replies that the other side could win too, for example by defending its plan without the criticized idea. It may also argue that some goals matter more than balance between the teams. Judges differ on how much this objection weighs. Teams should argue it, and judges should decide from what is argued.

Can the ballot do anything?

Teams also debate what a ballot achieves.

  • One view: the ballot only decides who won this round. Its effect outside the room is very small, so claims that the ballot changes the world deserve doubt.
  • Another view: the ballot is an endorsement. What judges reward shapes what debaters learn and what arguments they make next time. So the ballot matters, even if its effect is local.

Both views are common among judges. The answer often matters for alternatives that happen "in the round." See The alternative.

How to answer a ROB

  • Offer a counter-ROB: a different goal for the ballot, with reasons.
  • Show it is arbitrary or self-serving: ask, "What would the other team have to do to win under your ROB?" A weak answer is useful.
  • Win under their ROB too: show that your side meets their goal anyway.
  • Weigh: explain why your ROB is better for fairness, for education, or for both.

How to judge the role of the ballot

  1. Find each team's ROB and ROJ claims. Write them in one sentence each.
  2. Decide which one won. Use the reasons and answers the teams gave.
  3. Apply it. List which arguments count under the winning ROB, then decide the round with them.
  4. Check "either way" arguments. If one team wins under both ROBs, the ROB may not decide the round.
  5. If no one argued a ROB, use your normal approach, and state it in your paradigm.
  6. Explain it. In the reason for decision, say which ROB you used and why. See The ballot and the RFD.

  ROJ: judge is an educator

  2. Self-serving: only neg

2NR

     CP or DA; both sides can
     win
  2. They never showed how;
     our 1AC uses threat cards
  3. (no answer)

How to read it: point 1 is the main ROB clash. The 2AR argues that its ROB lets both sides win, since the neg could win with a counterplan or a disadvantage. On point 2, the 2NR says the aff could win under the neg's ROB by defending the plan without threat framing. The 2AR replies that the neg never showed how, because the 1AC relies on threat cards. On point 3, the 2AR does not answer the 2NR's claim that the judge is an educator. That helps the neg, but the judge must still decide how much the dropped point matters next to the ROB clash in points 1 and 2.

Common mistakes

  • Both: a ROB with no reasons. Fix: say why this is the right goal for the ballot, not only what the goal is.
  • Both: winning the ROB, then not applying it. Fix: tell the judge which arguments now count, and how they decide the round.
  • Neg: a ROB not tied to the K. Fix: show how your link and alternative meet your own ROB.
  • Aff: ignoring the neg's ROB. Fix: offer a counter-ROB, answer the reasons, and show you win under theirs too if you can.
  • Both: no answer to "self-serving." Fix: explain how the other team could win under your ROB.
  • Judges: swapping in your own role when the teams argued one. Fix: decide the ROB as argued, and use your default only if no one argued it.

Under a policy ROB

The aff wins this role of the ballot (ROB): "Vote for the team with the best policy option." The neg's kritik links only to the aff's words. The neg never argues that the plan's results would be worse.

What does this ROB change about how the kritik counts?

  • The kritik counts only if it shows the plan is a bad policy, so a link only to words does little
  • The kritik wins, because the ROB is about the ballot
  • The kritik counts more than the plan, because words come first
  • Nothing changes, because a ROB never affects the decision
Show the answer

Answer: The kritik counts only if it shows the plan is a bad policy, so a link only to words does little

Why: A ROB tells the judge what matters most. Under "best policy option," a kritik counts only as a reason the plan is a worse policy. A link only to the aff's words, with no claim about results, does little work under this ROB. Under a different ROB, the same link could count for more.

Under a critical ROB

The neg wins this ROB: "Vote for the team that best challenges harmful ways of thinking."

What does this ROB change about the decision?

  • The plan's results become the only thing the judge weighs
  • The ideas behind the aff become central, and the plan's results may count for less
  • The neg wins, whatever else happens in the round
  • The judge must ignore the flow
Show the answer

Answer: The ideas behind the aff become central, and the plan's results may count for less

Why: Under this ROB, the judge rewards the better critique of ideas. The plan's results may count for less than the ideas behind them. The neg still has to win its arguments, and the aff can try to win under this ROB too, for example by showing that its plan does not rely on the criticized idea.

Test a self-serving claim

The aff says the neg's ROB is self-serving: only the neg could ever win under it.

Which cross-ex question best tests that claim?

  • "Who wrote your ROB card?"
  • "Is your ROB longer than ours?"
  • "What would we have to do to win under your ROB?"
  • "Did you read your ROB in the 1NC?"
Show the answer

Answer: "What would we have to do to win under your ROB?"

Why: A ROB is self-serving if the other team could never win under it. Asking what the other team would have to do to win tests that directly. A weak answer is useful to the aff. The neg often replies that the aff could win too, or that some goals matter more than balance between the teams. Judges differ on how much this objection weighs.

If the neg wins under both ROBs, how much does the ROB debate decide?

  • Little, because the neg is ahead either way
  • Everything, because the ROB always comes first
  • Everything, because the aff extended its ROB
  • Nothing, because a ROB only counts if it is read in the 1NC
Show the answer

Answer: Little, because the neg is ahead either way

Why: If one team wins under both ROBs, the ROB may not decide the round. The judge still says in the RFD which ROB it used and why. Here the 2AR did not answer the neg's "even if" argument. That helps the neg under the aff's ROB too, but the judge still decides how much the dropped point weighs.
