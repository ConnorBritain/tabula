Topicality · 7 min read · intermediate

Interpretation and violation (topicality)

Plain version. Topicality, or T, is the negative's argument that the plan does not fit the resolution, the topic sentence for the season. Every T argument starts with two parts. The interpretation says what one word or phrase in the resolution means. The violation says how the plan falls outside that meaning. If either part is weak, the whole T argument is weak, no matter how good the rest of it is.

Where these two parts fit

A T argument has four parts: interpretation, violation, standards and voters. This page covers the first two. Standards say why one meaning is better for debate. See Standards. Voters say why T should decide the round. See Voters. For the whole argument in one place, see Topicality.

The interpretation and the violation do different jobs. The interpretation sets a line. The violation shows the plan is on the wrong side of that line.

The interpretation

The interpretation ("interp") is the neg's claim about what a word or phrase in the resolution means. It usually comes with a definition card: a short piece of evidence from a source that defines the word.

The definition and the interpretation are not quite the same thing. The definition is the evidence. The interpretation is the rule the neg draws from it. Many teams say the rule out loud: "The aff must increase funding by a net amount, not just move money between programs."

What makes a strong definition

Not every definition is equally good. Teams argue about quality all the time. These are the usual tests.

QualityWhat it meansWhy it helps
Qualified sourceA law, a legal dictionary, an agency, or an expert in the fieldThe source has a reason to be careful about meaning
Intent to defineThe author is trying to say what the word meansA sentence that only uses the word does not show its limits
Topic contextThe source is about the same subject as the resolutionA word can mean different things in different fields
Clear lineIt says what is outside the word, not only what is insideThe judge can tell whether a plan meets it
Fits the grammarIt defines the word the way the resolution uses itA noun definition does not fit a word used as a verb

A clear line is often called a bright line. Without one, no one can say for sure which plans meet the interpretation.

Which of these tests matters most is itself a debate. It belongs to the standards, mainly precision (the meaning experts use) and predictability (the meaning both teams could expect). See Standards.

Words and phrases

Some interpretations define one word. Others define a phrase, such as an adjective and the noun it goes with. A phrase definition can be more precise, because it reads the words together. It is also harder to find.

The aff sometimes answers that the neg defined a word out of its place. For example, a word can mean one thing alone and another thing inside a legal phrase.

The words that get T arguments most often are the same kinds every year: the actor (who acts, such as the federal government), the action (the verb, such as "increase" or "reduce"), the topic area (the subject), and limiting words (such as "substantial").

The violation

The violation compares the plan to the interpretation. It says, in one or two sentences, why the plan does not meet the line. A good violation is concrete: "The plan moves $2 billion from one program to another. The total does not go up. So there is no net increase."

Where the proof comes from

The neg can prove a violation from three places:

  • The plan text: the words of the plan itself.
  • The aff's evidence: what the aff's own cards say the plan does.
  • Cross-ex answers: what the aff said when asked.

Cross-ex is often the best place to set up a violation. Ask short, closed questions: "Does the plan add new money, or move money that is already spent?" A clear answer is hard for the aff to walk back later. See Cross-examination.

Plan text, or the whole aff?

Judges differ on what the violation is measured against. Some judges look only at the plan text. Others also look at what the aff's evidence and speeches say the plan does. Teams can argue this, and the judge should decide from what is argued.

Special kinds of violation

Most violations say the plan does not do what the word requires. Two special kinds have their own page. Effects topicality says the plan meets the resolution only through its later effects. Extra topicality says part of the plan goes beyond the resolution. See Effects and extra topicality.

How the aff answers: we meet and counter-interpretation

The aff has two direct answers. Most 2ACs make both.

We meet. The aff says the plan meets the neg's own interpretation. It must point to proof: the plan text, a card, or the plain words. A won "we meet" ends the T argument, no matter who wins the standards. Most judges treat it as yes or no: the plan meets, or it does not.

Counter-interpretation (CI). The aff offers its own meaning of the word, ideally with its own definition card. Then the debate becomes a choice between two interpretations.

One rule matters a lot: the plan must meet the aff's counter-interpretation. A CI that the plan does not meet gives the aff nothing. The neg will check, and may argue the plan violates the CI too.

The two answers work together: "We meet. And even if we do not, our counter-interpretation is better."

How to judge the interpretation and violation

  1. Check the we meet first. If the plan meets the neg's interpretation, T is over.
  2. Check the violation. Did the neg prove, with the plan text, evidence or cross-ex, that the plan is outside the line?
  3. Check the counter-interpretation. Is there one, and does the plan meet it?
  4. Compare the interpretations through the standards. Which one is better for debate depends on the standards that were argued, not on which dictionary you like.

Use the definitions the teams read. Do not bring your own meaning of the word into the decision.

How to read it: the neg's violation is that the plan only moves money, so the total does not rise. The 2AC makes a we meet, a counter-interpretation, and a claim that the judge should look only at the plan text. The block answers all three: the money for program B comes from cutting program A, the CI card does not intend to define, and the second affirmative speaker (the 2A) said in cross-ex that the plan funds B "by cutting A." The 1AR answers the first two rows but not the third. So the 2NR can use the aff's own cross-ex answer as proof of the violation, if the judge agrees that cross-ex answers count.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a definition that only uses the word. Fix: find a source that is trying to define it, and say so in your tag.
  • Neg: an interpretation with no clear line. Fix: state the rule in one sentence, so the judge can test any plan against it.
  • Neg: a violation that is assumed, not proven. Fix: point to the plan text, an aff card, or a cross-ex answer.
  • Aff: a counter-interpretation the plan does not meet. Fix: check your CI against your own plan text before the round.
  • Aff: saying different things about the plan in cross-ex and in speeches. Fix: decide what the plan does before the round, and say the same thing each time.
  • Judges: using your own definition of the word. Fix: decide between the interpretations the teams read.
