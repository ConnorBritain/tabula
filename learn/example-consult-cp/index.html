The consult counterplan (consult CP)

7 min read · Advanced

The consult counterplan says: before doing the plan, ask someone else first.

  • How it works: The government asks a close ally, or an international body, whether it supports the plan. It does the plan only if that party says yes.
  • The neg's case: Asking first builds trust, and that trust matters.
  • The aff usually answers: the plan should simply be done, asking first causes delay, and the counterplan may not be a fair choice.

The shape

The consult counterplan is a kind of process counterplan: it changes how the plan comes about, not what the plan does. It has the usual four parts of a counterplan. See Counterplans.

PartWhat it usually looks like (generic)
Text"The federal government should consult a close ally about the plan, and adopt the plan only if the ally agrees."
SolvencyEvidence that the ally would say yes, so the plan still happens.
CompetitionThe plan acts at once and for sure; the counterplan waits and acts only on a yes.
Net benefitAsking first improves relations with the ally, or not asking hurts them.

The text

Some texts say the consultation is binding, and some do not. Check the words. Binding means the government will follow the answer, yes or no. That word matters. If the answer does not bind the government, the aff can argue the consultation is just talk, and that the perm can do the same thing. Some texts name a specific process, or say what happens if the party asks for changes. Each word affects the perm and the solvency debate. See Counterplan text.

The "say yes" evidence

The neg usually reads evidence that the party would agree to the plan. If the party says yes, the counterplan ends up doing the plan, so it solves the case. If the party might say no, the counterplan might not do the plan at all. So the chance of a yes is the core of the solvency debate.

Teams sometimes argue whether the neg may simply imagine that the party says yes. Fiat lets a team imagine its own actor acts. It does not usually let a team decide how another party responds. So most teams treat the party's answer as a prediction, proven with evidence, not as something the neg can fiat. See Fiat.

The net benefit: relations

The most common net benefit is about relations: how well the two sides trust and work with each other.

  • Consult good. Being asked shows respect. Trust grows, and the ally helps more on other issues. This is an internal net benefit: a good thing that comes from the counterplan itself.
  • Not consulting bad. Acting without asking makes the ally feel ignored. Trust falls, and cooperation suffers. This works like a disadvantage: a harm the plan causes by acting alone. So it is an external net benefit.

Both versions lead to an impact through lost or gained cooperation on another issue. The perm treats them differently. Against "consult good," the aff says the perm consults too, so it gets the same benefit. Against "not consulting bad," the neg says the perm still links, because the plan happens whatever the ally says. See Net benefits.

The competition question

This is the hardest part of the consult debate. Competition asks whether the judge must choose between the plan and the counterplan, or could do both. See Competition.

The counterplan and the plan usually end in the same action. So the neg needs another difference. It usually claims two:

  • Certainty. The plan happens for sure. The counterplan happens only if the party says yes.
  • Timing (sometimes called immediacy). The plan happens at once. The counterplan happens only after the consultation ends.

The neg argues these differences make the counterplan a real choice. The aff often argues they are not enough. The plan and the counterplan do almost the same thing, so the counterplan is said to be "plan plus a step." Some judges accept competition through certainty and timing. Others think it lets the neg steal the plan. Teams should argue which standard applies.

The usual aff answers

The 2AC (second affirmative constructive) usually mixes several of these. A perm (permutation) is the aff's test of whether both could be done together.

  • Perm do both. Consult the party and do the plan. The aff argues the party still feels respected. The neg answers that consultation is only real if the government is willing to accept a no. If the plan happens no matter what, the party knows it was not truly asked.
  • Perm do the counterplan. Do the counterplan: if the party agrees, the plan happens. The neg answers that this drops the certainty of the plan, so it is a severance perm (a perm that leaves out part of the plan). See Permutations.
  • Solvency deficit: delay. Consultation takes time. If the aff's harm is urgent, the delay costs part of the advantage.
  • Solvency deficit: the party says no. If there is a real chance of a no, the counterplan may not do the plan at all.
  • Solvency deficit: changes. The party may ask for changes that weaken the plan.
  • Answer the net benefit. Relations are already good, or already bad. The party does not care about this plan. Relations do not drive cooperation.
  • The counterplan links too. Asking one party may anger another party that was not asked.
  • Theory. Consult counterplans, or process counterplans in general, are argued to be unfair. A common claim is that they compete only through certainty or timing. See Common theory arguments.

How to judge a consult counterplan

  1. Theory first, if argued. If the aff extended theory into the 2AR, decide it as argued. The usual remedy asked for is to reject the counterplan, not the team, but teams can argue either.
  2. Competition. Does the perm get the relations benefit? Is the perm legitimate, or does it sever certainty?
  3. Solvency. How likely is a yes? How long is the delay? What does that cost the case?
  4. Weigh. Compare the deficit with the net benefit.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic consult counterplan. The middle speeches are left out to save space. The 1NC comes first, then each 2AC answer with the 2NR and 2AR replies. Key: CP = counterplan, Solv = solvency, NB = net benefit, N/U = non-unique (the benefit is already here), adv = advantage, arg = argument, -> = leads to.

1NC
CP: consult a close ally; adopt
  plan only if ally agrees
Solv: ally says yes (Analyst 22)
NB: consult builds trust ->
  ally helps on joint program

2AC 1: Perm do both
2NR: Not real consult if plan
  happens anyway
2AR: Ally still gets a voice in
  how it is put in place

2AC 2: Deficit: delay
2NR: Talks are short; harm is
  not urgent
2AR: Even short delay costs
  lives in adv

2AC 3: Consult CPs bad: compete
  only on certainty
2NR: Tests certainty; core neg
  ground; reject the arg at most
2AR: Steals the plan; reject
  the CP

2AC 4: N/U: ties already strong
2NR: Being asked is new and
  builds trust
2AR: (no answer)

How to read it: on theory, both teams ask only to reject the counterplan, not the team, so the judge decides theory first. The 2AR never answers the 2NR's point that being asked is new and builds trust. If the counterplan survives theory, the judge asks whether the perm gets the trust benefit, and then weighs the delay against the net benefit.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: no "say yes" evidence. Fix: read evidence that this party would agree to this kind of plan.
  • Neg: no reason the perm loses the net benefit. Fix: explain why consultation is only real if a no would stop the plan.
  • Aff: "the ally will say no" with no evidence. Fix: read evidence, or argue that even a small chance of a no is a deficit.
  • Aff: theory in one line. Fix: if you plan to win on theory, explain the harm to the aff and develop it in the 1AR.
  • Both: forgetting the word "binding." Fix: read the text closely. It shapes both the perm and the solvency debate.
  • Judges: deciding consult theory before the round. Fix: judge it as argued, unless your paradigm says otherwise and the teams knew it.
