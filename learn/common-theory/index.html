Theory · 10 min read · advanced

Common theory arguments

Plain version. A theory argument says the other team's strategy is unfair or bad for debate. A few theory arguments come back every season, whatever the topic. Most are about counterplans, the negative's own plan, or about the permutation, the affirmative's test of a counterplan. For each one, both sides have real arguments, and judges differ. For most of these, many judges ignore the argument if the theory is won. Conditionality is the main exception: many judges will reject the team.

How these arguments work

Every theory argument has four parts. The interpretation is the rule a team wants. The violation says how the other team broke it. The standards say why the rule is good for debate. The voter says what the judge should do about it. See Theory.

The answering team usually gives a counter-interpretation: a different rule that it meets. Then the debate is a choice between two rules, judged on the standards.

The voter is often the biggest question. For most of these, many judges ignore the argument. This is called reject the argument: the judge sets aside the unfair counterplan or perm and decides the rest of the round. Conditionality is the main exception: many judges will reject the team, which means the team that broke the rule loses. See Reasons to reject.

Fiat comes up again and again below. Fiat means that, for the debate, we imagine a policy is passed. Many of these arguments ask how far fiat may go. See Fiat.

Conditionality and dispositionality

Conditionality ("condo") means the neg may drop a counterplan or kritik later in the round. Dispositionality ("dispo") is a middle status: the neg may drop the option only under set conditions, often only if the aff makes a permutation. Both are full debates of their own, so see Conditionality and the worked example Condo with three or more advocacies.

PICs bad

A plan-inclusive counterplan (PIC) does most of the plan but leaves out one part. A word PIC leaves out a word in the plan text, such as a label the neg says is harmful. The aff usually claims that PICs are bad.

  • The aff's side: PICs steal the aff's ground, and almost any small change becomes a counterplan, so the neg's options have no limit.
  • The neg's side: PICs test every part of the plan, the aff chose each word, and a common middle rule allows PICs when the neg has evidence about the part it cuts.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the counterplan, not the team.

For a full worked round, see The word PIC.

Consult and process counterplans bad

A process counterplan does the plan, but by a different process. The best-known kind is the consult counterplan: ask another party first, and do the plan only if that party agrees. The aff usually claims that these counterplans do not compete.

  • The aff's side: the counterplan does the whole plan and wins only on a small net benefit tied to the process, so it teaches little about the topic.
  • The neg's side: real decisions do involve process, such as asking allies, and if topic evidence discusses that process, it is fair game.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the counterplan, not the team. Some judges look mostly at competition. See Competition.

For a full worked round, see The consult counterplan.

Multiple actor fiat

Multiple actor fiat imagines several separate actors all acting at once. For example, a counterplan has the federal government and many separate agencies, or many governments, each do part of a policy.

  • The usual interpretation: "The neg may fiat only one actor," or "only the actor in the resolution."
  • Arguments against it (usually the aff's): in real life, no one person can make many separate actors act together. It lets the neg avoid the hard parts of coordination. It allows counterplans no real decision-maker could choose.
  • Arguments for it (usually the neg's): the aff fiats a whole government with many parts. Testing the plan against real alternatives needs some flexibility. If evidence supports the combined action, the counterplan is a fair test.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the counterplan, not the team.

Uniform fifty-state fiat

The states counterplan has all fifty states do the plan at the same time, in the same way: uniform fifty-state fiat. This is a special case of multiple actor fiat. The aff usually claims that this fiat goes too far.

  • The aff's side: no real decision-maker can make all fifty states act, and the aff cannot use evidence about states that would refuse.
  • The neg's side: it tests a core question, why the federal government must act, and the topic literature often debates federal versus state action.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the counterplan, not the team. Judges differ. Some accept it by default. Others are more skeptical, so the aff should develop the theory.

For a full worked round, see The states counterplan.

International fiat

International fiat imagines another country, or a world body, acting. For example, a counterplan has another nation's government do the plan.

  • The usual interpretation: "The neg may fiat only the domestic government named in the resolution."
  • Arguments against it (usually the aff's): the resolution asks what our government should do. A domestic decision-maker cannot choose for another nation. It lets the neg pick any actor in the world, so the aff cannot prepare.
  • Arguments for it (usually the neg's): comparing the plan with what other nations could do is real policy analysis. The topic literature may debate who should act. The aff can still argue solvency deficits and perms.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the counterplan, not the team. Judges differ. Some accept it by default. Others are more skeptical, so the aff should develop the theory.

Severance and intrinsic perms bad

A permutation (perm) is the aff's test of whether a counterplan really competes. A severance perm drops part of the plan. An intrinsic perm adds something that is in neither the plan nor the counterplan. See Permutations.

  • The usual interpretation: "The aff's perm must include all of the plan and nothing outside the two texts."
  • Arguments against these perms (usually the neg's): the aff should defend the plan it read. If it can drop or add parts, the neg's links vanish and the target keeps moving.
  • Arguments for them (usually the aff's): a perm is only a test, not a new plan. Some counterplans compete in narrow ways, and these perms show it.
  • Remedy: many judges reject the perm, not the team. The aff keeps its other perms and answers.

Two more that come up often

Object fiat. The aff fiats the thing the plan acts on, not only the actor. For example, the plan fiats that a foreign government or a group of people will respond a certain way. The neg argues this is unfair, because the aff can fiat away solvency problems. The aff answers that it only fiats its own actor's action. Many judges treat this as a fiat question and decide it as argued.

Floating PIKs bad. A kritik's alternative is the approach it offers instead of the plan. A floating PIK is an alternative that, the aff says, could include the plan itself. Then the kritik works like a PIC. The aff argues the neg should say early whether the alternative includes the plan. The neg answers that the alternative is not a policy, and the aff can perm. Many judges want this made clear in CX. See Kritiks.

How to judge these arguments

  1. Was it extended? The argument must be in the last rebuttal of the team that wants it. A two-line theory argument rarely decides a round unless it is dropped.
  2. Who won the interpretation? Compare the two rules, using the standards argued.
  3. What is the remedy? Decide as argued. If no one argued it, use your stated default. Many judges reject the argument.
  4. What is left? If a counterplan is set aside, compare the plan with the status quo. The net benefit can still count as a disadvantage against the plan.

Example in flow shorthand

The neg ran a PIC that does the plan in every area but one. Only the theory rows are shown.

2AC                 Block               1AR                 2NR                 2AR
PICs bad: neg       CI: PICs OK if      Their CI lets neg   Ext CI: ev says     CP steals the aff;
   takes 99% of        neg has ev on       cut any word;       this part is        reject the CP
   the plan            the part cut        no limit            debated
Voter: reject       Reject the arg:     (no answer)         Ext: arg, not       Reject the CP, not
   the team            ignore the CP                           team; 1AR drop      the team

How to read it: the 2AC said PICs are bad and asked the judge to reject the team. The block gave a counter-interpretation: PICs are fine if the neg has evidence about the part it cuts. It also said the remedy is to ignore the counterplan. The 1AR said the counter-interpretation has no limit, but it did not answer the remedy. The 2NR extended its evidence point and pointed out the 1AR's drop of the remedy. The 2AR now asks only to reject the counterplan. So the remedy is settled: the most the aff can win is that the counterplan goes away. The judge decides the interpretation. If the aff wins it, the judge compares the plan with the status quo.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: a list of theory arguments, each two lines long. Fix: pick the one or two that fit this counterplan, and develop them in the 1AR.
  • Aff: asking to reject the team with no reason. Fix: explain why ignoring the counterplan would not fix the harm, or ask only to reject the argument.
  • Neg: no counter-interpretation. Fix: give a rule you meet, such as "PICs are fine with evidence on the part cut."
  • Neg: forgetting the net benefit after the counterplan is rejected. Fix: the disadvantage can still count against the plan. Extend it.
  • Both: treating fiat limits as settled rules. Fix: there is no rulebook. Argue the standards in the round.
  • Judges: deciding by your own view of PICs or fiat. Fix: decide what the teams argued, and use your default only to break a tie.
