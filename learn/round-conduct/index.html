Round conduct, safety and evidence ethics

7 min read · Beginner

Debaters argue hard, but they must treat each other with respect.

  • If someone feels unsafe: The round can stop, and the judge should get tournament staff.
  • Honest evidence: Evidence must be honest. No made-up quotes, no changed words, and no claiming to read words that were skipped.
  • If evidence is challenged: If someone says the evidence is dishonest, follow your tournament's procedure.
  • You are not alone: You do not have to handle a serious problem alone.

Respect in the round

Debate is a contest of ideas. Strong disagreement is the point. Personal attacks are not. Most tournaments expect everyone in the room, debaters and judges, to:

  • Talk about arguments, not people. "Their evidence is old" is fine. Insults about a person are not.
  • Avoid slurs, threats and mocking of anyone's identity, background, body, accent or ability.
  • Be polite in cross-ex (the question time after a speech). Questions can be sharp. Shouting over someone, or not letting them answer, is not.
  • Respect time and space. Do not use the other team's materials. Do not interrupt speeches.
  • Treat the judge and the other team fairly, before, during and after the round.

Judges can respond to rude behavior. Common steps are a calm reminder in the round, lower speaker points, and a note on the ballot. See Speaker points. For serious problems, get tournament staff.

An argument about conduct is not the same as conduct. Some teams argue about language or ideas, or about how the other team acted in the round. One example is a kritik (an argument that criticizes the ideas behind the other side). Another is a theory argument (an argument about the rules of debate). These are part of the debate, and the other team can answer them. But many leagues treat unsafe behavior as something to stop and report, not something to win in the judge's notes.

Safety

Every tournament has its own safety rules. Many share a few common ones:

  • Keep the room door open, or follow the rules about adults and students alone in a room.
  • Online rounds: keep cameras and chats to what the tournament allows. Do not record without the tournament's permission.
  • Know where the tournament staff are. The central office is often called the tab room. It runs the schedule and handles problems.

If a debater feels unsafe

Any debater can say they feel unsafe, at any point in the round. When that happens:

  1. Stop the round. Stop the timers. Do not ask the debater to argue or prove the problem first.
  2. Check that everyone is safe. In an emergency, call for help the way the tournament directs, or call emergency services.
  3. Get tournament staff. Go to the tab room, or contact staff the way the tournament says. Let staff decide what happens next.
  4. Write down what happened, in plain facts, while it is fresh: the time, what was said or done, and who was there.
  5. Do not decide the round on your own until staff tell you how to go on.

Judges, you are not expected to investigate or fix the problem. Your job is to stop the round, keep people safe, and bring in the people whose job it is.

Evidence ethics

Debaters back up claims with cards: short quotes from experts. Each card has a tag (the debater's one-line summary) and a cite (who wrote it, and where). Debaters read only the highlighted words out loud. See Evidence and cards.

Evidence must be honest. The common problems are:

  • Fabrication: a quote or a source that does not exist.
  • Changed text: words added to or changed inside a quote.
  • Miscut evidence: words left out that change the author's meaning. Examples: skipping "not," or ending a quote right before "but this view is wrong."
  • Clipping: claiming to read highlighted words out loud that were skipped.
  • A missing or false cite: no way to find or check the source.

Not every weak card is an ethics problem. A card that is old, short, or overstated by its tag is usually a matter for debate. The other team can point it out in a speech. An ethics problem is about honesty: did the team present the evidence truthfully?

Ethics challenges: follow your tournament's procedure

Leagues handle evidence challenges in different ways. Do not invent your own rule. Read the tournament's rules, or ask the tab room.

Some things are common across many leagues:

  • Raise it at the right time. A debater who sees a problem usually tells the judge between speeches, not in the middle of a speech.
  • Some leagues use a formal challenge. In many of them, a formal challenge stops the round. The judge then decides whether the violation happened. The result is often a loss for the team found at fault. Sometimes it is a loss for the team that made the challenge, if the challenge is not upheld. The details differ, so check the rules.
  • Some leagues let teams debate it instead. A team makes the problem an argument in its speech, and the judge weighs it.
  • Proof matters. A judge needs evidence of the problem. Examples: the original source, the speech doc (the file of cards the team shared), or a recording where one is allowed. A clipping claim is hard to prove without a recording.
  • Staff can help. If you are not sure what to do, stop the round and ask the tab room before you decide.

If you are a judge and you see a problem no one raised, such as clear clipping, follow the tournament's procedure. Many tournaments want judges to contact the tab room, not act alone.

Judge conduct

Judges set the tone. A fair judge:

  • pays attention to every speech, and keeps phones and other work away;
  • stays neutral in words during the round (some judges keep a neutral face too);
  • gives feedback about arguments and speaking, never about looks or identity. See Oral critiques and feedback;
  • follows the tournament's rules on disclosure (telling the teams who won), time and safety;
  • reports problems instead of ignoring them.

After the 2AC (2nd aff speech):

  "not" -> miscut
JUDGE: stops timers. Asks:
  debate it, or formal
  challenge?
NEG: formal challenge
JUDGE: checks the rules:
  a challenge ends the round
AFF: gives the source
NEG: shows the cut line
JUDGE: reads the full source
  paragraph. "Not" was cut

  per the rules. Tells tab

Common mistakes

  • Judges: asking an upset debater to prove they are unsafe before stopping. Fix: stop the round first, then get tournament staff.
  • Judges: making up a rule for an ethics challenge. Fix: read the tournament's rules or ask the tab room.
  • Debaters: accusing someone in the middle of a speech. Fix: raise it with the judge between speeches, calmly.
  • Debaters: calling a weak card an ethics violation. Fix: if the card is old or overstated, answer it in your speech. Save challenges for honesty problems.
  • Debaters: not marking cards you cut short. Fix: say "mark the card at..." out loud, to show where you stopped. See Speech docs.
  • Everyone: treating unsafe behavior as an argument to win. Fix: arguments get debated. Unsafe behavior gets stopped and reported.

When to raise it

What counts as proof

A team makes a formal evidence challenge. You must decide whether the violation happened.

  • The speech doc (the file of cards the team shared)
  • A recording of the speech, where the tournament allows one

Answer: The original source the card came from; The speech doc (the file of cards the team shared); A recording of the speech, where the tournament allows one

You see it yourself

No one raises anything. But you see a debater clearly skip highlighted words while saying they read the whole card.

What is the right step?

  • Vote against that team, and say nothing to anyone
  • Ignore it, because no one raised it
  • Follow the tournament's procedure, which often means contacting the tab room
  • Ask the other team to make it an argument in their next speech
