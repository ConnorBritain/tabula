Competition

7 min read · Intermediate

A counterplan is the negative's own plan. It is only a reason to vote negative if it competes with the affirmative's plan. To compete means the judge has to choose: the plan or the counterplan, not both. If the judge could simply do both and lose nothing, the counterplan does not compete. Then it is not a reason to reject the plan. Teams test competition in two main ways: mutual exclusivity and net benefits.

Why competition matters

A vote is a choice. The judge votes for the plan or against it. A counterplan is a reason to vote against the plan only if picking the plan would mean giving up something better.

Here is a simple picture. Someone offers you soup for lunch. A friend says, "Have a salad instead." That is only a real choice if you cannot have both, or if having both is worse than having the salad alone. If you can happily eat both, the salad is not a reason to say no to the soup.

Debaters sometimes call this opportunity cost: the plan is bad if choosing it costs you a better option.

The two tests

TestWhat it asksHow common
Mutual exclusivityIs it impossible to do the plan and the counterplan together?Rare
Net benefitsIs doing the counterplan alone better than doing both?Most common

A counterplan needs to pass at least one test. Most counterplans compete through net benefits.

Mutual exclusivity

Two policies are mutually exclusive when both cannot happen. For example, a plan that builds a facility and a counterplan that bans that kind of facility cannot both be true.

This is rare, because most actions can be done side by side. Two governments can fund the same program. One agency can study a problem while another acts on it. When the neg claims mutual exclusivity, the aff usually answers by showing how both could happen at once.

Net benefits

A net benefit is a reason the counterplan alone beats the plan plus the counterplan. Most often it is a disadvantage (DA): a harm the plan causes and the counterplan avoids. If doing both would still cause the harm, then doing the counterplan alone is better, and the judge has a real choice.

Net benefits have their own page, which explains the kinds and how they are weighed. See Net benefits.

The permutation: how competition is tested

The aff tests competition with a permutation ("perm"). The perm says: imagine doing the plan and all or part of the counterplan together. If that combination is possible and avoids the net benefit, the counterplan does not compete.

So the competition debate is usually a perm debate. The neg tries to show that the perm still causes the net benefit, or cannot happen. The aff tries to show the perm works and avoids it. See Permutations.

What happens when a counterplan does not compete

If the aff wins that the counterplan does not compete, the counterplan drops out of the round. Most judges then compare the plan with the status quo (the world as it is now, without the plan).

The net benefit does not disappear. If it is a DA that the plan causes, it still counts against the plan. But now it must outweigh the whole case on its own, without a counterplan to take away the aff's advantages.

Advanced: kinds of competition

This part is for experienced debaters and judges. Theory pages go deeper. See Theory.

  • Functional competition looks at what the policies do. Do the two actions differ in effect?
  • Textual competition looks at the words of the texts. Does the counterplan text differ from the plan text?

Some judges want a counterplan to compete in function. Some accept text alone. Some want both. Teams argue which standard should apply, because it changes which perms are allowed and which counterplans compete. For example, a counterplan that does the whole plan but changes one word may compete in text and not clearly in function.

  • Artificial competition. Some counterplans compete only through a part that seems added just to compete. A plank (one step in the counterplan text) that says "and the plan should not happen" is one example. Many judges look at this kind of competition with doubt, and teams often debate it as theory.
  • Competition through certainty or timing. Some process counterplans (which do the plan by a different process) compete because the plan acts at once and for sure, while the counterplan acts later or only if someone agrees. Teams argue whether those differences are a fair basis for competition. Some judges accept it; others do not.

How to answer a competition claim

For the aff:

  • Perm. Say what the perm does, and why it avoids the net benefit.
  • No mutual exclusivity. Show how both policies could happen at once.
  • No net benefit. Answer the DA. If it is not true, the counterplan has no reason to be chosen.
  • Theory. If the counterplan competes only in an artificial way, say why that is unfair, and what the judge should do.

For the neg:

  • The perm links. Explain why doing both still causes the net benefit.
  • The perm is not legitimate. If the perm drops part of the plan or adds new things, say so. See Permutations.
  • Keep the net benefit alive. Extend it fully. Without it, most counterplans do not compete.

How to judge competition

  1. Decide competition first. Before you weigh solvency, ask whether the counterplan is a real alternative.
  2. Look at the perm. Is it legitimate, as argued? Does it avoid the net benefit?
  3. If the counterplan does not compete, set it aside. Compare the plan with the status quo, and still count any DA the plan causes.
  4. If it competes, weigh what the counterplan misses (the solvency deficit) against the net benefit. See Solvency deficits.

Example in flow shorthand

Each group follows one argument through the round. Key: "Comp" means competition, "ME" means mutual exclusivity, "NB" means net benefit, "fed" means federal, "$" means money, and "->" means "leads to".

COMPETITION 1: MUTUAL EXCLUSIVITY
1NC: Comp 1: ME. Only one gov
     can run X
2AC: 1. Not ME: fed and states
     fund many programs together
2NR: Concede not ME; go for net
     benefit
2AR: ME is gone; the perm is
     the only test

COMPETITION 2: NET BENEFIT
1NC: Comp 2: NB. Fed $ ->
     Spending DA
2AC: 2. Perm do both: no extra
     cuts
2NR: Perm still spends fed $
     -> cuts happen
2AR: Plan's cost is small; DA
     risk is tiny

How to read it: the neg gave up mutual exclusivity, so the perm is the only test. The judge asks: does the perm still cause the spending DA? If yes, the counterplan competes, and the judge weighs the DA against any solvency deficit. If no, the counterplan drops out.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: claiming mutual exclusivity that is not real. Fix: rely on a net benefit, and save mutual exclusivity for policies that truly cannot coexist.
  • Neg: no explanation of why the perm links. Fix: say exactly why doing both still causes the harm.
  • Aff: a perm with no reason it avoids the net benefit. Fix: explain how doing both escapes the DA.
  • Aff: forgetting the DA still counts if the CP goes away. Fix: answer the DA on its own merits too.
  • Judges: weighing solvency before checking competition. Fix: ask first whether the counterplan is a real choice.
