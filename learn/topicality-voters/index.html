Topicality · 9 min read · intermediate

Topicality voters, competing interpretations and reasonability

A voter is a reason an argument should decide the round. For topicality (T), the usual voters are fairness and education. The judge also needs a test for T. Under competing interpretations, the judge picks the better of the two meanings of the word. Under reasonability, the affirmative wins T if its meaning is good enough. Teams should argue which test to use, and the judge should use the one that wins.

What a voter is

A topicality argument has four parts: interpretation, violation, standards and voters. See Topicality. The first three show that the plan is outside the best meaning of the resolution. The voter answers the last question: so what? Why should that decide who wins?

Standards lead into voters. "Limits" is a standard. "Limits, so the neg can prepare, so the round is fair" ties it to the voter, fairness. See Standards.

The common voters

VoterThe claimExample (generic)
FairnessBoth teams should have an equal chance"The neg could not prepare for an aff outside the topic."
EducationDebate should teach the most"Debates about the topic teach more than debates about anything at all."
JurisdictionThe judge can only vote for a plan within the resolution"The resolution is the question the judge was asked to answer."

Fairness and education are the two most common voters. Teams often weigh them against each other. Some judges see fairness as the main reason debate has rules. Others see education as the reason the activity exists, with fairness serving it. Both views are common, and teams should argue which comes first.

Jurisdiction is an older voter. Some judges find it persuasive. Others think it only restates the claim that the aff should be topical, without a new reason. Teams that use it should explain it.

Why T is a reason to vote neg

Most theory arguments (arguments about the rules of debate) can end in one of two ways: the judge rejects the unfair argument, or the judge rejects the whole team. See Reasons to reject. T is different. The plan is the aff's whole advocacy: everything it defends. There is no smaller part to set aside. So when the neg wins T, the usual result is a neg ballot. That is what teams mean when they say "T is a voting issue."

What about an RVI?

An RVI (reverse voting issue) is a claim that a team should win because the other side's theory or topicality argument was wrong. It is usually made by the aff. On topicality, the aff says that T took time away from its case. Many judges do not vote on RVIs, especially on topicality. The common reasoning: being topical is a basic job of the aff, and the aff does not earn a win for doing its basic job. A few judges will listen to an RVI that is well explained and not answered.

Competing interpretations

Under competing interpretations, the judge treats the two meanings like two policies. The judge asks: which interpretation is better for debate? If the neg's interpretation is better, even by a little, and the plan does not meet it, the neg wins T.

This test works on offense and defense. Offense is a reason to prefer your interpretation. Defense only makes the other side's reasons smaller. Under competing interpretations, the aff usually needs its own offense: a counter-interpretation (its own meaning of the word) with standards behind it. Only saying "your limits claim is too big" may not be enough.

Teams that favor competing interpretations often say:

  • It gives the judge a clear test, so the judge does not have to guess.
  • It treats T like any other argument: compare, then weigh.
  • It sets a good precedent: it rewards the meaning that would make the best topic.

Reasonability

Under reasonability, the judge asks a different question: is the aff's interpretation good enough? If it is, the aff wins T, even if the neg's interpretation is a little better.

Teams use the word in slightly different ways. Some mean "is the aff reasonably topical?" Others mean "is the counter-interpretation reasonable?" Others mean "is the neg's offense big enough to justify voting on T?" A good aff explains which one it means.

Teams that favor reasonability often say:

  • Small differences between two meanings should not decide a whole round.
  • Competing interpretations invites T in every round, which crowds out debate about the topic itself.
  • A predictable aff that the neg prepared for is fair, even if a stricter meaning exists.

Teams that oppose reasonability often say it is arbitrary: "good enough" has no clear line, so the judge has to guess. Some judges ask the aff to give a line for what counts as reasonable. Teams should argue this, and judges should decide from what is argued.

In-round abuse or precedent

A related question is what kind of harm the neg must show.

  • In-round abuse: the neg shows real harm in this round. "We lost our best disadvantage."
  • Precedent (sometimes called potential abuse): the neg shows what debate would look like if this meaning were allowed. "If this counts, hundreds of affs count."

Aff teams often say the neg must show in-round abuse. Neg teams often say T is about the topic as a whole, not one round. Some judges want in-round abuse. Others accept precedent alone. Both views are common, and the choice often follows the competing interpretations vs reasonability debate.

T and judge styles

Different judges come to T with different habits. A stock issues judge (one who checks the aff's basic jobs) treats topicality as a basic requirement the aff must meet. Some policymaker judges (who weigh costs and benefits, like a lawmaker) default to competing interpretations. A lay judge, with little debate background, wants a plain story: what the word means, why the plan does not fit, and why that matters. Many judges state a default in their paradigm, for example "I default to competing interpretations." See Judge paradigms.

How to answer it

The voter.

  • Neg: say which voter comes first and why. For example, "fairness first, because a fair round is needed to learn anything."
  • Aff: say the voter does not apply here. For example, "the neg still had ground, so the round was fair." Or argue that the other voter comes first.

The test.

  • Neg, for competing interpretations: say reasonability is arbitrary, and show why your interpretation is better on the standards.
  • Aff, for reasonability: give a clear line for "good enough," and show that your counter-interpretation meets it.

Abuse.

  • Neg: show lost ground in this round, or explain why precedent matters even without it.
  • Aff: point to the arguments the neg still ran, and say precedent is only a guess.

The RVI.

  • Aff: if you go for one, explain why beating T should win the round, and spend real time on it.
  • Neg: answer it, even briefly. A short "being topical is the aff's job" answer is often enough, but a dropped RVI gives the aff an easy argument.

How to judge voters and the test

  1. Check the we meet first. If the plan meets the neg's interpretation, T is over. See Interpretation and violation.
  2. Decide the test. Which did the teams argue for: competing interpretations or reasonability? Who won that? If no one argued it, use your stated default.
  3. Apply the test. Under competing interpretations, ask which interpretation is better on the standards. Under reasonability, ask whether the aff's meaning is good enough, using the line the teams gave.
  4. Check the voter. Did the neg extend a voter to the 2NR? If not, T may not be a reason to vote neg.
  5. Explain it. In the reason for decision, name the test you used and why.

How to read it: on the test, the 2NR says the aff never defined "good enough," and the 2AR answers that it is a core aff and the neg named no lost DA or CP. On abuse, the neg's precedent claim matters most under competing interpretations. Under reasonability, the aff's point that the neg still ran three DAs is strong. The RVI is gone: the 1AR did not answer the block or extend it, and the 2AR does not bring it back.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: no voter in the 2NR. Fix: extend fairness or education, and say why it outweighs the aff's reasons to reject T.
  • Neg: claiming competing interpretations with no offense. Fix: show why your interpretation is better, not only that theirs has problems.
  • Aff: saying "reasonability" with no line. Fix: say what makes an interpretation good enough, and show that yours meets it.
  • Aff: relying on an RVI. Fix: spend the time on a we meet and a counter-interpretation. Many judges do not vote on RVIs.
  • Both: no argument about the test. Fix: say which test the judge should use, and why. It often decides T.
  • Judges: using reasonability to mean "it felt topical to me." Fix: apply the test and the line the teams argued.
