Kritikal affirmatives (K affs)

9 min read · Advanced

A kritikal affirmative, or K aff, puts a critique of ideas at the center of its case.

  • How it differs: most affirmative teams defend a plan: a government action that fits the topic.
  • Two kinds: some K affs still have a plan, but give critical reasons for it. Others do not defend a government plan at all, and argue for a way of thinking or acting about the topic.
  • How the negative answers: the negative often answers with framework, an argument that the aff should have defended a plan.
  • How judges decide: judges differ a lot on these debates, so both teams should explain their view in plain words.

What a K aff is

A kritik (K) is an argument that questions the ideas behind a position. See Kritiks. Usually the negative runs it. A kritikal affirmative turns that around: the aff's own case is built on a critique.

A usual aff says, "The government should do the plan, because it solves a harm." See The affirmative case. A K aff often says something closer to, "The way people usually think about this topic causes harm, and here is a better approach." The aff then asks the judge to vote for that approach.

The range of K affs

K affs are not all the same. They sit on a range, from close to a usual plan to far from it.

KindWhat the aff defendsExample (generic)
Plan with critical advantagesA topical government plan, with reasons drawn from a critique"The government should end a program, because the program rests on a harmful idea about a group."
Advocacy in the topic's directionA statement that agrees with the topic's goal, but not action by the government"We should challenge the way this topic area treats a group of people."
Method or performanceA way of doing debate, such as personal story, poetry or music, used as the argument"Debating this topic through a community's own words is the better method."
Critique of the resolutionAn argument that the topic sentence itself rests on a harmful frame"The resolution assumes the government is the right actor. We say that assumption is the problem."

An advocacy statement is a short sentence that says what the aff stands for, a bit like a plan text. A method is the aff's way of approaching a problem. Performance means the way the aff presents its argument is part of the argument.

The first kind is usually topical: it fits the topic. The neg can answer it with the same tools it uses against any aff, plus the K tools below. The other kinds raise a bigger question: must the aff defend the resolution in the usual way? That question is the framework debate. See Framework against a kritikal affirmative.

Why teams run K affs

Teams that run K affs give many reasons. Here are common ones, in the terms those teams often use.

  • Debate shapes the people in it. Students spend years in the activity. What they practice in rounds, they carry into life. A K aff asks what debate should train people to think and do.
  • The usual frame can leave things out. A topic about government action can treat some people as numbers, or leave out their own accounts. A K aff can bring those accounts in.
  • Ideas come before policy. How a problem is described shapes which solutions seem possible. Testing that description first can lead to better answers.
  • The round is real. A plan is only imagined to pass. The words, arguments and choices in the room really happen. Some teams argue that this makes the round itself a place to act.
  • Scholarship. Many K affs draw on serious work in philosophy, history, law and the social sciences. Teams say that debating it is good research and good education.
  • Access and voice. Some debaters say the K aff lets them speak about their own experience and community. They argue this makes debate more open.

Many K affs are deeply engaged with the topic's subject. They often argue that they debate the topic more fully than a plan would, not less.

Why some negs and judges are wary

Other teams and judges have concerns. Here are common ones, in the terms those teams often use.

  • Predictable prep. The resolution tells the neg what to prepare for. If the aff can defend any approach, the neg cannot research it before the round.
  • Shared ground for clash. A plan gives both teams a fixed point to argue about. Without one, the two teams can talk past each other.
  • Skills from defending an action. Defending a concrete government action teaches research, weighing and how policy works. Negs say those skills are worth keeping.
  • The aff should say why it is not a plan. Negs say the aff must explain why its ideas need to step away from a plan, not only why its ideas are good.

These points are the core of the framework argument. Both sides of that debate are on Framework against a kritikal affirmative.

How the neg engages a K aff

The neg has several options, and many teams combine them. The main one is framework (often called T-USFG: a topicality argument that the aff must defend action by the United States federal government): the aff should have defended government action that fits the resolution. The full debate is on Framework against a kritikal affirmative.

The neg can also argue presumption: the aff does nothing a ballot can support, so the judge should vote neg. Presumption is the rule that the judge stays with the present system when no team proves a change is better. See Presumption. Other options are case arguments, a kritik of the aff (often called a K v K or methods debate), and a PIK (a plan-inclusive kritik) that keeps most of the aff but rejects one part. In a methods debate, teams also argue whether the aff may use a permutation to combine its approach with the neg's. See Permutations.

How the aff defends a K aff

  • Say what the aff does. In one or two sentences, explain the advocacy and what happens if the judge votes aff.
  • Say what the ballot means. Many K affs offer a role of the ballot (ROB), a claim about what the judge's vote rewards. See Role of the ballot.
  • Answer presumption. Explain why the aff's method makes a difference, in the round or beyond it.
  • Prepare for framework. Have a counter-interpretation (the aff's own rule for what an aff may or must do, offered against the neg's rule), impact turns, or both. The full list is on the framework page.
  • Defend the case. Answer impact defense (claims the harm is smaller) and solvency arguments, as any aff must.

How judges differ

Judges hold a wide range of views on K affs. This is one of the most debated questions in the activity.

  • Some judges believe the aff should defend the resolution with a plan. They are often open to framework.
  • Some judges welcome K affs and are skeptical of framework.
  • Many judges say they decide these debates on the flow, as argued, and try to set their own view aside.

Most experienced judges state their view in their paradigm. Read it before the round. See Judge paradigms.

If you are a new judge, you do not need to know the theory behind the aff. Ask: what does the aff ask me to vote for? What did the neg say is wrong with that? Which side explained its reasons better? Write down what each team said, and decide from that.

How to read it: the 1AC has an advantage and an advocacy, but no government plan. On point 1, the 1NC says the aff's method does nothing a ballot can back. The 2AC says the method changes how we study the topic, and that this happens in the round. The 2NR says a ballot cannot change how anyone studies outside the room. The 2AR says the ballot endorses a better way to study, and that the neg read no card. On point 2, the 1NC reads framework and the 2AC gives a counter-interpretation; the rest of that debate is on the framework sheet. On point 3, the 2AC does not answer the impact defense, the 2NR extends it as dropped, and the 2AR does not answer it. So the neg wins that the harm is smaller than the 1AC claimed. The round may still turn on framework and on the presumption clash in point 1.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: no clear statement of what the aff does. Fix: give the advocacy in one or two plain sentences, and say what a vote for it means.
  • Aff: no answer to presumption. Fix: explain what the method changes, and why that is a reason to vote aff.
  • Neg: only framework. Fix: also answer the case, or run a K or presumption, in case the judge does not vote on framework.
  • Neg: treating every K aff the same. Fix: read the 1AC closely. A K aff with a plan needs different answers from one with no plan.
  • Both: assuming the judge knows the literature. Fix: explain each idea in plain words, as you would to a smart friend.
  • Judges: deciding on your view of K affs in general. Fix: decide the round on what the teams argued, and explain it in the RFD.
