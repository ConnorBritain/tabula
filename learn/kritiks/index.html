Kritiks (K)

7 min read · Intermediate

A kritik, or K, asks a different question from most negative arguments: "Are the ideas behind the plan a good way to think?"

  • How it differs: most negative arguments ask, "Is the plan a good idea?"
  • What it argues: the affirmative relies on a harmful way of seeing the world. The K shows what that way of thinking leads to, and it offers a different approach.
  • What it can target: the plan, the affirmative's reasons, or the words it used.
  • The name: the word comes from the German word for "critique."

What a kritik does

A disadvantage (DA) says the plan causes something bad. A kritik says the aff's starting point is the problem. For example, a kritik might argue:

  • the aff describes some groups of people as threats, and that way of describing people causes harm;
  • the aff assumes a certain economic system is natural, and that assumption hides who that system hurts;
  • the aff uses a word or a frame that does damage, no matter what the plan does.

Kritiks draw on ideas from philosophy, politics, history and the social sciences. Many debaters find them some of the most interesting arguments in debate. Some judges prefer them; some are skeptical. Both are common views.

The parts

The neg reads a kritik in the 1NC, its first speech. There it usually has three core parts. The debate around it adds two more.

PartThe question it answers
LinkWhat does the aff do, say or assume that the kritik criticizes?
ImpactWhy is that way of thinking harmful?
AlternativeWhat should we do or think instead?
FrameworkHow should the judge decide this round? Weigh the plan's results, or the aff's ideas, or both?
Role of the ballotWhat is the judge's vote for?

Link

The link shows that the aff is part of the problem. Links can come from:

  • the plan (what it does),
  • the advantages (the reasons and evidence the aff uses),
  • the words the aff used,
  • the assumptions under the aff's argument.

Specific links are stronger. "Your second advantage describes this region as a danger that must be controlled" is better than "all policies do this."

Impact

The impact explains what the harmful way of thinking leads to. Some kritik impacts are about real-world results (violence, exclusion, a cycle of conflict). Some are about the debate itself (what we learn, and what this round teaches). Some kritiks also argue that the aff's own advantages will fail, because they rest on a flawed idea. This is often called "the K turns the case." See Turns case.

Alternative

The alternative ("alt") is what the neg offers instead. It can be:

  • a different way of thinking about the problem;
  • a call to reject the aff's frame;
  • a different action, by a different actor.

The alt is often the hardest part of a kritik to explain. Good neg teams say clearly what the alt does and how it solves, or at least avoids, the impact.

Some neg teams go for a kritik without the alternative, as a reason the aff's way of thinking should not be rewarded. Teams and judges disagree on whether that is enough.

Framework

Framework is the debate about how the judge should decide. The aff often argues: "The judge should weigh the plan's results against the neg's best policy option." The neg often argues: "The judge should first ask whether the aff's ideas are sound, because ideas shape what policies do."

Framework is often where a K round is won or lost. See Framework against a kritik.

Role of the ballot

The role of the ballot (ROB) is a short claim about what the judge's vote means. For example: "The ballot should go to the team that best challenges harmful ways of thinking." Or: "The ballot should go to the team with the best policy." A related idea is the role of the judge: "The judge is a policymaker," or "The judge is an educator." See Role of the ballot.

How to answer a kritik (for the aff)

The aff first answers the K in the 2AC, its second speech. A full 2AC answer often has:

  1. Framework: the judge should weigh the plan's results, and the neg must offer a competing policy.
  2. Perm (permutation): the aff can do the plan and the alternative together. See Permutations.
  3. No link or link defense: the aff does not do what the K says, or the link is too general.
  4. Case outweighs: the aff's advantages are larger and more certain than the K's impact.
  5. Alt fails: the alternative does not solve, or is too vague to evaluate.
  6. Impact turn: in some debates, the aff argues that the idea the K criticizes is actually good. This is risky but possible, and the aff must defend it with care.

How to judge a kritik

A kritik can feel new to a parent judge. These steps help:

  1. Start with framework. Which way of deciding did each team argue for? Who won that debate? This tells you what counts.
  2. Then the link. Did the neg show that this aff does the thing the K criticizes? A K with no link should not win.
  3. Then the impact and the alternative. What harm does the K show? What does the alt offer, and does it solve that harm?
  4. Then the perm. Can the aff do both the plan and the alt? If yes, the K is not a reason to reject the plan, unless the neg shows why doing both fails.
  5. Weigh. Compare the K's impact (with the alt) to the aff's advantages, using the framework that won.

You do not need to know the authors or the philosophy behind the K. Judge it like any other argument: decide on what was explained, not on what you already know.

Some affirmatives are critical too. See Kritikal affirmatives. For a fair summary of the kritik families debaters read most, see Common kritik families.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic security kritik against an aff whose advantage says a rival country is a threat that must be contained.

The 1NC is the neg's first speech, and the 2AC is the aff's answer. The 2NR and 2AR are each team's last speech. Shorthand: L = link, I = impact, FW = framework, adv = advantage, "->" = "leads to", w/o = without. Names in parentheses, like (Scholar 19), are invented evidence authors and years.

1NC
  K: Security
  L: adv 2 calls rival a
    "threat" to be controlled
    (Theorist)
  I: threat talk -> cycle
    of conflict (Scholar 19)
  Alt: reject the threat frame

2AC
  1. FW: weigh plan vs a
     policy alt
  2. Perm: do the plan, and
     drop adv 2's threat
     framing
  3. Alt fails: no concrete
     action
  4. Case outweighs

2NR
  FW: ideas shape policy;
    weigh framing first
  Perm fails: adv 2 still
    needs the threat to matter
  Alt = new way to see rival
    -> less conflict
  K turns case: threat talk
    makes adv fail

2AR
  FW: "ideas shape policy"
    has no card; plan first
  Perm solves: the plan works
    w/o adv 2's framing
  Alt is vague; case is
    concrete
  Case still wins under any FW

How to read it: the round turns on framework and the perm. If the judge weighs only the plan's results, the aff is ahead. If the judge weighs the aff's framing first, the neg is ahead, unless the perm solves. The perm fight is about whether the plan still works once the threat framing is dropped. A good RFD would say which framework won, and why.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a link to "all policies." Fix: quote or describe what this aff does. Specific links beat generic ones.
  • Neg: an alternative no one can explain. Fix: say in one or two plain sentences what the alt does and why it solves.
  • Neg: ignoring the aff's case. Fix: explain why the K turns the case, or why the case does not matter under your framework.
  • Aff: answering only with framework. Fix: also make a perm and answer the link. If you lose framework with nothing else, you lose the round.
  • Aff: a perm with no explanation. Fix: say what the perm looks like, and why doing both is better than the alt alone.
  • Judges: deciding on how they feel about kritiks in general. Fix: decide the framework debate as argued, then apply it.

Label the shell

The 1NC reads a kritik in three sentences. (1) The plan lets national experts decide what each town needs. (2) Letting faraway experts decide silences the people who live there, so their real needs go unmet. (3) The judge should vote neg to reject the idea that experts far away know best.

Which labels fit sentences 1, 2 and 3, in that order?

  • Link, impact, alternative
  • Impact, link, alternative
  • Alternative, link, impact
  • Link, alternative, impact
Show the answer

Answer: Link, impact, alternative

Why: Sentence 1 says what the aff does that the kritik criticizes: that is the link. Sentence 2 says why that way of thinking is harmful: that is the impact. Sentence 3 says what the judge should do instead: that is the alternative.

Kritik or disadvantage?

The neg reads two arguments against a plan that gives cash aid to poor families. Argument A: "The plan costs a lot of money, so the law will force cuts to school programs." Argument B: "The 1AC describes poor families as a burden on everyone else, and that way of describing people causes harm."

Which argument is a kritik?

  • Argument A
  • Argument B
  • Both, because both are against the plan
  • Neither, because a kritik must be about the judge
Show the answer

Answer: Argument B

Why: Argument A says the plan causes something bad. That is the shape of a disadvantage. Argument B questions the aff's starting point: how the aff describes people. A kritik criticizes the ideas, words or assumptions behind the aff.

Which part is this?

In the 2NR, the neg says: "Before you weigh the plan's results, first ask whether the aff's ideas are sound. Ideas shape what policies do."

Which part of the kritik debate is the neg arguing?

  • The link
  • The impact
  • The alternative
  • Framework
Show the answer

Answer: Framework

Why: Framework is the debate about how the judge should decide: weigh the plan's results, the aff's ideas, or both. The neg here asks the judge to weigh ideas first. The aff often argues the other way: weigh the plan's results against the neg's best policy option. The judge decides which side argued this better in the round.

An author you do not know

You judge a round with a kritik. The neg names a philosopher you have never heard of. The neg explains in plain words what the aff assumes and why that assumption causes harm. The aff answers some of it.

How should you judge the kritik?

  • Look up the philosopher after the round, and decide on what you find
  • Decide on what the teams explained in the round, like any other argument
  • Vote against the kritik, because you cannot check the theory
  • Vote for the kritik, because the theory must be well known
Show the answer

Answer: Decide on what the teams explained in the round, like any other argument

Why: You do not need to know the authors or the philosophy behind a kritik. Judge it like any other argument. Decide on what each team explained and answered, not on what you know or find later.
