Framework against a kritik

Plain version. When the negative runs a kritik, the two teams often disagree about what the judge should weigh. A kritik, or K, questions the ideas behind the affirmative. The affirmative usually says: weigh the plan's results against the present system or another policy. The negative usually says: first ask whether the aff's ideas, words and assumptions are sound. Many judges land somewhere in between. This debate is called framework, and it often decides which arguments count at all.

Where this fits

The word "framework" has two main uses. See Framework. This page covers framework against a kritik: an aff argument about how to judge a neg K. The other use is the neg's argument against an aff with no plan. That has its own page. See Framework against a kritikal affirmative.

Framework against a K is usually a claim about how to weigh, not a reason to reject a team. Most judges treat it that way. A few teams argue it as a voting issue on its own.

The main interpretations

An interpretation here is a team's proposed rule for what the judge weighs. These four are common, from most plan-focused to most idea-focused.

InterpretationWhat the judge weighsOften run by
Plan onlyThe plan's results against the present system or a competing policy. A K counts only as a reason the plan is a bad policy.The aff
Plan, but links to its reasons countThe plan's results, but the neg may show that the aff's reasons or words make those results worseEither side, as a middle ground
Representations firstThe aff's words and framing, before the plan's resultsThe neg
Knowledge firstHow the aff knows what it claims, before what the plan doesThe neg

Representations (often "reps") are the words, images and framing a team uses. The last row is often called epistemology first. Epistemology means the study of how we know things.

The aff's case for weighing the plan

Teams that defend weighing the plan often argue:

  • Predictability. The aff prepared to defend its plan. If the judge weighs anything the aff said or assumed, the target can move in every round.
  • Limits. There are countless ways to criticize a speech's words. Weighing reps first gives the neg too many options to prepare against.
  • Aff ground. If the plan's results do not count, the aff's main arguments, its advantages, may count for nothing.
  • Policy education. Comparing the results of real choices is a useful skill. The resolution asks about a policy.
  • Clash. A shared focus on the plan makes both teams answer each other directly.

The neg's case for weighing ideas first

Teams that defend weighing ideas first often argue:

  • Ideas shape policy. How we describe a problem decides which answers seem possible. So ideas come before results.
  • The aff chose its words. The aff picked its reasons and its framing. It should defend them, like any other part of its case.
  • Education. Questioning assumptions is a key part of what debate teaches. A framework that leaves out ideas leaves out that learning.
  • What is real in the round. The plan does not really pass; we only imagine it does. The words in the round really are said. Some teams argue that this makes them fair to judge.
  • Limits still apply. The K must still link to this aff. That link requirement keeps the debate tied to what the aff did.

Both lists have strong supporters among coaches and judges. Neither side's view is the "real" rule. The judge decides which side argued better in this round.

Middle grounds

Middle grounds are common, and many judges prefer them. They let both teams keep their core arguments. Some examples:

  • Weigh the plan, but the K can turn it. The aff keeps its advantages. The neg can win that the aff's ideas make those advantages fail.
  • Weigh both. The judge weighs the plan's results and the K's impact on the same scale.
  • The neg gets links to the plan and its reasons, but not to words the aff did not stress. This narrows reps links without banning them.

Teams should say which middle ground they want, if any. If one team offers a middle ground and the other does not answer, many judges will use it.

Win either way

The best framework debaters plan to win even if they lose framework. These are called "even if" arguments.

  • The aff: "Even if you weigh ideas first, our case outweighs, or the perm solves."
  • The neg: "Even if you weigh only the plan, the K turns the case." See Turns case.

If one team wins under both frameworks, framework may not decide the round. That is common.

How to answer it

For the aff (answering the neg's interpretation):

  • Say what your rule lets the neg do. Show that the neg still has ground, such as links to the plan, so your rule is fair.
  • Attack their standards one by one. For example, say that "ideas shape policy" does not prove ideas must come first.
  • Offer or accept a middle ground if you can still win under it. Then go for the "even if."

For the neg (answering the aff's interpretation):

  • Say what your rule asks of the aff. Show that the aff only has to defend its own words and reasons, so the aff can prepare.
  • Answer limits with the link. Say the K must still link to this aff, so the debate stays tied to the 1AC.
  • Say why a K can count under plan focus. Explain how the K turns the case, so you win even if the aff wins framework.

Both sides should answer each standard the other team extends. A dropped standard is easy for a judge to vote on.

How to judge framework against a kritik

  1. Write down each interpretation. Include any middle ground one side offered, and note whether the other side accepted or answered it.
  2. Decide which one won. Compare the standards each side extended, and the weighing between them.
  3. Apply it. List which arguments count under the winning framework. For example, under "plan only," reps links count only if they show the plan is worse.
  4. Check the "even if" arguments. A team that wins under both frameworks may win however you decide this part.
  5. Explain it. In the reason for decision, say which framework won and why. Teams want this part most.

Many judges have a view on this debate, and many say so in their paradigm. Even so, decide the framework debate as the teams argued it.

How to read it: the 2AC asks the judge to weigh the plan. The block offers a counter-interpretation (reps first) and, on the fourth row, a middle ground. The 1AR answers the first three rows but not the middle ground. On the first row, the 2NR says reps first is fair, because the aff picked its own words and can defend them. On the second row, the 2NR does not answer the 1AR's limits point, and the 2AR extends it as dropped. The 2NR also makes an "even if": under the plan framework, the K still turns the case. So even if the aff wins framework, the round may come down to the fourth row: does the K turn the case, or does the plan work anyway?

Common mistakes

  • Aff: framework with no "even if." Fix: also show the case outweighs, or the perm solves, if the judge weighs ideas.
  • Neg: "reps first" with no explanation of what changes. Fix: say what the judge should weigh under your framework, and how that decides the round.
  • Both: treating framework as all or nothing. Fix: offer a middle ground, or answer the one the other side offered.
  • Both: standards with no impact. Fix: say why predictability, limits or education matter, not only that you have them.
  • Neg: ignoring the case under the aff's framework. Fix: argue that the K turns the case, so you can win even if the plan is weighed.
  • Judges: deciding by personal preference. Fix: decide the framework debate as argued, then apply it to the rest of the round.
