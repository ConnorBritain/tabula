Advantages (Adv)

7 min read · Intermediate

An advantage is a good result the plan causes: the affirmative's reason to vote for the plan.

  • Each tells a story: Something bad is happening now, and the plan changes it. That change leads, step by step, to a better world.
  • How to attack it: The negative can attack any step. A broken step makes the whole advantage weaker.

What an advantage is

The 1AC, the first affirmative speech, has a plan (what the aff will do) and one or more advantages (why doing it is good). Each advantage is a separate reason to vote aff. A 1AC usually has one to three. See The affirmative case.

An advantage is built like a chain. It mirrors a disadvantage (DA), the neg's argument that the plan causes something bad. A DA says "the plan starts a chain that ends in harm." An advantage says "the plan starts a chain that ends in a better world."

The parts of an advantage

Teams describe an advantage in two ways. Both describe the same chain.

Harms and inherencyUniquenessWhat is wrong now, and why does it last?"Rural clinics are closing, and no program funds them."

Traditional and stock issues judges often hear advantages in the first set of words. Policy-style judges often hear them in the second. A good team can explain its advantage in either way. See Stock issues.

Uniqueness

On an advantage, uniqueness means the good result is not happening now. This is why the plan is needed. If the world will get the good result anyway, the plan adds nothing, and the advantage is non-unique. In stock issues words, this is the harm plus inherency. See Uniqueness and Inherency.

The link (the plan's action)

The first step from the plan to the result. It is usually proven by the solvency advocate, an expert who says the plan's kind of action works. See Solvency.

Internal links

An internal link is a step between the plan's action and the final impact. Many advantages have more than one: "Funding opens clinics. Clinics lead to early treatment. Early treatment prevents deaths." Each step needs a reason, and ideally a card. Each step is also a place the chain can break.

Internal links work the same way on advantages and on disadvantages. See Internal links for the full page.

Impact

The impact is why the end result matters. It can be large and less likely (preventing a conflict between countries) or smaller and very likely (people keep their health care). How big, how likely, and how soon it is all matter when the judge weighs the advantage. See Impacts and Impact calculus.

Building a strong advantage

  • Keep the chain short. Every extra internal link lowers the chance the impact happens. A short chain is easier to defend.
  • Match each step to the plan. The solvency card should be about what the plan text does, not about a general idea.
  • Give each step a warrant. A warrant is the reason a claim is true. "Clinics lead to early treatment, because people go when a clinic is near."
  • Pick impacts you can weigh. Know whether your impact wins on size, chance, timing or reversibility, and be ready to say so.

Add-on advantages

Some affs read a new advantage in the 2AC, the second affirmative speech. This is called an add-on. It is a new reason to vote aff, and it often answers a neg argument too. Most judges allow add-ons in the 2AC, because it is still a constructive speech (a speech where new arguments are allowed). The neg can then answer it in the 2NC or 1NR. Local rules and judges differ, so read the paradigm.

How to answer an advantage (for the neg)

Each part of the chain is a target. Neg answers to an advantage are on-case arguments, written on the case sheet.

  • Non-unique: the status quo already gets the good result. "A new program already funds clinics."
  • No solvency: the plan does not start the chain. "There are no doctors to staff the clinics."
  • No internal link: a step does not follow. "People skip care because of cost, not distance."
  • Impact defense: the end result is smaller or less likely than claimed.
  • Case turns: the plan makes the problem worse, or the "good" result is actually bad. See Case turns and case defense.

The neg can also answer an advantage off-case. An advantage counterplan gets the same result in a different way, without the plan. See Counterplans.

Most neg teams do not try to beat every step. They pick the weakest one or two, and shrink the advantage enough that a disadvantage or kritik outweighs it.

How to judge an advantage

  1. Trace the chain. For each step, did the aff prove it, and did the neg beat it? A dropped answer on one step can break the chain.
  2. Ask how much is left. Defense on a step rarely erases the advantage. It lowers the chance the impact happens. Weigh the risk that is left.
  3. Check the extension. The aff must extend the advantage into the 2AR, with its key steps and its impact. An advantage the 2AR does not talk about is usually gone.
  4. Weigh it against the neg's best argument. Compare size, chance and timing. See Impact calculus.

How to read it: the 2AC answer on the internal link points to the plan text, and the 2NR dropped it. On uniqueness, the 2AR says the program has only 5 sites, and the neg has no card that it grows. A judge would likely give the aff a real but smaller advantage, then weigh it against the neg's other arguments.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: a long chain with weak steps. Fix: cut steps you cannot defend, and give each one a warrant.
  • Aff: skipping the internal link out loud. Fix: say each step in the 1AC and in rebuttals: "plan, so clinics, so early care, so lives."
  • Aff: extending only the impact. Fix: in the 2AR, extend the key steps too, or the judge cannot see how the plan gets there.
  • Neg: attacking every step a little. Fix: pick the weakest step and win it clearly.
  • Neg: ignoring uniqueness. Fix: if the status quo gets the good result, say so; it can erase the advantage.
  • Judges: treating one strong neg answer as zero risk. Fix: ask whether the answer breaks the chain or only shrinks it.
