The ballot and the RFD

8 min read · Beginner

The ballot is where the judge records the result.

  • What it holds: The winning team, speaker points for each debater, and a short note that explains the decision, called the RFD, the reason for decision.
  • A good RFD: It names the main issue, says which team won it, and says why. It uses the debaters' own arguments, not the judge's opinions.
  • Why it matters: Debaters and coaches read RFDs closely to learn what to fix.

What is on a ballot

Most ballots, paper or online, have:

  • The teams and sides: which team was aff (affirmative, for the plan) and which was neg (negative, against it).
  • The decision: the winning team. There are no ties.
  • Speaker points for each debater, and sometimes ranks (1st to 4th). See Speaker points.
  • The RFD: a written reason.
  • Comments for each debater (optional on many ballots).

A "low-point win" is when the winning team has lower speaker points than the losing team. Many tournaments forbid or flag a low-point win. Follow your tournament's rule.

How to decide: a five-step method

  1. Start at the end. Read the 2NR and 2AR, the last two speeches, on your flow (your notes). List the issues each one said were most important. Common issues are a disadvantage (a harm the plan causes), a counterplan (the neg's other plan), topicality (whether the plan fits the topic) and a kritik (an argument against the ideas behind the plan). Arguments either team dropped before the last two speeches are usually gone from the debate.
  2. Resolve each issue. For each issue, ask who had the better explanation, the better evidence, and the better answers. Use your flow to check what was answered and what was dropped.
  3. Find the framework (the rules for deciding). Did the teams argue about how to decide? For example, weigh the plan's results, or weigh a kritik first. Decide that first, because it tells you what counts.
  4. Weigh. Compare the issues each team won. Use the comparisons the debaters made: size, chance, timing. See Impact calculus.
  5. Decide and check. Pick a winner. Then ask: "Can I explain this using only what the debaters said?" If not, look again.

Do not do the work for a team. If a team never made an argument, do not make it for them. If neither team compared impacts, do your best, and say in the RFD that more comparison would have helped.

Writing the RFD

A clear RFD has four parts. Two to six sentences is enough for most rounds.

  1. The decision: "I vote affirmative."
  2. The main issue: "The round came down to whether the plan's cost causes cuts to school programs."
  3. Why that team won it: "The neg never answered the 2AC point that the plan saves money by year 3. So the link to the disadvantage is turned." (The link is how the plan causes the harm. It is "turned" when it works the other way: here, the plan saves money instead of costing it.)
  4. What would have changed it: "If the 2NR had explained why cuts happen before the savings arrive, I might have voted neg."

The last part is the most useful for the losing team. It tells them what to do next time.

Keep the RFD about arguments. Write about what was said and how it was weighed. Leave out comments about how a debater looks or sounds, except for clear speaking advice (pace, clarity, volume), which belongs in the speaker comments.

Oral decisions

Some tournaments let judges announce the decision and explain it in person after the round. If you do:

  • Give the decision first, then the reason. Do not make them wait.
  • Keep it short: 3 to 5 minutes.
  • Answer questions politely. You do not have to defend your decision line by line. "That is how I saw the round" is fine.
  • Never change a decision because a team argues with you after the round.

Example in flow shorthand

A judge's decision notes, with the RFD written from them.

Key:

  • FW (framework): how to decide.
  • CP (counterplan): the neg's other plan. Here, the 50 states act instead of the federal government.
  • perm (permutation): the aff says, "do both the plan and the CP."
  • DA (disadvantage): a harm the plan causes.
  • Net benefit: a reason the CP alone is better than doing both.
  • Link: how the plan causes the harm. A link card is the quoted evidence for it. No link says the plan does not cause it.
  • Warrant: the reason behind a claim.
  • fed $ = federal money. arg = argument.
Issue 1: FW
  2NR: weigh plan vs CP
  2AR: (agrees)
  Won: no fight

Issue 2: CP
  2NR: states CP solves all
       of case
  2AR: perm: do both; CP can't
       solve fed $
  Won: NEG. Perm answered
       ("both = DA links");
       fed $ arg new in 2AR

Issue 3: DA
  2NR: DA = net benefit
  2AR: no link
  Won: NEG. Link card
       extended; no-link
       had no warrant

Decision: NEG. CP solves the
  case; small DA risk is enough.

How to read it: the judge listed each issue from the last speeches and who won it. The perm was answered, and the 2AR's "federal money" point was new, so the judge did not count it. A short RFD: "I vote neg. The states counterplan solves the whole case. The perm was answered, because doing both still links to the DA. The 2AR's federal money point was new in the last speech, so I did not weigh it. A small risk of the DA is enough to prefer the counterplan."

Common mistakes

  • An RFD that only says who won. Fix: name the issue and the reason.
  • Voting on an argument no one made. Fix: decide from the flow. If you wanted an argument, say so as advice.
  • Counting brand-new arguments in the 2NR or 2AR. Fix: most judges do not weigh new arguments in the last two speeches, unless they answer something new.
  • Skipping the framework question. Fix: if the teams argued about how to decide, resolve that first.
  • Long, unclear RFDs. Fix: four parts, a few sentences each.
  • Arguing with debaters after the round. Fix: explain once, kindly, and stop.

Draft check: it is waiting for a coach to review it.

4 short questions. Get them all right to finish this step. You can try again as many times as you want. No score is kept.

Pick the best RFD

The last two speeches were about one disadvantage. The 2NR said the plan's cost forces cuts to school programs. The 2AR said the plan saves money by year 3. The neg never answered the year-3 point. You vote aff.

Which RFD best explains the decision?

  • "I vote aff. Good job, both teams."
  • "I vote aff. The round came down to the plan's cost. The neg never answered that the plan saves money by year 3, so the link to the disadvantage is turned. If the 2NR had explained why the cuts come before the savings, I might have voted neg."
  • "I vote aff because I think the plan is a good idea in real life."
Show the answer

Answer: "I vote aff. The round came down to the plan's cost. The neg never answered that the plan saves money by year 3, so the link to the disadvantage is turned. If the 2NR had explained why the cuts come before the savings, I might have voted neg."

Why: A good RFD names the decision, the main issue, why that team won it, and what could have changed it. "Good job" says nothing about the arguments. Your own view of the plan should not decide the round.

Where to start

Where should you start?

  • At the 1AC, and read every speech in order
  • At the 2NR and the 2AR, and list the issues each one said were most important
  • At the cross-ex notes
  • At the speech with the most cards

Answer: At the 2NR and the 2AR, and list the issues each one said were most important

Why: Start at the end. The last two speeches tell you what each team is going for. Then read each issue back across your flow to see who won it. Arguments that neither team kept in the last speeches are usually gone.

No weighing

Each team won one issue. Neither team compared the two impacts in the last speeches.

What should you do?

  • Flip a coin
  • Make new arguments for the team you prefer
  • Do your best to compare, and say in your RFD that more comparison would have helped
  • Give the round to the team that spoke last
Show the answer

Answer: Do your best to compare, and say in your RFD that more comparison would have helped

Why: Do not make arguments for a team. But you still must decide. Compare as fairly as you can from what was said, and tell the debaters that comparing impacts would have helped them.

Write a short RFD

You flow this at the end of a round.

2NR
  T: plan is not "substantial"
  ($ change is only 2%)
2AR
  We meet: plan changes 12%,
  above their 10% line
  (2NR never answered 12%)

In words: the neg said the plan is not topical, because it changes funding by only 2 percent. The aff said the plan changes funding by 12 percent, which is above the neg's own 10 percent line. The 2NR never answered the 12 percent point.

Did yours…

  • Did your RFD say who won?
  • Did it name the main issue (topicality, and whether the plan meets the neg's own line)?
  • Did it say why that team won it (the 2NR never answered the 12 percent point)?

Why: A short RFD can still name the decision, the issue and the reason. If you have room, add what would have changed your mind, for example: "If the neg had shown the 12 percent card was wrong, I might have voted neg."
