Timeless examples · 8 min read · intermediate

The politics DA

The politics disadvantage says the plan has a political cost. Leaders have only so much support to spend. If the leader must spend it on the plan, there is less left for another goal the leader cares about, and that goal fails. The failure of that other goal is the harm. The affirmative often answers that the plan is popular, that the other goal will fail or pass anyway, or that this kind of argument does not fit the debate's rules about imagining the plan passes.

The generic shape

A disadvantage (DA) is a neg argument that the plan causes something bad. It has four parts. See Disadvantages. Here is how the politics DA fills them in.

PartThe politics DA saysExample (generic)
UniquenessThe leader's priority will pass now"The leader's top bill has enough votes today."
LinkThe plan costs the leader support"Lawmakers dislike the plan, so pushing it costs the leader goodwill."
Internal linkSupport is limited, and the priority needs it"Without that goodwill, the leader cannot keep the votes for the top bill."
ImpactThe priority's failure is bad"The top bill fails, and the economy suffers."

Political capital is the key idea. It means the goodwill, trust and favors a leader can use to get lawmakers to vote a certain way. The DA treats it like a budget: spend it on one thing, and less is left for another.

Common versions

  • Capital version: the plan is a fight, and the fight uses up capital the priority needs. This is the classic shape.
  • Popularity version: the plan is unpopular with voters or lawmakers, so the leader loses standing.
  • Elections version: the plan changes how voters feel, so the result of a coming election changes, and that result leads to the impact.

Each version needs current evidence. Politics changes week to week, so a politics DA is only as good as its newest uniqueness card.

The usual answers

These answers are for the aff, usually in the 2AC (the second affirmative constructive).

  • Non-unique: the priority will fail anyway. It lacks votes for other reasons.
  • Thumper: the leader is already in other political fights. If those fights did not sink the priority, the plan will not.
  • No link: the plan is popular with lawmakers or voters, or it would pass quietly with little fight.
  • Link turn: winners win. Some evidence says a leader who wins a hard fight gains capital, because success builds strength. If so, the plan helps the priority.
  • No internal link: capital is not key. Lawmakers vote on the priority for their own reasons, such as local interests. The leader's capital does not decide it.
  • No impact: the priority would not do what the neg says, or its failure is not that harmful.
  • Case outweighs: the plan's benefit is bigger than the harm of the priority failing.

As with any DA, do not link turn and impact turn the same politics DA. "The plan helps the priority pass" plus "the priority is bad" argues the plan does harm. See Links and link turns.

The fiat debate

Fiat is the shared idea that the aff gets to imagine the plan passes. It keeps the round on whether the plan should happen, not whether it would. See Fiat.

The politics DA raises a hard question about fiat, because its link often comes from the fight to pass the plan. The aff often says fiat means the plan passes at once, with no fight, and that it is unfair to make the aff debate the leader's whole agenda. The neg often says fiat decides that the plan passes, not how, and that real policymakers weigh political cost. Many judges accept politics DAs, and some give them little weight on fiat grounds. See Fiat for the full arguments on both sides.

How judges weigh it

  • Uniqueness is time-sensitive. Judges often prefer the newest evidence on whether the priority will pass, if a team points out the dates.
  • Link specificity matters. Evidence that lawmakers dislike this kind of plan beats evidence that the leader is "busy."
  • The internal link is often the weak point. Does capital really decide the priority? Many judges ask this.
  • Clear explanation helps with lay judges. A judge with no debate background can follow "the leader has limited support" if it is told as a simple story.

How to read it: on uniqueness, the 2AC says the priority bill lacks votes. The 2NR reads newer evidence that a deal is set. The 2AR says that evidence is one lawmaker's view. On the link, the 2AC reads winners win. The 2NR says winners win fails for unpopular plans. The 2AR extends the link turn: a win shows strength. The 2AC's thumper is a budget fight. The 2NR says that fight is over, and the 2AR does not answer, so the thumper is gone. On fiat, the 2AC says the plan passes at once, with no fight. The 2NR says fiat covers passage, not how, so the cost is a real effect. The 2AR says it is unfair to make the aff debate the whole agenda. On the impact, the 2NR extends the 1NC impact over the 2AC's "no impact," and the 2AR gives no answer on that row. The judge must decide the fiat question, and then whether winners win beats the capital link.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: old uniqueness. Fix: update the priority's status before every tournament.
  • Neg: a link that says only "the leader is busy." Fix: show that lawmakers dislike this kind of plan, or that the fight would be costly.
  • Aff: ignoring the internal link. Fix: ask why capital decides the priority. It is often the weakest step.
  • Aff: winners win plus "the priority is bad." Fix: that is a double turn. Pick one.
  • Both: a fiat argument with no reason. Fix: explain why the link is or is not fair under fiat, not just that it is.
  • Judges: deciding the fiat question before the round. Fix: judge the theory as argued, unless your paradigm says otherwise and the teams knew it.
