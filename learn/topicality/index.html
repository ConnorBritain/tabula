Topicality (T)

7 min read · Beginner

Topicality, or T, is the negative's argument that the plan does not fit the resolution, the season's one topic sentence.

  • The rule: Every season has one resolution. The affirmative's plan has to fit inside it.
  • How the negative argues it: It picks a word in the resolution, says what it should mean, and shows that the plan is outside that meaning. Then it explains why its meaning makes for fairer, better debates.
  • What happens if it wins: If the judge agrees, the affirmative usually loses, because it did not debate the topic.

Why topicality exists

The resolution is announced months before the season. It tells the neg (negative) what to prepare for. If the aff (affirmative) could run any plan at all, the neg could never be ready. Topicality keeps the aff inside the shared space both teams agreed to study.

Topicality is also one of the classic stock issues: the basic jobs an aff must do. See Stock issues.

The four parts

A T argument almost always has four parts. In the 1NC (the neg's first speech) it can be short, often under a minute.

In the table, "affs" means aff cases: the different plans an aff team could run.

PartWhat it saysExample (generic)
InterpretationWhat a word in the resolution means, often from a dictionary or a field expert"'Substantial' means at least a 10% change."
ViolationHow the plan falls outside that meaning"The plan changes funding by only 2%."
StandardsWhy the neg's meaning is better for debate"Limits: small changes allow thousands of tiny affs."
VotersWhy T is a reason to vote neg"Fairness and education. Vote neg."

Interpretation

The interpretation defines a word or phrase in the resolution. Good ones come from a source with a reason to be precise: a law, a legal dictionary, a field expert. A random online dictionary is weaker.

The aff often answers with its own meaning of the word, which the plan does meet. This is a counter-interpretation (CI).

Violation

The violation says how the plan falls outside the interpretation. It should be clear and concrete. Cross-ex is a good place to set it up: "Does your plan change funding by more than 10%?"

Standards

Standards are the reasons one interpretation is better. The common ones:

  • Limits: the meaning keeps the number of possible affs to a size the neg can prepare for. Too many affs means the neg cannot research them all.
  • Ground: the meaning leaves the neg good arguments to make. If an aff is too small or too odd, the neg's usual arguments may not apply. Those include disadvantages (DAs: "the plan causes harm") and counterplans (CPs: "do this other plan instead").
  • Precision: the meaning is the one experts in the field use.
  • Predictability: both teams could expect this meaning before the round, so both could prepare.
  • Education: the meaning leads to better learning about the topic.

The aff makes its own standards for its counter-interpretation. For example: "Our meaning allows more creative affs, which is good for education." Or: "Our meaning is how the field uses the word."

Voters

Voters say why topicality should decide the round. The two usual voters are fairness (the neg could not prepare) and education (debates about the topic teach the most).

The neg also often says T is a "voting issue". That means: if the aff is not topical, the judge should vote neg, not just ignore one argument.

How judges decide T: competing interpretations vs reasonability

This is the biggest choice in a T debate. Teams should argue it, and judges should decide from what is argued.

  • Competing interpretations: the judge picks the better interpretation, like choosing the better of two policies. If the neg's interpretation is even a little better, and the plan violates it, the neg wins T.
  • Reasonability: the judge asks only whether the aff's meaning is good enough. If the aff is reasonably topical, the aff wins T, even if the neg's meaning is a bit better.

Neg teams usually argue for competing interpretations. Aff teams usually argue for reasonability. Many judges default to competing interpretations, but many paradigms say otherwise. A paradigm is a judge's written note on how they decide rounds. Read it before the round.

"In-round abuse." Some judges want the neg to show real harm in this round ("we lost our best DA"). Others say the harm is about the precedent: what debate would look like if this interpretation were allowed. Both views are common.

How to answer T (for the aff)

A full 2AC answer often has:

  1. We meet: the plan meets the neg's interpretation. (If true, this alone answers T.)
  2. Counter-interpretation: a better meaning, which the plan meets.
  3. Standards for the counter-interpretation, and answers to the neg's standards.
  4. Reasonability: the judge should not vote neg if the aff is reasonably topical.
  5. Other arguments. For example: "The neg's interpretation overlimits: it allows only one or two affs."

Two related violations say the plan is topical only through its effects, or does topical things plus extra things. See Effects and extra topicality.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic T argument about the word "substantial." Each group follows one argument through the speeches.

Key: "interp" = interpretation, "CI" = counter-interpretation, "ev" = evidence, "yr" = year, "->" = "leads to". "Legal Dict" and "Field Expert" are invented sources.

T: "substantial"

-- We meet --
1NC  Interp: substantial = at
     least 10% change (Legal Dict)
     Violation: plan is 2% change
2AC  We meet: plan is 12% by yr 2
2NR  No we meet: their ev counts
     yr 2; plan is 2% at start
2AR  Extend we meet: plan is 12%
     when fully in place

-- Counter-interpretation --
2AC  CI: substantial = "of real
     importance" (Field Expert)
2NR  Their CI has no number
     -> no limit
2AR  CI is how the field uses it

-- Standards --
1NC  Limits, ground
2AC  Limits: their interp leaves
     only 3 affs
2NR  Limits outweigh: any change
     counts under the CI
2AR  Reasonability: we are a core
     aff; no prep lost

-- The test --
1NC  Voters: fairness, education
2AC  Reasonability
2NR  Competing interps:
     reasonable = arbitrary
2AR  Reasonability is the
     standard

How to read it: the round turns on two questions. Does "12% by year 2" meet "at least 10%"? And should the judge use competing interpretations or reasonability? A judge would explain both in the RFD (reason for decision).

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a violation that is not clear. Fix: set it up in cross-ex, and say in one sentence how the plan falls outside the interpretation.
  • Neg: standards with no impact. Fix: explain why limits or ground matter: "If any change counts, there are thousands of affs, and the neg cannot be ready for any of them."
  • Aff: no counter-interpretation. Fix: give a meaning the plan meets. Without one, the judge has only the neg's meaning to choose.
  • Aff: "we meet" with no proof. Fix: point to the plan text or a card (a piece of evidence) that shows the plan meets.
  • Both: no argument about competing interpretations vs reasonability. Fix: say which one the judge should use, and why.
  • Judges: voting on T only because "the aff felt off-topic." Fix: decide on the interpretations and standards that were argued.

Check your understanding

5 short questions. Get them all right to finish this step. You can try again as many times as you want. No score is kept.

Find the violation

The resolution asks the government to support public parks. The plan funds a garden that a private club owns. The 1NC reads this T (topicality) argument:

1. "Parks" = land a city or
   state owns for public use
   (Legal Dict)
2. Plan funds a private garden
3. Their meaning lets the aff
   pick any green space, so we
   cannot prepare
4. Fairness: vote neg

In words: sentence 1 says what "parks" means. Sentence 2 says the plan funds a private garden. Sentence 3 says the aff's meaning allows any green space, so the neg cannot prepare. Sentence 4 says vote neg for fairness.

Which sentence is the violation?

  • Sentence 1
  • Sentence 2
  • Sentence 3
  • Sentence 4
Show the answer

Answer: Sentence 2

Why: The violation says how the plan falls outside the interpretation. Sentence 1 is the interpretation: what the word means. Sentence 2 shows the plan is outside that meaning, because a private garden is not land a city or state owns. Sentence 3 is a standard, and sentence 4 is the voter.

Name the part

A different T argument says: "'Reduce' means to cut by at least half. The plan cuts by only 5%. If any small cut counts, there are thousands of possible affs, and the neg cannot research them all. Vote neg for fairness."

What is the sentence "If any small cut counts, there are thousands of possible affs, and the neg cannot research them all"?

  • The interpretation
  • The violation
  • A standard: limits
  • A standard: ground
Show the answer

Answer: A standard: limits

Why: Standards are the reasons one meaning is better for debate. Limits says the meaning keeps the number of possible affs to a size the neg can prepare for. Ground is different: it says the meaning leaves the neg good arguments to make, such as DAs and counterplans.

Does the plan meet?

The 1NC says: "'Substantial' means at least a 10% change (Legal Dict). The plan is too small." The plan text says: "The government will raise school library funding by 15% this year."

Does the plan meet the neg's interpretation, and what can the aff say?

  • Yes. The aff can say "we meet": 15% is more than the 10% line.
  • No. The plan must change funding by exactly 10%.
  • No. The aff must give a counter-interpretation, or it loses T.
  • Yes, but only if the judge uses reasonability.
Show the answer

Answer: Yes. The aff can say "we meet": 15% is more than the 10% line.

Why: "We meet" means the plan meets the neg's own interpretation. A 15% change is at least a 10% change, so the plan is inside the neg's meaning. If that is true, "we meet" alone answers T. Point to the plan text to prove it.

A plan that does not meet

The 1NC says: "'Increase' means new money for the program, not money moved from another program (Field Expert)." The plan text says: "The city will move one million dollars from its road budget to its bus program."

Does the plan meet this interpretation?

  • Yes, because the bus program gets more money
  • No, because the plan moves money from another program, and the interpretation excludes that
  • Yes, because one million dollars is a substantial amount
  • We cannot tell until the neg reads standards
Show the answer

Answer: No, because the plan moves money from another program, and the interpretation excludes that

Why: Read the interpretation, then read the plan text. The interpretation says money moved from another program does not count. The plan moves money from roads to buses, so it is outside that meaning. The aff could still answer with a counter-interpretation: its own meaning of "increase", which the plan meets.

How should the judge decide?

In the 2AR, the aff says: "Even if their meaning of the word is a little better, our meaning is good enough. We are a core aff (a plan at the heart of the topic) on this topic."

Which way of deciding T is the aff asking the judge to use?

  • Competing interpretations: pick the better meaning
  • Reasonability: ask only whether the aff's meaning is good enough
  • A counter-interpretation: a new meaning the plan meets
  • Precision: use the meaning experts use
Show the answer

Answer: Reasonability: ask only whether the aff's meaning is good enough

Why: Reasonability asks only whether the aff is reasonably topical. Under competing interpretations, the judge picks the better meaning, so a slightly better neg meaning would win. Aff teams usually argue for reasonability, and neg teams for competing interpretations. Many judges default to competing interpretations, but many paradigms say otherwise.
