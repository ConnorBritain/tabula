The affirmative case and stock issues · 7 min read · intermediate

Case turns and case defense

Plain version. When the negative argues directly against the affirmative's advantages, it has two kinds of tools. Case defense says the advantage is smaller than claimed, or will not happen. A case turn goes further: it says the plan makes the problem worse. Defense can shrink the affirmative's reasons to vote for it. A turn can give the judge a reason to vote against the plan. Good negative teams often use both.

On-case arguments

The neg's arguments directly against the aff's advantages are called on-case arguments. They go on the case sheet, next to the part of the advantage they answer. Arguments that start on their own sheets, such as disadvantages and counterplans, are off-case. See The affirmative case.

On-case arguments come in two kinds:

KindWhat it doesExample (generic)
DefenseMakes the advantage smaller or stops it"The plan does not work: there are no doctors to staff the clinics."
Offense (a case turn)Says the plan makes things worse"The plan pulls doctors out of city clinics, so more people lose care."

The words offense and defense are used across debate. Offense is a reason to vote for your side. Defense is a reason the other side's argument is weaker.

Case defense

Case defense attacks the steps of an advantage. If it works, the advantage shrinks, and the neg's other arguments become easier to win. The common kinds match the stock issues:

  • Harm defense: the problem is smaller than claimed. See Harms.
  • Inherency or non-unique: the status quo already solves. See Inherency.
  • Solvency defense: the plan does not work, or works only a little. See Solvency.
  • Internal link defense: one step in the chain does not follow. See Advantages.
  • Impact defense: the final result will not happen, or is not as bad as claimed.

Defense alone rarely wins a round in front of a policymaker judge. It makes the advantage smaller, but some risk of the advantage is usually left. The neg then needs something on the other side of the scale, such as a disadvantage. In front of a stock issues judge, defense that clearly wins one stock issue can win the round. See Stock issues.

Defense and presumption

If the neg wins that the plan does nothing at all, some judges vote neg on presumption: with no reason to change, they keep the status quo. Other judges think there is almost always some risk the plan helps. Teams should argue this. See Presumption.

Case turns

A case turn is neg offense on the case sheet. It says the plan does not just fail: it makes things worse. There are two main kinds.

Solvency turns (link turns on the case)

A solvency turn says the plan causes more of the harm it wants to stop. "The plan funds rural clinics by pulling money from city clinics. More people lose care in total." If true, the advantage now points the other way: it becomes a reason to vote neg.

Impact turns on the case

An impact turn says the advantage's "good" result is actually bad. For example, if the aff says the plan grows a certain industry, the neg may argue that growth in that industry causes harm. Impact turns are risky. The neg must defend the claim that the aff's goal is bad, and some impact turns can sound harsh to a judge.

Do not double turn the case

If the neg says the plan cuts a result (a solvency turn) and also that the result is bad (an impact turn), it has said the plan cuts a bad thing, which is a reason to vote aff. This is a double turn; see Links and link turns.

Case turns vs "turns case"

These names sound alike but mean different things.

  • A case turn is an on-case argument. It is about the plan itself: the plan makes the harm worse.
  • "Turns case" is a weighing argument from an off-case position. It says the impact of a disadvantage or kritik would make the aff's harm worse, or stop the plan from working. "The recession from the DA would cut the funding the plan needs."

"Turns case" has its own page. See Turns case.

How to answer case arguments (for the aff)

  • Answer each attack on its line. Put each answer next to the neg argument it answers, so the judge can follow.
  • Extend your evidence. If the 1AC already has a card that answers the attack, say so, and explain it.
  • Answer turns first. A turn is offense. If it stands, the judge has a reason to vote neg. Answer it with a no link (the plan does not cause that), a counter-card, or a reason the turn's impact is small.
  • Point out a double turn. If the neg's turns contradict each other, explain why that helps you.
  • Weigh what is left. Even if the neg shrinks the advantage, explain why the part that is left still outweighs the neg's arguments.

How to judge case arguments

  1. Separate defense from offense. Defense shrinks an advantage. A turn that stands is a reason to vote neg.
  2. Ask how much of each advantage is left. Defense rarely takes an advantage to zero. Weigh the part that is left.
  3. Check every turn for an answer. A dropped turn, explained with a reason, is a strong neg argument.
  4. Watch for double turns. If the neg made both kinds of turn on one chain, the result may help the aff, but only if the aff points it out.
  5. Follow the rule the teams argued. A stock issues judge asks whether the neg clearly won a stock issue. A policymaker weighs the risk that is left against the neg's offense.

How to read it: the 1AC plan text gives new funds for rural clinics and staff training. The 1NC made two defense arguments and one solvency turn: the plan's funds come out of city clinic budgets, so more people lose care in total. The aff answered all three in the 2AC, and answered the turn with a no link. The 2NR extends the staff defense and the turn, but drops the 2AC answer on impact defense, so the 2AR calls it conceded. The key clash is the turn: does the plan's money come from city clinic budgets? The 2AR reads the plan text, which says "new funds," to show there are no cuts. If the judge agrees, the turn is gone, and the aff keeps most of its advantage.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: no case arguments at all. Fix: even two or three defense arguments make the advantage smaller.
  • Neg: only defense, with nothing to weigh. Fix: pair case defense with a turn or a disadvantage, so the judge has a reason to vote neg.
  • Neg: double turning the case. Fix: on one chain, pick solvency turns or impact turns, not both.
  • Aff: answering defense but dropping the turn. Fix: answer turns first; they are offense.
  • Aff: mixing up a case turn and "turns case." Fix: a case turn is about the plan; "turns case" is about a DA or K impact. Answer each on its own sheet.
  • Judges: treating defense as offense. Fix: defense shrinks the advantage; it does not by itself give the neg an impact to weigh.
