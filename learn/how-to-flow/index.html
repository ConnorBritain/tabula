How to flow

9 min read · Beginner

Flowing is how you take notes in a debate.

  • The layout: you give each speech its own column. You write each argument in a few words, in the order it was said.
  • Reading across: you put each answer next to the argument it answers. Then you can read across a row and follow one argument from start to end.
  • Dropped arguments: if a row stops early, that argument was dropped.
  • Why it matters: a good flow lets a judge decide the round, and lets a debater know what to say next.

Why flowing matters

Debate moves fast. Without a flow, you remember the loudest points, not the best ones. The flow shows three things memory gets wrong:

  • What was said. Not what you think was said.
  • What was answered. Each answer sits next to its argument.
  • What was dropped. An argument with no answer next to it is called a drop or a concession. Most judges treat a dropped argument as true, if it came with a reason.

Set up your sheets

Use one sheet (a page, or a tab in Tabula) for each big argument. The aff (affirmative) is the team for the plan. The neg (negative) is the team against it. Common sheets:

SheetWhat goes on it
Case (or one sheet per advantage)The aff's plan and its reasons: the problem (harms), the good results (advantages), and how the plan fixes it (solvency). Also the neg's answers.
TEach topicality argument: the plan does not fit the topic
DAEach disadvantage (a harm the plan causes), one sheet each
CPEach counterplan (a different plan from the neg)
KEach kritik (an argument against the ideas behind the plan)
FWFramework (how the judge should decide), if it is its own debate

On each sheet, make one column per speech. The aff's arguments start in the 1AC column, the first speech. The neg's own arguments (T, DA, CP, K) start in the 1NC column, the neg's first speech. These are called off-case arguments.

Case sheet columns:
1AC|1NC|2AC|2NC/1NR|1AR|2NR|2AR

DA sheet columns:
1NC|2AC|2NC/1NR|1AR|2NR|2AR

The 2NC and 1NR share a column. They come back to back, and the neg splits the arguments between them.

Tip: use two colors, one for aff and one for neg. Then you can see at a glance who said the last word on each row.

Write less than you hear

You cannot write every word. Do not try. For each argument, write:

  1. The claim, in 2 to 5 words.
  2. The source, if it is a card: the author and year ("Analyst 21").
  3. The warrant (the reason it is true), if you can, in a few words.

The warrant matters most. "DA: econ collapse" (the plan hurts the economy) tells you the claim. "Plan costs $ -> cuts other programs" tells you why. Rebuttals (the last four speeches) are won by explaining why.

Line up the answers

When the 2AC (the aff's second speech) answers the 1NC, write each answer next to the argument it answers. Number the answers ("1, 2, 3") if the speaker does. This is the most important habit in flowing. If you line up answers well, a dropped argument jumps out at you as an empty space.

If a speaker jumps around, leave space and draw an arrow. A messy flow that is lined up beats a neat flow that is not.

Listen for signposts

Good debaters tell you where they are. These words are called signposts:

  • "On the DA..." (which sheet)
  • "Go to the link debate..." (which part of the argument)
  • "Their number 3 says..." (which argument)
  • "Off-case: one off is T, two is the DA..." (the order of the neg's new sheets)

At the start of a speech, many debaters give a roadmap: the order of sheets. Use it to put your sheets in order before the speech starts.

Flowing as a judge

A judge's flow has one more job: it is the record of the decision.

  • Flow the whole round, both teams, all speeches. Do not stop when you think you know who is winning.
  • Mark drops. A small star or a circle next to an argument nobody answered helps a lot at decision time.
  • Mark new arguments in late speeches. If a brand-new argument appears in the 2NR or 2AR (the last two speeches), note it. Most judges do not count new arguments in the last rebuttals, unless they answer something new.
  • Flow cross-ex lightly. Write down key answers ("CX: plan costs $5B") in the margin. They count only if a team uses them in a speech.
  • Read your flow before you decide. Start at the 2NR and 2AR columns. Read each row back to the left. Ask: who won this row, and why?

You can also ask for the speech documents (the files of quoted evidence, called cards). They show the cards, not the analysis. The flow is still the record of what was argued.

Example in flow shorthand

One disadvantage (DA: a harm the plan causes), flowed through the 2NR. The full DA is explained in Disadvantages.

On paper, each part of the DA is one row, read left to right. So that it fits a phone, the example shows each row top to bottom, one speech per line.

Key:

  • U (uniqueness): the harm is not happening now.
  • L (link): how the plan causes it.
  • IL (internal link): the middle step.
  • I (impact): the final harm.
  • Block: the 2NC and 1NR together.
  • Turns case: the DA's harm makes the aff's own problem worse.
  • $ = money. > = outweighs. yr = year. progs = programs. \* = dropped.
DA: Spending

Row 1: U
1NC:   budget tight now
       (Analyst 21)
2AC:   1. N/U: a big bill
       already passed
Block: That bill is done.
       No new $ this yr
1AR:   Ext N/U
2NR:   No new $ this yr.
       Their card is old

Row 2: L
1NC:   plan costs $
       (Expert 20)
2AC:   2. L/T: plan saves $
       (Author 22)
Block: Savings take 5 yrs.
       Cost is now
1AR:   Ext L/T: plan pays
       for itself
2NR:   Savings come too late
       to matter

Row 3: IL
1NC:   cost forces cuts to
       school progs
2AC:   3. No IL: no cut shown
Block: Law requires cuts
       when $ is short
1AR:   (no answer) *
2NR:   IL dropped in 1AR

Row 4: I
1NC:   kids lose services
2AC:   4. Case outweighs
Block: DA turns case: same
       kids hurt
1AR:   Case > DA
2NR:   DA turns case. 1AR
       never answered the turn

How to read it: each row is one part of the DA. Follow a row to follow one argument. The 1AR did not answer the internal link row (marked *). The 2NR points that out. A judge reading across the row sees the gap at once.

Common mistakes

  • Writing everything. Fix: write the claim and the reason in a few words. Skip filler.
  • Not lining up answers. Fix: put each answer next to the argument it answers, even if you must leave white space.
  • Flowing only one side. Fix: flow both teams. A judge's flow that has only one team's arguments cannot show drops.
  • Stopping during rebuttals. Fix: rebuttals decide the round. Flow them closely.
  • Writing the tag but not the warrant. Fix: add the "because" in 2 or 3 words.
  • Using a new symbol mid-round. Fix: pick your symbols before the season and stick to them.

Find the drop

You flow this on the DA sheet.

1NC              2AC
DA: Spending
U: budget        1. N/U: big
   tight now        bill already
                    passed
L: plan costs    (no answer)
   $50B
I: school        2. Case
   programs cut     outweighs

In words: the 1NC read uniqueness, a link and an impact. The 2AC answered the uniqueness line and the impact line. The 2AC said nothing about the link: that the plan costs fifty billion dollars.

Which part of the DA did the 2AC drop?

  • The uniqueness
  • The link
  • The impact
  • Nothing was dropped
Show the answer

Answer: The link

Why: Read across each row. The link row has an empty space in the 2AC column. That empty space is a drop. Lining up answers next to the arguments they answer makes drops easy to see.

What a drop means

The neg made an argument with a clear reason. The aff never answered it. In the 2NR, the neg says: "They dropped it."

How do most judges treat this argument?

  • As true, because it had a reason and no answer
  • As false, because the aff did not talk about it
  • As a reason to stop the round
  • As worth nothing until the judge checks the evidence
Show the answer

Answer: As true, because it had a reason and no answer

Why: An argument with no answer is called a drop or a concession. Most judges treat a dropped argument as true, if it came with a reason. The judge still decides how much it matters in the round.

Why line up answers

A new judge asks why the flow puts each answer on the same row as the argument it answers.

What is the main reason?

  • It makes the notes look neat
  • It shows which arguments were answered and which were dropped
  • It saves paper
  • It tells you which team spoke faster
Show the answer

Answer: It shows which arguments were answered and which were dropped

Why: When each answer sits next to its argument, an argument with no answer shows as an empty space. A messy flow that is lined up is more useful than a neat flow that is not.

What to write

A speaker says: "Their plan costs a lot of money, so the law will force cuts to school programs, and children will lose services."

What should you write on your flow?

  • Every word the speaker said
  • The claim and the reason in a few words, such as "plan costs $ -> forced cuts -> kids lose services"
  • Only the word "DA"
  • Nothing, because the speech document has it
Show the answer

Answer: The claim and the reason in a few words, such as "plan costs $ -> forced cuts -> kids lose services"

Why: You cannot write every word, so do not try. Write the claim and the reason in a few words. The reason (the "because") is what wins rebuttals. The speech document has the cards, but not all of the explanation.
