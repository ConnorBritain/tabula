Stock issues

6 min read · Beginner

The stock issues are a checklist of five things the affirmative must show to win.

  • The problem: There is a real problem (harms), it will not fix itself (inherency), and it is big enough to matter (significance).
  • The plan: The plan fixes the problem (solvency), and the plan fits the topic (topicality).
  • How some judges decide: Some judges decide the whole round on this checklist. If the negative wins any one item, the negative wins.

The five stock issues

Two words first. The status quo is the world now, without the plan. The resolution is the topic statement that every team debates.

Stock issueThe questionNeg attack, in plain words
HarmsIs there a real problem now?"The problem is not real, or not bad."
InherencyWill the problem last without the plan?"The status quo is already fixing it."
SolvencyDoes the plan fix the problem?"The plan does not work."
SignificanceIs the problem, or the fix, big enough to matter?"The change is too small to care about."
TopicalityDoes the plan fit the resolution?"The plan is off the topic."

A common memory aid: the aff must prove a significant harm, that is inherent, which the plan solves, with a topical plan.

Harms

Harms are the bad things happening now. The aff shows who is hurt, how, and how many. Harms are often stated as the impact (why it matters) of an advantage (a reason the plan is good). See Harms.

Inherency

Inherency says the harm is built into the status quo. Something keeps it from being fixed. There are a few kinds:

  • Structural: a law or rule blocks the fix.
  • Attitudinal: the people in charge do not want to fix it.
  • Existential: the harm exists and nothing now solves it, so something must be blocking a fix.
  • Gap: the current law or program leaves a hole, and the harm falls through it.

Inherency is why the aff needs a plan. If the world is already solving the problem, the plan adds nothing. See Inherency.

Solvency

Solvency says the plan works. It is usually proven with expert evidence that the plan's action reduces the harm. Solvency does not need to be total. Fixing half of a big problem can still be worth it. See Solvency.

Significance

Significance says the harm, or the plan's effect on it, is large enough to matter. It can be measured in numbers (how many people) or in quality (how serious, such as loss of life or rights). Many judges fold significance into harms and solvency. Traditional judges often treat it as its own item. See Significance.

Topicality

Topicality says the plan fits inside the resolution. It is the only stock issue usually argued on its own sheet (its own page of notes), apart from the case. Debaters call that an off-case position. See Topicality.

Why stock issues matter

For judges. Some judges, often called stock issues judges, use this checklist as their decision rule. The aff must win all five. If the neg clearly wins one, the neg wins the round. That is true even if the neg has no disadvantage (an argument that the plan causes something bad). This view is common among traditional and lay judges (judges with no debate background). It is also common on circuits (groups of tournaments) with many parent and community judges.

For debaters. Even judges who do not use the checklist strictly still expect the aff to prove each item. Say the neg wins "the status quo solves" or "the plan does not work." Then the aff's advantage is tiny, and every other neg argument gets stronger. So stock issues are useful in every round.

Adapt. A judge's paradigm is a short statement of how they judge. If it says "stock issues," make your arguments in those words. Say "they lose inherency, so you vote neg." Do not only say "their advantage is non-unique" (the policy-style words for "the good result happens anyway").

How to answer stock issue attacks (for the aff)

  • Harms: read more or better evidence about the harm, and explain who is hurt and how.
  • Inherency: show why the status quo is not solving. Point to the barrier.
  • Solvency: explain how the plan works, step by step. Read solvency cards (quoted evidence, with author and year) that match the plan text.
  • Significance: put a number or a clear quality on the harm, and say why even partial solvency matters.
  • Topicality: see Topicality for the full answer.

How to judge with stock issues

  1. Decide which rule applies. If the teams argue that stock issues are the rule, use them. If they argue for weighing advantages against disadvantages, do that. If no one says, use your paradigm.
  2. Go through the five items. For each one, did the aff prove it? Did the neg clearly beat it?
  3. Be careful with "clearly." Under a strict checklist, a small doubt on solvency is not the same as no solvency. Many stock issues judges ask whether the neg won the issue, not whether it raised a question.
  4. If no one wins clearly, some judges use presumption: the tie goes to the neg, because the aff must prove change is good. See Presumption.

Example in flow shorthand

A neg strategy built only on stock issues, in front of a traditional judge. Each stock issue is shown with the five speeches that argue it.

Key: Inh = inherency, Solv = solvency, Sig = significance, SQ = status quo, Ext = extend (carry forward), Ev = evidence, K = thousand. "(Ev 21)" is an invented card.

1AC  No law requires
     safety checks
1NC  SQ solves: new state
     rule passed (Ev 21)
2AC  State rule covers 3
     states only
2NR  Ext: 3 states have
     most of the problem
2AR  3 states = 20% of
     problem; 80% left

SOLVENCY

     40%

     inspectors to do it

     minor

2NR  (dropped)

How to read it: the neg went for inherency and solvency, and dropped significance. On solvency, the plan text decides it. A stock issues judge checks each item; if the aff wins all of them, the aff wins.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: assuming inherency is obvious. Fix: read a card that names the barrier, and extend it when the neg says the status quo solves.
  • Neg: raising a stock issue without proving it. Fix: "they do not solve" needs a reason and, ideally, a card.
  • Neg: going for five stock issues in the 2NR. Fix: pick the one or two you are clearly winning, and explain why each one alone is a reason to vote neg.
  • Both: forgetting the judge's paradigm. Fix: read it. A stock issues judge wants to hear those words.
  • Judges: treating any doubt as a loss for the aff. Fix: ask which team won the issue as argued.

Already fixing it

The aff plan: the federal government requires smoke alarms in all rental homes. The aff says renters die in fires because many rental homes have no alarm.

The neg says: "Most states passed new alarm laws last year. The status quo is already fixing the problem." Which stock issue does this answer attack?

  • Harms
  • Inherency
  • Solvency
  • Topicality
Show the answer

Answer: Inherency

Why: Inherency asks whether the problem will last without the plan. The neg says the world now is already fixing it. If that is true, the plan adds little.

Will it work?

The aff plan: the federal government requires safety checks on all school buses. The aff says the checks will cut bus accidents.

The neg says: "There are no inspectors to do the checks. The plan will not work." Which stock issue does this answer attack?

  • Harms
  • Inherency
  • Solvency
  • Significance
Show the answer

Answer: Solvency

Why: Solvency asks whether the plan fixes the problem. The neg does not say the problem is fake. It says the plan will not fix it.

Does it fit?

The topic says the federal government should change its policy on education. The aff plan: the federal government funds new roads in rural areas.

The neg says: "Roads are not education. The plan is off the topic." Which stock issue does this answer attack?

  • Harms
  • Inherency
  • Solvency
  • Topicality
Show the answer

Answer: Topicality

Why: Topicality asks whether the plan fits the resolution, the topic statement. It is usually argued on its own sheet, apart from the case.

Is the problem real?

The aff plan: the federal government bans a certain cleaning product in schools. The aff says the product makes students sick.

The neg says: "Their only card is about factory workers who use the product all day. There is no proof that it makes any student sick." Which stock issue does this answer attack?

  • Harms
  • Inherency
  • Solvency
  • Topicality
Show the answer

Answer: Harms

Why: Harms asks whether there is a real problem now. The neg says the aff has not proven that students are hurt at all. That attacks the harm, not the plan.

Too small to matter

The aff plan: the federal government puts a warning label on a certain toy. The aff says children get hurt by the toy.

The neg says: "Their own card says about ten children a year get a small cut. That is too small to care about." Which stock issue does this answer attack?

  • Significance
  • Inherency
  • Solvency
  • Topicality
Show the answer

Answer: Significance

Why: Significance asks whether the problem is big enough to matter. The neg agrees the harm happens, but says it is too small. Many judges fold significance into harms. Traditional judges often treat it as its own item, and a stock issues judge may vote neg if the neg wins it.
