Judging your first round

Plain version. You do not need debate experience to judge a fair round. Your job is to listen, take notes, and decide which team made the better case, based only on what they said. Do not add your own opinions about the topic. If you are confused, it is fine to say so in your decision. Students are trained to explain things to judges like you. Many circuits rely on parent and community judges, and those judges are welcome and needed.

What to bring

  • Paper and two pens in different colors, or a laptop. (Tabula is free and keeps your notes on your own computer.)
  • A watch or phone timer. Debaters usually time themselves, but you should time too.
  • Water and a snack. Rounds take about 90 minutes.
  • Your judge code or login, if the tournament uses an online ballot.

Before the round

  1. Check in at the judge table, or online. You will get your room and the two teams.
  2. Get to the room on time. The round cannot start without you.
  3. Tell the debaters you are new, if you like. Something like: "This is my first round. Please speak clearly, and explain your arguments." That is a normal request. Good debaters will adjust.
  4. Ask about the speech documents. Many teams share their evidence by email or a shared folder. You can ask to be included. It helps you follow along. You do not have to read every word.
  5. Ask who is keeping time, and how much prep time each team gets.

During the round

  • Take notes on each speech. Use one column per speech. Write a few words per argument. See How to flow. Even rough notes help a lot.
  • Listen for what each team says is most important. The last two speeches (2NR and 2AR) should tell you.
  • Stay neutral in your face. Try not to nod or frown at arguments.
  • Do not argue with the debaters. You can ask them to slow down or speak up. Say "clear" if a speaker is too fast to understand.
  • Do not decide early. Rounds often change in the last speeches.

After the round: deciding

Take a few minutes alone. Then ask yourself:

  1. What was the main fight in the last two speeches?
  2. Which team explained its side of that fight better, with reasons?
  3. Did either team ignore an important argument from the other side?
  4. Which team told you how to compare the arguments? Did that make sense?

Vote for the team that won the most important issue. If it is close, trust your notes over your feelings.

What not to vote on: your own view of the policy, which school the team is from, how the team is dressed, or which team you liked more as people.

Filling out the ballot

The ballot has three parts. See The ballot and the RFD for more.

  1. The decision: circle or click the winning team (aff or neg).
  2. Speaker points: a score for each debater, often on a scale near 25 to 30. A solid, clear speech is about a 28. See Speaker points.
  3. The RFD (reason for decision): two to five sentences on why you voted as you did. Name the issue and the reason.

Some tournaments ask you to tell the debaters your decision out loud. Others do not. Follow the tournament's rule.

If you are confused

It is fine. Say so, kindly, in your RFD. For example: "I did not understand how the counterplan was different from the plan. The aff explained the plan's benefits more clearly, so I voted aff." That is useful feedback. It teaches the debaters to explain better.

Conduct and safety

  • Keep the door open or follow the tournament's rules about adults and students in rooms.
  • Be kind in feedback. Comment on arguments and speaking, never on a student's looks, voice or identity.
  • Report problems to the tournament staff: rude or unsafe behavior, a team that will not stop, or evidence that seems made up. You do not have to handle it alone.
  • See Round conduct, safety and evidence ethics.

Example in flow shorthand

A tiny piece of a judge's notes, at decision time.

2NR                          2AR                            Judge note
DA: plan costs $ ->          Plan saves $ by yr 3           Main fight = cost.
cuts to schools now          (they never answered yr 3)     2NR did not answer
                                                            yr-3 savings.
                                                            -> Aff wins cost.
                                                            Vote AFF.

How to read it: the judge wrote down the main fight from each last speech. The neg never answered the aff's "savings by year 3" point. So the judge voted aff and wrote that reason in the RFD.

Common mistakes

  • Voting on your own opinion of the topic. Fix: decide only from what the debaters said.
  • Not taking notes. Fix: even a few words per argument will help you decide.
  • Deciding in the first speech. Fix: wait for the last two speeches. They often change things.
  • An RFD that says only "good job." Fix: name the issue that decided the round and why.
  • Giving very low speaker points for small mistakes. Fix: most debaters land between 27 and 29. Save very low points for rude or unsafe behavior.
  • Being afraid to say you were confused. Fix: say it kindly. It helps the debaters improve.

Keeping time

Before the round, a debater asks: "Will you keep time for us too?"

What should you do?

  • Say yes, and time the speeches and prep on your watch or phone
  • Say no, because only debaters keep time
  • Say yes, but time only the first speech
  • Ask the other team to decide for you
Show the answer

Answer: Say yes, and time the speeches and prep on your watch or phone

Why: Debaters usually time themselves, but the judge should time too. It is a normal request.

New in the 2AR

You flow this in the last two speeches.

2AC                   2NR                       2AR
No link: plan         Ext link: plan costs $    Link turn: plan SAVES $
costs nothing         (Analyst 21)              -> DA is a reason to vote aff

In words: in the 2AC, the aff said there was no link. In the 2NR, the neg extended its link. In the 2AR, the aff said for the first time that the plan saves money, so the disadvantage helps the aff.

How do most judges treat the 2AR's link turn?

  • Count it, because the 2AR is the last speech
  • Not count it, because it is new, and the neg had no speech left to answer it
  • Count it only if the aff reads a card
  • Give the round to the neg, because new arguments are cheating
Show the answer

Answer: Not count it, because it is new, and the neg had no speech left to answer it

Why: The link turn was not in the 2AC, so it is new in the 2AR. The neg has no speech left to answer it. Most judges do not count it. Some judges want the neg to point this out in the round, but most will not count it either way. It does not end the round by itself.

What to vote on

At the end of a round, you think about how to decide.

Which of these should decide your vote? Pick all that apply.

  • Which team won the most important issue in the last two speeches
  • Which team explained its side with better reasons
  • Whether you agree with the plan in real life
  • Which team you liked more as people
Show the answer

Answer: Which team won the most important issue in the last two speeches; Which team explained its side with better reasons

Why: Decide only from what the debaters said. Look at the main fight in the 2NR and 2AR, and at who explained it better. Your own view of the policy, and how much you liked a team, should not count.

If you are confused

You did not understand how the counterplan was different from the plan. The aff explained the plan's benefits clearly.

What is a good thing to do?

  • Vote for the team with the most cards, and do not mention it
  • Decide from your notes, and say kindly in your RFD that you did not understand the counterplan
  • Refuse to sign the ballot
  • Ask the debaters after the round which team should win
Show the answer

Answer: Decide from your notes, and say kindly in your RFD that you did not understand the counterplan

Why: It is fine to be confused. Decide from what you understood. Saying so kindly in the RFD is useful feedback, because it teaches the debaters to explain better.
