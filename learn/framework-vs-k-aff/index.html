Framework against a kritikal affirmative (T-USFG)

9 min read · Advanced

Framework is the negative's common answer to a kritikal affirmative, or K aff: an affirmative that does not defend a government plan.

  • The neg's case: Framework argues the aff should have defended government action that fits the topic. The neg says this makes the game fair and the debate better.
  • The aff's case: The neg's rule leaves out important ideas and people, and the aff's own approach teaches more.
  • How judges decide: The judge decides which model of debate is better, based on what the teams argued.

Names and shape

This argument goes by several names. Many teams call it framework or FW. Others call it T-USFG, short for "topicality: United States federal government," because many resolutions name the federal government as the actor. Some circuits say T-resolved, T-must defend the resolution, or simply T. The idea is the same.

It has the same shape as topicality. See Topicality. The neg offers an interpretation (a rule for what the aff must do), shows a violation (the aff breaks the rule), gives standards (reasons the rule is good), and ends with voters (reasons the rule should decide the round).

This page covers framework against an aff with no plan. Framework against a neg kritik is a different debate. See Framework against a kritik. To learn what K affs are and why teams run them, see Kritikal affirmatives.

The neg's case

Interpretation. A common version: "The aff must defend a topical action by the United States federal government." Some versions are narrower, such as "the aff must defend a plan." Some are wider, such as "the aff must defend the resolution."

Violation. "The aff does not defend government action." This is usually clear from the 1AC and cross-ex.

Standards. The neg picks from these. Each one is a reason the neg's model of debate is better.

  • Limits. The topic sets a boundary. Without it, the aff could defend almost anything, and the neg could not prepare for it all.
  • Ground. A plan gives the neg arguments it can count on, such as disadvantages and counterplans. Without a plan, those arguments may not apply.
  • Predictability. The resolution is public months before the season. Both teams can research the same question.
  • Fairness. Limits, ground and predictability together give both teams an equal chance to win. Many negs argue fairness is an impact on its own: a game must be fair to be worth playing, and fairness is the one thing a ballot can fully protect.
  • Clash. A shared, stable starting point makes both teams answer each other directly. Many negs say clash is what makes debate educational, because ideas get tested against their best answers.
  • Skills. Debating government action teaches research, policy analysis and how to argue for concrete change. Negs often call these portable skills: skills students carry into life.

Defensive answers to the aff's offense. The neg often adds two arguments.

  • Topical version of the aff (TVA). A plan that fits the topic and keeps the aff's main ideas. The neg says the aff could make its critique while defending that plan.
  • Switch-side debate (SSD). The aff's ideas can be argued on the negative, as a kritik. So the neg's rule does not shut those ideas out of debate.

Voters. Fairness, clash, or both. The neg asks the judge to vote neg because the aff's model makes debate worse.

The aff's case

We meet. Some affs argue they meet the neg's interpretation ("we meet" it), at least in part. For example, the aff may defend some action related to the topic, or the 1AC may discuss government action directly. A we meet is strongest when the aff is closer to the plan end of the range, that is, closer to a usual plan.

Counter-interpretation. The aff offers its own rule for what an aff must do. A common version: "The aff must be in the direction of the topic," or "The aff must relate to the resolution." The aff then argues its model still limits debate, because the topic's subject sets the boundary.

Impact turns. The aff argues that the neg's model is not only weaker but harmful.

  • Exclusion. A rule that requires a government plan leaves out some ideas, ways of arguing and people. The aff says this costs debate more than the neg's rule gains.
  • The skills cut both ways. Training students to speak as if they were the government can teach them to see problems from the government's view, and to overlook those it affects. The aff says that is a harm, not a benefit.
  • The neg's model repeats the aff's harm. If the aff's critique is right, a rule that forces the aff into the criticized frame proves the critique.

Education and access. The aff argues its model teaches more. Students study the topic's subject through a wider set of ideas. Debaters who do not see themselves in the usual frame can take part on their own terms. The aff says this keeps the activity open and alive.

Answers to the neg's standards.

  • Fairness is not an impact by itself. It matters only because it serves education. Some affs say this and then argue their model gives better education.
  • Limits are overstated. The topic's subject is still a limit. The neg can also prepare general answers, such as kritiks and case arguments.
  • The TVA does not solve. A plan would change what the aff says. The method or the critique of government action is the core of the aff, and a plan drops it.
  • SSD does not solve. Arguing these ideas only on the neg treats them as a way to answer others, not as a stance a team can defend.

How the debate comes together

By the last speeches, most framework debates come down to a few questions.

  • Which interpretation is better? Like topicality, the judge may compare the two models. Some judges use a version of competing interpretations (pick the better model). Others ask whether the aff's model is reasonable. See Topicality voters.
  • Which impact comes first? The neg's fairness or clash, or the aff's exclusion or education? Each side should say why its impact matters more.
  • Does the TVA or SSD answer the aff's offense? If yes, the aff's impact turns may be smaller. If no, they may outweigh.
  • Is there a middle ground? Some counter-interpretations ask for less than a plan, but more than nothing. Judges often find these useful.

Many negs choose between two main stories in the last speech: fairness (the game must be fair) or clash (debate needs a stable point of disagreement to teach anything). Many affs choose between a counter-interpretation story (our model is better and still limited) and an impact turn story (the neg's model does harm).

How to judge framework against a K aff

  1. Write down each model. What must an aff do under each? Note any we meet.
  2. List the impacts each side extended. Fairness, clash, skills; or exclusion, education, access.
  3. Check the defense. Did the TVA or SSD answer the aff's impact turns? Did the aff's answers shrink the neg's limits claim?
  4. Weigh as the teams told you to. Which impact did each team say comes first, and why?
  5. Explain it. In the reason for decision, name the model you chose and the reason that decided it.

Judges hold strong and varied views on this debate. Some lean toward framework, some lean toward K affs, and many try to decide it purely on the flow. Most state their view in their paradigm. Even so, decide the debate the teams had. See Judge paradigms.

Example in flow shorthand

A neg framework argument against a K aff with no plan. The middle speeches are left out. The TVA first came in the neg block. Each group starts with one 1NC line (the neg's first speech). Then come the 2AC (the aff's answer), the 2NR and the 2AR (each side's last speech). Key: USFG = United States federal government. CI = counter-interpretation. Std = standard. Prep = prepare. X = any action in the topic.

INTERP / WE MEET
1NC: interp: aff must defend
     USFG action in the
     resolution
2AC: 1. We meet: aff is about
     the topic
2NR: no we meet: being about it
     is not defending it
2AR: (not extended)

VIOLATION / CI
1NC: violation: no plan
2AC: 2. CI: aff must be in the
     topic's direction
2NR: CI has no limit: any aff
     can claim a direction
2AR: limit is the topic's
     subject; neg can prep that

LIMITS AND FAIRNESS
1NC: std: limits and fairness
2AC: 3. Fairness only serves
     education
2NR: fairness first: no fair
     round, no learning
2AR: exclusion outweighs: their
     model shuts out our method

CLASH AND SKILLS
1NC: std: clash and skills
2AC: 4. Impact turn: their model
     excludes our method
2NR: TVA: a plan that does X
     keeps the aff's core
2AR: TVA drops the core: a plan
     changes what we say

How to read it: in the first group, the 2NR says being about the topic is not the same as defending it, and the 2AR does not extend the we meet. In the second group, the 2NR says the counter-interpretation has no limit, and the 2AR answers that the topic's subject is the limit. In the third group, the 2NR says fairness comes first, because there is no learning without a fair round. The 2AR says exclusion outweighs. In the fourth group, the 2NR answers the impact turn with a TVA, and the 2AR says the TVA drops the aff's core. The round turns on two questions: is the aff's counter-interpretation limited enough, and does fairness or exclusion matter more? A judge who thinks the TVA keeps the aff's core will find the neg ahead. A judge who thinks it does not will find the aff's impact turn stronger.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: standards with no impact. Fix: say why limits or clash matter, and which voter they lead to.
  • Neg: no TVA or SSD. Fix: give a defensive answer to the aff's impact turns, so they do not outweigh by default.
  • Aff: impact turns with no model. Fix: also say what your model of debate allows, or the neg's limits claim goes unanswered.
  • Aff: ignoring fairness. Fix: answer it directly. Say why it is not first, or why your model is fair enough.
  • Both: no weighing. Fix: say which impact comes first, and why, in every rebuttal.
  • Judges: deciding on your view of K affs. Fix: decide the framework debate as argued, and explain the reason in the RFD.
