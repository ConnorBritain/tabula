Policy debate glossary

17 min read · Beginner

Debate has a lot of special words, and this page lists them in A to Z order, in plain words.

  • How to use it: Each entry makes sense on its own, so you can look up one word in the middle of a round. When a term has its own page, the entry links to it.
  • If you are new: Start with Debate 101, and come back here when you hear a word you do not know.

Two words come up in almost every entry. The aff (affirmative) is the team for the plan. The neg (negative) is the team against it.

Numbers

  • 1AC (first affirmative constructive): the first speech of the round. The aff (the team for the plan) reads its plan and its reasons. See Speeches and times.
  • 1AR (first affirmative rebuttal): the 6th speech. The aff (the team for the plan) has 5 minutes to answer the whole negative block: the neg's two speeches in a row, 13 minutes in all.
  • 1NC (first negative constructive): the 2nd speech, and the neg's first. The neg (the team against the plan) starts all its arguments here.
  • 1NR (first negative rebuttal): the 5th speech. It is the second half of the negative block: two neg speeches in a row, the 2NC and then the 1NR.
  • 2AC (second affirmative constructive): the 3rd speech. The aff (the team for the plan) answers everything in the 1NC, the neg's first speech.
  • 2AR (second affirmative rebuttal): the 8th speech, by the aff (the team for the plan). It is the last speech of the round.
  • 2NC (second negative constructive): the 4th speech. It is the first half of the negative block: two neg speeches in a row, the 2NC and then the 1NR.
  • 2NR (second negative rebuttal): the 7th speech, and the neg's last. The neg (the team against the plan) picks the arguments it will try to win on.

A

  • Add-on: a new advantage (a reason the plan is good) that the aff reads in its second speech, the 2AC. Many judges allow it because the 2AC is a constructive (a speech for building arguments). See Advantages.
  • Advantage: a reason the plan is good. It is part of the aff's case (the plan and the arguments for it). See Advantages.
  • Affirmative (aff): the team that supports the resolution (the season's topic sentence) and defends a plan. See The affirmative case.
  • Agent counterplan: a counterplan (a different plan the neg offers instead) where a different actor does the plan. See Common counterplans.
  • Alternate cause: another cause of the harm that the plan does not fix. So the plan solves less. See Solvency.
  • Alternative (alt): what a kritik (an argument against the ideas behind the aff) offers instead of the aff. See The alternative.
  • Analytic: an argument made by reasoning alone, without a card (a quote from a source).

B

  • Ballot: the form where the judge records the winner, each debater's points, and the reason. See The ballot and the RFD.
  • Block: the 2NC and the 1NR together: two neg speeches in a row, 13 minutes of neg speaking. Also called the negative block.
  • Bright line: a clear test for where a team's rule draws its limit, so both teams know what counts. It is used in topicality debates (does the plan fit the topic?) and theory debates (was a choice fair?). See Interpretation and violation.
  • Brink: the point where a small push causes the impact (the final bad result). A DA (an argument that the plan causes harm) says the status quo (the world now) is near the brink, and the plan pushes past it. See Uniqueness.
  • Burden of proof: the duty to prove a claim. The aff (the team for the plan) carries it for its case.

C

  • Card: one piece of evidence. It has a tag (the debater's one-line claim), a cite (who wrote it, when, and where) and the quoted text. See Evidence and cards.
  • Case: the aff's plan and the arguments for it.
  • Case turn: a neg argument that turns the aff's case into a reason to vote neg. Either the plan makes its own problem worse, or the aff's "good" result is actually bad. See Case turns and case defense.
  • Circumvention: the claim that people will stall, dodge or undo the plan after it passes. It is a solvency argument (an argument about whether the plan works). See Solvency.
  • Cite: who wrote a card (a piece of quoted evidence), when, and where it was published.
  • Clash: direct disagreement between the teams on the same point.
  • Clipping: claiming to read more of a card (a piece of quoted evidence) than was read out loud. It is a serious ethics violation. See Round conduct.
  • Competition: the test of whether a counterplan (the neg's different plan) is a real alternative to the plan. See Competition.
  • Concession: something a team admits, or does not answer. A dropped (unanswered) argument is often treated as conceded.
  • Conditionality (condo): the neg's choice to stop defending a counterplan (its different plan) or a kritik (its argument against the aff's ideas) later in the round. See Conditionality.
  • Constructive: one of the first four speeches, where teams build arguments.
  • Consult counterplan: a counterplan (the neg's different plan) that asks another party first, and does the plan only if they agree. See Example: consult CP.
  • Counter-interpretation (CI): the responding team's own interpretation (its own meaning of a word, or its own rule). It is used in topicality debates (does the plan fit the topic?) and theory debates (was a choice fair?). See Topicality.
  • Counterplan (CP): a policy the neg offers instead of the plan. See Counterplans.
  • Counterplan text: the exact wording of what the counterplan (the neg's different plan) does. See Counterplan text.
  • Critic of argument: a judge who weighs the quality of ideas, not only policy results. See Judge paradigms.
  • Cross-examination (CX): 3 minutes of questions after each constructive (each of the first four speeches). See Cross-examination.

D

  • Defense: an answer that makes an argument smaller or stops it, but does not give a reason to vote for you. For example: "no link" (the plan does not cause that). See Answering a DA.
  • Disadvantage (DA): a neg argument that the plan causes something bad. See Disadvantages.
  • Disclosure: telling the debaters the decision after the round. Some tournaments allow it and some do not. See Giving feedback.
  • Dispositional (dispo): a counterplan (the neg's different plan) that the neg can stop defending only under set conditions. Often the condition is that the aff makes a perm (says both plans can be done together). See Conditionality.
  • Double turn: making two turns on one DA (an argument that the plan causes harm): a link turn (the plan does the opposite of what the DA says) and an impact turn (the DA's "bad" result is actually good). Together they help the other team.
  • Drop: an argument that got no answer. Most judges treat it as true, if it came with a reason.

E

  • Education: a common standard or voter (a reason one rule is better, or a reason to decide the round): which choice helps debaters learn the most.
  • Effects topicality: the argument that the plan fits the topic only through its effects, not by what it does directly. See Effects and extra topicality.
  • Email chain: the shared email or folder where teams send speech documents (files of the cards they read). See Speech docs.
  • Even if: a weighing phrase that says you win even if the other team is right on a point.
  • Evidence: quoted expert material read in a speech. See Evidence and cards.
  • Existential inherency: a claim about inherency (why the problem is not solved now). It says the problem exists, so something must be stopping a fix. Some judges accept it, and others think it is weak. See Inherency.
  • Extend (ext): carry an argument forward into a later speech, with its reason.
  • Extra topicality: a part of the plan that goes beyond the resolution (the season's topic sentence). See Effects and extra topicality.

F

  • Fairness: a common standard or voter (a reason one rule is better, or a reason to decide the round): whether both teams had an equal chance.
  • Fiat: the idea that we imagine the plan is passed, so the debate is about whether it should be, not whether it would be. See Fiat.
  • Floating PIK: an alternative (what a kritik offers instead of the aff) that, the aff says, could include the plan. Then the neg gets the aff's benefits too. Teams debate whether it is fair. See The alternative.
  • Flow: the notes of the round, in columns, one per speech. See How to flow.
  • Framework (FW): the debate about how the judge should decide the round. See Framework.

G

  • Games player: a judge who sees debate as a game with fair rules. See Judge paradigms.
  • Ground: the arguments a team can fairly make. It is a standard (a reason one rule is better) in topicality and theory debates.

H

  • Harms: the problem in the status quo (the world now) that the aff wants to fix. It is a stock issue (one of the aff's basic jobs). See Harms.
  • Hypothesis tester: a judge who treats the resolution (the season's topic sentence) like a claim to be tested. See Judge paradigms.

I

  • Impact: the result at the end of an argument, and why it matters. See Impacts.
  • Impact calculus: comparing impacts (the final results of arguments) by size, chance, timing and more. See Impact calculus.
  • Impact turn: an argument that the other team's "bad" result is actually good.
  • Inherency: why the problem is not being solved now. It is a stock issue (one of the aff's basic jobs). See Inherency.
  • Internal link (IL): the middle step between a link (how the plan starts a chain of events) and an impact (the final result). See Internal links.
  • International fiat: imagining that another country or a world body acts, the way fiat imagines the plan passes. Teams debate whether the neg may do this. See Common theory arguments.
  • Interpretation: a team's meaning of a word in the resolution (the season's topic sentence). See Interpretation and violation.
  • Intrinsic perm: a perm (the aff's test that two options can be done together) that adds an action that is in neither the plan nor the counterplan. Many judges see it as unfair. See Permutations.

K

  • K v K: a round where a kritikal aff (an aff that criticizes ideas instead of defending a usual plan) meets a kritik from the neg. Teams debate whether perms (doing both together) apply in a debate about methods. See Kritikal affirmatives.
  • Kritik (K): an argument that questions the ideas or assumptions behind the aff. See Kritiks.
  • Kritikal affirmative (K aff): an aff that does not defend a usual plan, or that criticizes ideas. See Kritikal affirmatives.

L

  • Lay judge: a judge with little or no debate background, often a parent. See Judging your first round.
  • Limits: a topicality standard (a reason one meaning of the topic is better). It is about keeping the number of possible aff cases manageable. See Standards.
  • Link (L): how the plan starts the chain toward an impact (the final result). See Links.
  • Link turn (L/T): an argument that the plan does the opposite of what the other team's link says.

M

  • Magnitude: how big an impact (a final result) is.
  • Marked copy: the speech doc (the file of cards) with marks that show where a debater stopped reading each card. See Speech docs.
  • Multiple actor fiat: imagining that several separate actors all act at once, often in a counterplan (the neg's different plan). Its fairness is debated. See Common theory arguments.
  • Mutual exclusivity: the plan and the counterplan (the neg's different plan) cannot both happen. It is one test of competition (whether the counterplan is a real alternative).

N

  • Negative (neg): the team that opposes the resolution (the season's topic sentence) or the plan.
  • Net benefit: a reason the counterplan (the neg's different plan) alone is better than doing both. It is often a DA (an argument that the plan causes harm). See Net benefits.
  • New in the 2: a new argument made in the last rebuttals (the final speeches of the round). Most judges do not count it.
  • No link: an answer that the plan does not cause what an argument says. See Links and link turns.
  • Non-unique (N/U): the bad thing will happen anyway, so the plan does not cause it.

O

  • Object fiat: the neg imagining that the target of the plan (for example, another country) acts. Teams debate whether it is fair. See Common theory arguments.
  • Off-case: neg arguments that start on their own sheets (pages of notes): T (topicality), DA (disadvantage), CP (counterplan) and K (kritik).
  • Offense: an argument that gives the judge a reason to vote for your side. Examples are a turn (flipping the other team's point) or your own advantage. See Answering a DA.
  • On-case: neg arguments that answer the aff case directly.
  • Oral critique: spoken feedback from the judge after the round. See Giving feedback.
  • Overview: a short summary at the top of a sheet (a page of notes for one argument) that frames the argument.

P

  • Paradigm: a judge's way of deciding rounds, often posted online. See Judge paradigms.
  • Perm do both: the most common permutation (the aff's test that two options can be done together): do the whole plan and the whole counterplan together. See Permutations.
  • Permutation (perm): the aff's test that it can do the plan and the counterplan (the neg's different plan), or the plan and the alt (what a kritik offers instead), together. See Permutations.
  • PIC (plan-inclusive counterplan): a counterplan (the neg's different plan) that does most of the plan but leaves out one part. See Example: PICs.
  • PIK (plan-inclusive kritik): a kritik (an argument against the ideas behind the aff) whose alternative does the plan, but rejects one word, idea or part of how the aff justified it. Teams debate whether it is fair. See PICs and word PICs.
  • Plan: the aff's one-sentence statement of what it will do.
  • Policymaker: a judge who weighs costs and benefits like a lawmaker. See Judge paradigms.
  • Politics DA: a DA (an argument that the plan causes harm) saying the plan uses up a leader's support, so another policy fails. See Example: politics DA.
  • Precision: a topicality standard (a reason one meaning of the topic is better): the meaning that experts in the field use.
  • Predictability: a topicality standard (a reason one meaning of the topic is better): a meaning both teams could expect before the round.
  • Prep time: time each team can use to plan its speeches. See Prep time.
  • Presumption: which team wins if nothing else decides the round. See Presumption.
  • Probability: how likely an impact (a final result) is.
  • Process counterplan: a counterplan (the neg's different plan) that does the plan through a different process, for example after a review or a vote. Its competition (whether it is a real alternative) is often debated. See Common counterplan shapes.

R

  • Reasonability: a judge standard where the aff wins topicality (the debate about whether the plan fits the topic) if it is reasonably topical. See Voters.
  • Rebuttal: one of the last four speeches, used to extend (carry arguments forward) and weigh.
  • Reject the argument / reject the team: two possible results of winning a theory argument (an argument that a choice was unfair). The judge can ignore the unfair argument, or vote against the team that made it. See Reasons to reject.
  • Resolution: the season's topic sentence.
  • Reverse voting issue (RVI): a claim that a team should win because the other side's theory or topicality argument was wrong. It is usually made by the aff. Many judges do not vote on RVIs. See Theory.
  • Reversibility: whether an impact (a final result) can be undone.
  • RFD (reason for decision): the judge's explanation of the result. See The ballot and the RFD.
  • Roadmap: the order of sheets (pages of notes, one per argument) a speaker will cover, said before the speech.
  • Role of the ballot (ROB): a claim about what the judge's vote stands for. See Role of the ballot.

S

  • Scope: how widely an impact (a final result) spreads.
  • Severance perm: a perm (the aff's test that two options can be done together) that drops part of the plan. Many judges see it as unfair. See Permutations.
  • Sheet: one page or tab of the flow, for one big argument.
  • Significance: how big the harms (the problem) or the solvency (how much the plan fixes) are. It is a stock issue (one of the aff's basic jobs). See Significance.
  • Signpost: a word that tells the judge where you are ("On the DA...").
  • Solvency: whether the plan solves the problem. It is a stock issue (one of the aff's basic jobs). See Solvency.
  • Solvency advocate: a card (a piece of quoted evidence) that says the exact plan or counterplan would work. Some judges expect one. See Solvency deficits.
  • Solvency deficit: a part of the aff's case that the counterplan (the neg's different plan) cannot solve. See Solvency deficits.
  • Speaker points: a score for each debater's speaking and arguing. See Speaker points.
  • Speech document (speech doc): the file of cards a debater will read. See Speech docs.
  • Spreading: speaking very fast to fit in more arguments.
  • SSD (switch-side debate): the idea that debaters argue both sides over a tournament. It is a common standard (reason) in framework debates (debates about how the judge should decide). See Framework against a K aff.
  • Standards: reasons one interpretation (a meaning of a word, or a rule) is better, in topicality or theory debates. See Standards.
  • States counterplan: a counterplan (the neg's different plan) where the 50 states do the plan instead of the federal government. See Example: states CP.
  • Status quo (SQ): the world now, without the plan.
  • Stealing prep: preparing when your team is not using prep time (its set planning time), and it is not the other team's speech or cross-ex. See Prep time.
  • Stock issues: the basic jobs of the aff: harms, inherency, solvency, significance, topicality. See Stock issues.
  • Structural violence: harm built into how a society works (for example, poverty or exclusion), as opposed to a single event. See Extinction vs structural violence.

T

  • Tab room: the tournament office that runs pairings and collects ballots. Ask them when the rules are unclear. See Judging your first round.
  • Tabula rasa: "blank slate"; a judge who lets the debaters set the rules. See Judge paradigms.
  • Tag: the one-line claim a debater writes above a card (a piece of quoted evidence).
  • Terminal impact: the final harm at the end of a chain, such as loss of life, not a middle step such as a recession. See Impacts.
  • Theory: an argument about the rules of debate, such as whether a choice was fair. See Theory.
  • Thumper: an event already in the status quo (the world now) that should trigger a DA's link (the step where the plan causes the harm). So the harm comes anyway, and the DA is non-unique. See Uniqueness.
  • Timeframe: how soon an impact (a final result) happens.
  • Topicality (T): the argument that the plan does not fit the resolution (the season's topic sentence). See Topicality.
  • Turn: an argument that flips the other team's point into a reason for your side.
  • Turns case: an argument that one impact makes the other team's impact worse. See Turns case.
  • T-USFG: a common name for framework (an argument about how the judge should decide) against an aff that does not defend a plan by the government. "USFG" means the United States federal government. Some circuits use other names. See Framework against a K aff.
  • TVA (topical version of the aff): a neg claim that the aff could read a plan within the topic and still make its main points. See Framework against a K aff.

U

  • Uniqueness (U): whether the bad result of a DA (an argument that the plan causes harm) is not happening now. See Uniqueness.

V

  • Violation: how the plan breaks a topicality interpretation (the neg's meaning of a word in the topic). See Interpretation and violation.
  • Voter: a reason an argument should decide the round. See Voters.

W

  • Warrant: the reason a claim is true; the "because."
  • We meet: the aff's answer that the plan meets the neg's interpretation (its meaning of a word in the topic).
