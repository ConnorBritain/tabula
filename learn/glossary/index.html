Policy debate glossary

Plain version. Debate has a lot of special words. This page lists them in A to Z order, with one plain sentence each. When a term has its own page, the term links to it. If you are new, start with Debate 101, and come back here when you hear a word you do not know.

Numbers

  • 1AC (first affirmative constructive): the first speech; the aff reads its plan and case. See Speeches and times.
  • 1AR (first affirmative rebuttal): the aff's 5-minute answer to the whole negative block.
  • 1NC (first negative constructive): the neg's first speech, where it starts all its arguments.
  • 1NR (first negative rebuttal): the second half of the negative block.
  • 2AC (second affirmative constructive): the aff's answers to everything in the 1NC.
  • 2AR (second affirmative rebuttal): the last speech of the round.
  • 2NC (second negative constructive): the first half of the negative block.
  • 2NR (second negative rebuttal): the neg's last speech, where it picks what to win on.

A

  • Add-on: a new advantage the aff reads in the 2AC. Many judges allow it because the 2AC is a constructive speech. See Advantages.
  • Advantage: a reason the plan is good; part of the aff case. See Advantages.
  • Affirmative (aff): the team that supports the resolution and defends a plan. See The affirmative case.
  • Agent counterplan: a counterplan where a different actor does the plan. See Common counterplans.
  • Alternate cause: another cause of the harm that the plan does not fix, so the plan solves less. See Solvency.
  • Alternative (alt): what a kritik offers instead of the aff. See The alternative.
  • Analytic: an argument made without a card, using reasoning alone.

B

  • Ballot: the form where the judge records the winner, points and reason. See The ballot and the RFD.
  • Block: the 2NC and 1NR together, 13 minutes of neg speaking in a row.
  • Bright line: a clear test for where a topicality or theory interpretation draws its limit, so both teams know what counts. See Interpretation and violation.
  • Brink: the point where a small push causes the impact; a DA says the status quo is near it and the plan pushes past it. See Uniqueness.
  • Burden of proof: the duty to prove a claim; the aff carries it for its case.

C

  • Card: one piece of evidence: a tag, a cite and a quoted text. See Evidence and cards.
  • Case: the aff's plan and the arguments for it.
  • Case turn: a neg argument that turns the aff's case into a reason to vote neg: the plan makes its own problem worse, or the aff's "good" result is actually bad. See Case turns and case defense.
  • Circumvention: the claim that people will stall, dodge or undo the plan after it passes; a solvency argument. See Solvency.
  • Cite: who wrote a card, when, and where it was published.
  • Clash: direct disagreement between the teams on the same point.
  • Clipping: claiming to read more of a card than was read out loud; a serious ethics violation. See Round conduct.
  • Competition: the test of whether a counterplan is a real alternative to the plan. See Competition.
  • Concession: something a team admits, or does not answer. A dropped argument is often treated as conceded.
  • Conditionality (condo): the neg's choice to drop a counterplan or kritik later in the round. See Conditionality.
  • Constructive: one of the first four speeches, where teams build arguments.
  • Consult counterplan: a counterplan that asks another party first, and does the plan only if they agree. See Example: consult CP.
  • Counter-interpretation (CI): the responding team's own interpretation, in a topicality or theory debate. See Topicality.
  • Counterplan (CP): a neg policy that is offered instead of the plan. See Counterplans.
  • Counterplan text: the exact wording of what the counterplan does. See Counterplan text.
  • Critic of argument: a judge who weighs the quality of ideas, not only policy results. See Judge paradigms.
  • Cross-examination (CX): 3 minutes of questions after each constructive. See Cross-examination.

D

  • Defense: an answer that makes an argument smaller or stops it (for example, no link), but does not give a reason to vote for you. See Answering a DA.
  • Disadvantage (DA): a neg argument that the plan causes something bad. See Disadvantages.
  • Disclosure: telling the debaters the decision after the round. Some tournaments allow it and some do not. See Giving feedback.
  • Dispositional (dispo): a counterplan the neg can drop only under set conditions, often only if the aff makes a perm. See Conditionality.
  • Double turn: making both a link turn and an impact turn on one DA, which helps the other team.
  • Drop: an argument that got no answer. Most judges treat it as true, if it came with a reason.

E

  • Education: a common standard or voter: which choice helps debaters learn the most.
  • Effects topicality: the argument that the plan becomes topical only through its effects, not by what it does directly. See Effects and extra topicality.
  • Email chain: the shared email or folder where teams send speech documents. See Speech docs.
  • Even if: a weighing phrase that says you win even if the other team is right on a point.
  • Evidence: quoted expert material read in a speech. See Evidence and cards.
  • Existential inherency: the claim that the problem exists, so something must be stopping a fix; some judges accept it and others think it is weak. See Inherency.
  • Extend (ext): carry an argument forward into a later speech, with its reason.
  • Extra topicality: a part of the plan that goes beyond the resolution. See Effects and extra topicality.

F

  • Fairness: a common standard or voter: whether both teams had an equal chance.
  • Fiat: the idea that we imagine the plan is passed, so the debate is about whether it should be, not whether it would be. See Fiat.
  • Floating PIK: a kritik alternative that, the aff says, could include the plan, so the neg gets the aff's benefits too; teams debate whether it is fair. See The alternative.
  • Flow: the notes of the round, in columns, one per speech. See How to flow.
  • Framework (FW): the debate about how the judge should decide the round. See Framework.

G

  • Games player: a judge who sees debate as a game with fair rules. See Judge paradigms.
  • Ground: the arguments a team can fairly make. A standard in topicality and theory.

H

  • Harms: the problem in the status quo that the aff wants to fix; a stock issue. See Harms.
  • Hypothesis tester: a judge who treats the resolution like a claim to be tested. See Judge paradigms.

I

  • Impact: the result at the end of an argument, and why it matters. See Impacts.
  • Impact calculus: comparing impacts by size, chance, timing and more. See Impact calculus.
  • Impact turn: an argument that the other team's "bad" result is actually good.
  • Inherency: why the problem is not being solved now; a stock issue. See Inherency.
  • Internal link (IL): the step between a link and an impact. See Internal links.
  • International fiat: imagining that another country or a world body acts. Teams debate whether the neg may do this. See Common theory arguments.
  • Interpretation: a team's meaning of a word in the resolution. See Interpretation and violation.
  • Intrinsic perm: a perm that adds an action that is in neither the plan nor the counterplan; many judges see it as unfair. See Permutations.

K

  • K v K: a round where a kritikal aff meets a kritik from the neg; teams debate whether perms apply in a debate about methods. See Kritikal affirmatives.
  • Kritik (K): an argument that questions the ideas or assumptions behind the aff. See Kritiks.
  • Kritikal affirmative (K aff): an aff that does not defend a usual plan, or that criticizes ideas. See Kritikal affirmatives.

L

  • Lay judge: a judge with little or no debate background, often a parent. See Judging your first round.
  • Limits: a topicality standard about keeping the number of possible affs manageable. See Standards.
  • Link (L): how the plan starts the chain toward an impact. See Links.
  • Link turn (L/T): an argument that the plan does the opposite of what the link says.

M

  • Magnitude: how big an impact is.
  • Marked copy: the speech doc with marks that show where a debater stopped reading each card. See Speech docs.
  • Multiple actor fiat: imagining that several separate actors all act at once, often in a counterplan. Its fairness is debated. See Common theory arguments.
  • Mutual exclusivity: the plan and counterplan cannot both happen. One test of competition.

N

  • Negative (neg): the team that opposes the resolution or the plan.
  • Net benefit: a reason the counterplan alone is better than doing both; often a DA. See Net benefits.
  • New in the 2: a new argument made in the last rebuttals; most judges do not count it.
  • No link: an answer that the plan does not cause what an argument says. See Links and link turns.
  • Non-unique (N/U): the bad thing will happen anyway, so the plan does not cause it.

O

  • Object fiat: the neg imagining that the target of the plan (for example, another country) acts; teams debate whether it is fair. See Common theory arguments.
  • Off-case: neg arguments that start on their own sheets (T, DA, CP, K).
  • Offense: an argument that gives the judge a reason to vote for your side, such as a turn or your own advantage. See Answering a DA.
  • On-case: neg arguments that answer the aff case directly.
  • Oral critique: spoken feedback from the judge after the round. See Giving feedback.
  • Overview: a short summary at the top of a sheet that frames the argument.

P

  • Paradigm: a judge's way of deciding rounds, often posted online. See Judge paradigms.
  • Perm do both: the most common permutation: do the whole plan and the whole counterplan together. See Permutations.
  • Permutation (perm): the aff's test that it can do the plan and the counterplan, or the alt, together. See Permutations.
  • PIC (plan-inclusive counterplan): a counterplan that does most of the plan but leaves out one part. See Example: PICs.
  • PIK (plan-inclusive kritik): a kritik whose alternative does the plan but rejects one word, idea or part of how the aff justified it. Teams debate whether it is fair. See PICs and word PICs.
  • Plan: the aff's one-sentence statement of what it will do.
  • Policymaker: a judge who weighs costs and benefits like a lawmaker. See Judge paradigms.
  • Politics DA: a DA saying the plan uses up a leader's support, so another policy fails. See Example: politics DA.
  • Precision: a topicality standard: the meaning that experts in the field use.
  • Predictability: a topicality standard: a meaning both teams could expect before the round.
  • Prep time: time each team can use to plan its speeches. See Prep time.
  • Presumption: which team wins if nothing else decides the round. See Presumption.
  • Probability: how likely an impact is.
  • Process counterplan: a counterplan that does the plan through a different process (for example, after a review or a vote). Its competition is often debated. See Common counterplan shapes.

R

  • Reasonability: a judge standard where the aff wins topicality if it is reasonably topical. See Voters.
  • Rebuttal: one of the last four speeches, used to extend and weigh.
  • Reject the argument / reject the team: two possible results of winning a theory argument. See Reasons to reject.
  • Resolution: the season's topic sentence.
  • Reverse voting issue (RVI): a claim that a team should win because the other side's theory or topicality argument was wrong. It is usually made by the aff. Many judges do not vote on RVIs. See Theory.
  • Reversibility: whether an impact can be undone.
  • RFD (reason for decision): the judge's explanation of the result. See The ballot and the RFD.
  • Roadmap: the order of sheets a speaker will cover, said before the speech.
  • Role of the ballot (ROB): a claim about what the judge's vote stands for. See Role of the ballot.

S

  • Scope: how widely an impact spreads.
  • Severance perm: a perm that drops part of the plan; many judges see it as unfair. See Permutations.
  • Sheet: one page or tab of the flow, for one big argument.
  • Significance: how big the harms or the solvency are; a stock issue. See Significance.
  • Signpost: a word that tells the judge where you are ("On the DA...").
  • Solvency: whether the plan solves the problem; a stock issue. See Solvency.
  • Solvency advocate: a card that says the exact plan or counterplan would work. Some judges expect one. See Solvency deficits.
  • Solvency deficit: a part of the case the counterplan cannot solve. See Solvency deficits.
  • Speaker points: a score for each debater's speaking and arguing. See Speaker points.
  • Speech document (speech doc): the file of cards a debater will read. See Speech docs.
  • Spreading: speaking very fast to fit in more arguments.
  • SSD (switch-side debate): the idea that debaters argue both sides over a tournament; a common standard in framework debates. See Framework against a K aff.
  • Standards: reasons one interpretation is better, in topicality or theory. See Standards.
  • States counterplan: a counterplan where the 50 states do the plan instead of the federal government. See Example: states CP.
  • Status quo (SQ): the world now, without the plan.
  • Stealing prep: preparing when your team is not using prep time and it is not the other team's speech or cross-ex. See Prep time.
  • Stock issues: the basic jobs of the aff: harms, inherency, solvency, significance, topicality. See Stock issues.
  • Structural violence: harm built into how a society works (for example, poverty or exclusion), as opposed to a single event. See Extinction vs structural violence.

T

  • Tab room: the tournament office that runs pairings and collects ballots. Ask them when the rules are unclear. See Judging your first round.
  • Tabula rasa: "blank slate"; a judge who lets the debaters set the rules. See Judge paradigms.
  • Tag: the one-line claim a debater writes above a card.
  • Terminal impact: the final harm at the end of a chain, such as loss of life, not a middle step such as a recession. See Impacts.
  • Theory: an argument about the rules of debate, such as whether a choice was fair. See Theory.
  • Thumper: an event already in the status quo that should trigger the DA's link, so the DA is non-unique. See Uniqueness.
  • Timeframe: how soon an impact happens.
  • Topicality (T): the argument that the plan does not fit the resolution. See Topicality.
  • Turn: an argument that flips the other team's point into a reason for your side.
  • Turns case: an argument that one impact makes the other team's impact worse. See Turns case.
  • T-USFG: a common name for framework against an aff that does not defend a plan by the government ("USFG" means the United States federal government). Some circuits use other names. See Framework against a K aff.
  • TVA (topical version of the aff): a neg claim that the aff could read a plan within the topic and still make its main points. See Framework against a K aff.

U

  • Uniqueness (U): whether a DA's bad result is not happening now. See Uniqueness.

V

  • Violation: how the plan breaks a topicality interpretation. See Interpretation and violation.
  • Voter: a reason an argument should decide the round. See Voters.

W

  • Warrant: the reason a claim is true; the "because."
  • We meet: the aff's answer that the plan meets the neg's interpretation.
