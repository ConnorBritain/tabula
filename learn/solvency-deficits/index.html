Counterplans · 7 min read · intermediate

Solvency deficits

Plain version. A counterplan is the negative's own plan, offered instead of the affirmative's. Most counterplans try to solve the same problem as the plan. A solvency deficit is the part of the problem the counterplan cannot solve. The affirmative points it out and says why it matters. The judge then weighs that missing part against the net benefit: the harm the counterplan avoids. If the missing part is bigger, the affirmative wins.

Why deficits matter

A counterplan works by taking away the aff's reasons to choose the plan. If it solves the whole case, the aff has no advantage left to weigh. Then even a small net benefit can win the round for the neg.

A solvency deficit puts some of the case back on the aff's side. It is a piece of the aff's advantage that only the plan gets. The bigger that piece, the bigger the net benefit has to be. See Net benefits.

Solvency is how well a policy fixes the problem. The aff must prove its own solvency too. See Solvency.

Common kinds of deficit

KindWhat the aff saysExample (generic)
AbilityThe counterplan's actor lacks the money, power or skill."States cannot fund a program this large."
CoverageIt reaches fewer people or places."Some states will run it poorly; gaps remain."
Unique toolsOnly the plan's actor has a needed tool."Only the federal government can set one rule across states."
DurabilityIt is easier to undo."Fifty separate laws can be repealed one at a time."
SignalOthers react to who acts, not just what is done."Other nations copy federal action, not state action."
Certainty or timingIt may not happen, or happens later."The counterplan waits for another body to agree."

Some deficits interact with fiat: the shared idea that we imagine a policy is passed and carried out. Fiat makes the counterplan's actor act. It does not make the counterplan work well. So "states will not pass it" is usually not a fair deficit, but "states lack the money to run it well" can be. See Fiat.

How to make a deficit (for the aff)

A good deficit has three steps.

  1. Name the gap. Say exactly what the counterplan does not do. Read the counterplan text closely. See Counterplan text.
  2. Prove it. Use evidence or clear reasoning. "States lack money" is stronger with a card.
  3. Impact it. Tie the gap to a part of your advantage. Say what harm keeps happening under the counterplan, and how big it is.

The last step is the one most often skipped. A deficit with no impact gives the judge nothing to weigh. "The counterplan is slower" means little unless you say what happens during the delay.

Then weigh. Compare the deficit with the net benefit. Use size, chance and timing. See Impact calculus.

How to answer a deficit (for the neg)

  • The counterplan solves. Read evidence that the actor can do this part.
  • The deficit is small. Even if there is a gap, little of the advantage depends on it.
  • The net benefit outweighs. Even if the gap is real, the harm the plan causes is bigger.
  • Case defense applies. If the aff's advantage is weak in the first place, the part the counterplan misses is weak too.
  • Add a plank. Some neg teams add a step to the counterplan to close the gap. Whether new planks are allowed after the 1NC is debated. See Counterplan text.

Sufficiency framing. Some neg teams ask the judge to decide whether the counterplan solves enough of the case that the net benefit outweighs the rest. Some judges find this a helpful way to compare. Others say it hides the size of the deficit, and a small gap in a big impact can still matter. Both teams can argue how to weigh.

Solvency advocates. Many judges expect the neg to read evidence that the counterplan works: a card from someone who supports that approach. Without it, the aff may argue the counterplan has no proven solvency at all. How strictly judges apply this differs.

How to judge a deficit

  1. Is the deficit real? Does the counterplan, as written, miss this part of the case?
  2. Is it impacted? Did the aff say what harm continues because of the gap?
  3. How big is it? How much of the advantage does it cover?
  4. Weigh. Compare the deficit with the risk of the net benefit, as the teams argued.

If the aff wins a big deficit and the net benefit is small, vote aff. If the counterplan solves most of the case and the net benefit is real, vote neg.

How to read it: the 1NC solvency card says the states can fund X. The 2AC made two deficits and one answer to the net benefit. The 2NR answered the first deficit with the Analyst card: all states fund X under the counterplan. The 2AR replied that the card is about funding, not quality. The 2NR did not answer the second deficit, so the aff can treat it as true: the counterplan loses the global part of the advantage. The 2AR weighed that dropped deficit against the spending DA. The judge must decide how big the global part of the advantage is, and whether it outweighs the risk of the DA.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: a deficit with no impact. Fix: say what harm keeps happening because of the gap, and how big it is.
  • Aff: a "won't pass" deficit. Fix: fiat passes the counterplan. Argue it would not work well, not that it would not happen.
  • Aff: many thin deficits. Fix: make one or two deficits you can prove and weigh.
  • Neg: ignoring a deficit because the net benefit "outweighs." Fix: still answer it, then weigh. A dropped deficit is the aff's to use.
  • Neg: a text that misses part of the case it could have covered. Fix: write the counterplan to match the aff's advantages.
  • Judges: treating any deficit as fatal, or any as trivial. Fix: weigh its size against the net benefit, as argued.
