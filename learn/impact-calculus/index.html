Impact calculus

9 min read · Beginner

Impact calculus, or weighing, is how debaters help the judge compare what each team is winning.

  • Why it is needed: most rounds end with each team winning something. The judge must decide if the good result of the plan is bigger than the bad result the other team warns about.
  • What to compare: how big each harm is, how likely it is, how soon it happens, and whether it can be undone.
  • Why it matters: the team that explains the comparison best often wins, even if the other team won more arguments.

Words on this page

  • The aff (affirmative) is the team for the plan. The neg (negative) is the team against it.
  • An impact is the end result an argument warns about, or promises. "People lose jobs" is an impact.
  • An advantage is a good result the aff says the plan causes.
  • A disadvantage (DA) is a bad result the neg says the plan causes.
  • The 2NR is the neg's last speech. The 2AR is the aff's last speech, and the last speech of the round. The 1AR is the aff's speech just before the 2NR.

Why it matters

Judges do not want to guess. Say the aff says "the plan saves lives," and the neg says "the plan causes a recession." The judge needs a reason to prefer one. Impact calculus gives that reason. Without it, the judge must do the weighing alone, and either team might be unhappy with the result.

The last two speeches (2NR and 2AR) should spend real time on it.

The main measures

MeasureThe questionExample (generic)
MagnitudeHow big is the harm? How many people, how badly?"A war affects millions; the program cut affects thousands."
ProbabilityHow likely is it to happen?"The cuts are written into law; the war is only a risk."
TimeframeHow soon does it happen?"The cuts start next year; the conflict is decades away."
ReversibilityCan it be undone?"A budget can be fixed later; lives lost cannot."
ScopeWho is affected, and how widely?"The harm falls on the whole country, not one town."

Some debaters also use severity (how deep the harm is for each person) as part of magnitude.

Magnitude

Big impacts, like a large war or a collapse of the economy, can dominate a round. But a big impact with a weak chain of steps may have a small real risk.

Probability

A harm that is very likely can beat a bigger harm that is unlikely. Probability depends on every step of the DA. Those steps are: why the harm is not happening now (uniqueness), how the plan starts it (the link), and the steps in between (internal links). Each weak step lowers the chance. See Disadvantages.

Timeframe

A harm that comes first can matter more. This is most true if it would stop the other harm from ever happening, or make it worse.

Reversibility

Some harms can be fixed later. Others cannot. A harm that cannot be undone often gets extra weight.

Turns case

Turns case means your impact makes the other team's impact worse, or stops their plan from working. For example: "The recession from the DA would cut the very funding the plan needs. So the DA turns the case." If true, this is very strong. You win your impact and shrink theirs. See Turns case.

The other team answers with "case turns the DA" or "no turn." They show the connection goes the other way, or that there is none.

Big and unlikely vs small and likely

This is the most common weighing debate. There is no single right answer. It depends on what the teams argue.

  • The case for the big impact: even a small chance of a huge harm, like a large war, can be worth more than a sure small harm. Multiply size by chance, and the big impact can still be larger.
  • The case for the likely impact: long chains of "could lead to" make big impacts far less likely than they sound. A harm we can see happening now is more certain. Some judges count very small risks as almost zero.

Judges differ here. Many say in their paradigm (a judge's written note on how they decide) which way they lean. A good debater makes the argument either way, with reasons. They do not just say "extinction (the end of all humans) outweighs."

How to judge weighing

  1. Find the weighing in the last speeches. List each team's comparisons: magnitude, probability, timeframe, turns case.
  2. Check that each impact was won first. An impact a team did not win has nothing to weigh.
  3. Resolve the turns. Did a "turns case" argument survive the answers to it? If not, set it aside.
  4. Compare the comparisons. One team may say why its measure matters more. For example: "probability first, because tiny risks are guesses." If the other team did not answer, use that.
  5. Only then add your own judgment, if the teams left a gap. Say so in your RFD (reason for decision).

How to do impact calculus in a speech

  1. Name both impacts. "Our advantage is X. Their DA is Y."
  2. Pick two or three measures where you win. You do not need all of them.
  3. Compare directly. "Even if their impact is bigger, ours is far more likely, because their link card only says 'might.'" (A card is a quote from a source, read as evidence.)
  4. Use "even if." "Even if you think the DA happens, the case outweighs, because it comes first and cannot be undone."
  5. Tell the judge what to write. "Vote aff because the case is more likely and comes first."

Example in flow shorthand

The weighing at the end of one round. The aff's advantage helps a public health program. The neg's DA says the plan's cost causes a recession, and a recession raises the risk of conflict.

Key: "->" means "leads to". ">" means "matters more than". Mag = magnitude, Prob = probability, TF = timeframe, Ext = extend (repeat an earlier point). A set-aside is money kept apart for one use. "Expert 22" is an invented source.

1AR (aff):
  No turn: plan is funded by a
    set-aside; recession can't cut
    it (Expert 22)
2NR (neg):
  Mag: recession -> conflict,
    millions at risk
  TF: recession hits in 1 yr
  DA turns case: recession cuts
    health budget -> case fails
  Even if risk is small, size wins
2AR (aff):
  Prob: 4-step chain; each step
    "could" -> tiny risk
  TF: case saves lives now, in yr 1
  Ext set-aside: recession can't
    cut it. 2NR never answered it
  Sure, real lives > speculative war

How to read it: the 1AR answers the turn with a set-aside card. The 2NR repeats the turn but never answers the set-aside card. The neg weighs magnitude, timeframe and turns case. The aff weighs probability and timeframe, and extends the set-aside card. If the judge believes the set-aside card, the turn is gone. Then the round comes down to big-but-unlikely against sure-but-smaller.

Common mistakes

  • No weighing at all. Fix: in the last speech, compare your impact to theirs out loud.
  • Saying "extinction outweighs" and stopping. Fix: explain the risk, not just the size.
  • Using every measure. Fix: pick the two or three where you really win, and go deep.
  • Ignoring the other team's weighing. Fix: answer it with "even if" arguments.
  • Weighing impacts you have not won. Fix: first win that your impact is real, then weigh it.
  • Judges: weighing for the debaters when they did it themselves. Fix: use the comparisons that were argued, before your own.

How big?

The neg says: "The disadvantage could affect millions of people. The aff's plan helps a few thousand."

Which question is the neg using to compare?

  • How big each harm is
  • How likely each harm is
  • How soon each harm happens
  • Who spoke last

Answer: How big each harm is

Why: The neg compares the size of the two impacts: millions against thousands. Debaters call this magnitude.

How likely?

The aff says: "Their harm needs four steps, and each step only 'could' happen. Our harm is happening right now."

Which question is the aff using to compare?

  • How big each harm is
  • How likely each harm is
  • How soon each harm happens
  • Which team has more cards
Show the answer

Answer: How likely each harm is

Why: The aff says its harm is more certain, and the neg's harm is a long chain of "could" steps. Debaters call this probability.

How soon?

The aff says: "Our program saves lives this year. Their harm is decades away."

Which question is the aff using to compare?

  • How big each harm is
  • How likely each harm is
  • How soon each harm happens
  • Whether the plan fits the topic
Show the answer

Answer: How soon each harm happens

Why: The aff compares when each harm happens: now against decades away. Debaters call this timeframe.

Who weighed better?

In the last speeches, the neg says: "Our harm is a war. War is bad." The aff says: "Even if a war is bigger, their harm needs four weak steps, and our harm is happening now. So our harm is more likely and comes first." The neg never answers this.

Which team weighed better, and why?

  • The aff, because it compared the two harms directly, and the neg did not answer
  • The neg, because a war is always the biggest harm
  • Neither, because both teams have harms
  • The neg, because it spoke first
Show the answer

Answer: The aff, because it compared the two harms directly, and the neg did not answer

Why: The neg only said its harm is bad. The aff compared the two harms on how likely and how soon they are, and the neg did not answer that. When one team compares and the other does not, the judge usually uses the comparison that was made.
