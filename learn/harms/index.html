Harms

7 min read · Beginner

Harms are the bad things happening now that the affirmative wants to fix. The affirmative must show who is hurt, how, and how badly. Harms are one of the stock issues, the basic jobs of an affirmative case. If there is no real harm, the plan fixes nothing that matters. The negative can attack harms by showing the problem is smaller, or less bad, than the affirmative says.

What harms are

Every affirmative case starts with a problem. The harm is that problem: something bad in the status quo (the world now, without the plan). The aff proves it with evidence, usually in the first part of each advantage (a reason the plan is good).

A good harm answers three questions:

QuestionExample (generic)
Who is hurt?"Families in rural areas."
How are they hurt?"They cannot reach a clinic, so illness goes untreated."
How badly, and how many?"One million people each year (Analyst 21)."

"(Analyst 21)" names a card: a short quote from a source, with the author and year. The cards on this page are invented.

Harms are one of the five stock issues: harms, inherency, solvency, significance and topicality. See Stock issues for the full checklist.

Kinds of harms

Quantitative harms

A quantitative harm is one you can count. It uses numbers: people hurt, money lost, deaths per year. Numbers make a harm easy to compare. "One million people" is easy to weigh against a disadvantage (DA, a neg argument that the plan causes something bad) that hurts ten thousand.

Qualitative harms

A qualitative harm is about the kind of harm, not the count. Loss of a right, unfair treatment, or loss of dignity are examples. A qualitative harm can matter even if few people are hurt. Many teams argue that some harms, such as a loss of rights, matter in a way numbers do not show.

Many cases use both. "One million people lack care (quantitative), and lack of care is a denial of a basic need (qualitative)."

Harms and impacts

Harms and impacts overlap, and debaters often use the words loosely. One common way to split them:

  • The harm is the problem now: "People cannot reach clinics."
  • The impact is why that problem matters in the end: "Untreated illness leads to deaths."

In your notes (the flow), a stock issues debate often puts the harm and impact together under "H." In a policy-style advantage, the impact is the last step of a chain. See Advantages for how that chain is built.

Harms and the other stock issues

Harms do not stand alone. They work with the other stock issues:

  • Inherency says the harm will last without the plan. A harm that is already being fixed does not need a plan. See Inherency.
  • Solvency says the plan reduces the harm. A huge harm the plan cannot touch is not a reason to vote aff. See Solvency.
  • Significance says the harm is big enough to matter. Many judges treat significance as part of harms. See Significance.

So a harm is only as useful as the plan's effect on it. The aff should always tie the harm to what the plan does.

How to answer harms (for the neg)

Two words help here. Defense is a reason the other side's argument is weaker. Offense is a reason to vote for your side.

Most neg arguments against harms are case defense: arguments on the aff's case that make its advantage smaller. The common ones:

  • Harms are exaggerated: the numbers are too high, old, or from a weak source. "Their card is from ten years ago; the number has dropped."
  • Harms are not caused as claimed: the bad outcome comes from something else, not the problem the aff names.
  • Impact defense: even if the harm is real, the final result will not happen. "Most people recover without care."
  • Harms are not bad: this one is offense, not defense. Rarely, the neg argues the "harm" is actually good. This is an impact turn. It is risky, and it can sound harsh to a judge if the harm is human suffering. See Case turns and case defense.

Neg teams often pair harm defense with a DA. A smaller harm is easier for a DA to outweigh.

How to defend harms (for the aff)

  • Read strong, recent evidence. A clear number from a good source is hard to answer.
  • Explain the harm in human terms. Say who is hurt and how, not only how many.
  • Answer each attack directly. If the neg says the number is old, read a newer card or explain why the trend has not changed.
  • Use both kinds of harm. If the neg wins that the number is smaller, a qualitative harm can still carry weight.
  • Extend the harm into the last speech. To extend is to carry an argument forward into later speeches. An advantage with no harm in the 2AR (the aff's last speech) is hard to vote for.

How to judge harms

  1. Ask what harm is left. The neg may shrink a harm without erasing it. Weigh the part that is left.
  2. Check the evidence fights. If both teams read cards about the size of the harm, prefer the team that explained why its card is better: newer, more specific, or from a better source.
  3. Separate harms from impacts. The neg may win that the impact is unlikely while the harm itself is real. That still leaves some harm for the aff.
  4. Follow the paradigm the teams argue. A stock issues judge asks whether the aff proved a harm at all. A policymaker judge (who weighs costs against benefits) asks how big the harm is compared to the neg's arguments. See Judge paradigms.

Harms rarely go to zero. A neg usually wins a harms debate by making the harm small enough that a DA outweighs it. It rarely wins by proving there is no problem at all.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: a harm with no number and no quality. Fix: say who is hurt, how, and how many.
  • Aff: a harm the plan does not touch. Fix: make sure the solvency evidence is about the same harm.
  • Aff: dropping the harm in rebuttals. Fix: extend the harm and the impact in the 1AR and 2AR, with the card names.
  • Neg: saying "no harm" with no reason. Fix: give a reason, such as an old card or a weak source, and read a card if you have one.
  • Neg: impact turning human suffering. Fix: this is a strategic choice; if you make it, explain it with care and evidence, and know that many judges dislike it.
  • Judges: treating a smaller harm as no harm. Fix: weigh the part of the harm that the aff still wins.
