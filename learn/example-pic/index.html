Plan-inclusive counterplans (PICs) and the word PIC

8 min read · Advanced

A plan-inclusive counterplan, or PIC, is a counterplan (the negative's own plan) that does almost all of the affirmative's plan but leaves out one part.

  • The neg's case: That one part causes a harm, so the plan without it is better.
  • Word PICs: A special kind. It does the whole plan but drops or changes one word the negative says is harmful.
  • Fairness: PICs are hard for the affirmative to answer, so teams often argue about whether they are fair. Judges decide that question as it is argued in the round.

The shape

A PIC is a counterplan, so it has the usual four parts. See Counterplans.

PartWhat it usually looks like (generic)
Text"Do the plan, but not in area Y." Or: "Do the plan, but without part Z."
SolvencyThe counterplan does almost everything the plan does, so it solves almost all of the case.
CompetitionA net benefit tied only to the part the counterplan leaves out.
Net benefitA harm that the left-out part causes, and the rest of the plan does not.

The key idea is simple. The PIC agrees with most of the plan. It fights over one piece. If the neg can prove that the piece does harm, the plan without it looks better than the plan with it.

Two common shapes

The plan-minus PIC

This PIC removes one part of the plan. The part can be a place, a group of people, a funding source, a step in the plan, or one of several actions. For example, the plan funds a program everywhere, and the PIC funds it everywhere except one area.

The net benefit is a harm that comes only from that part. In the example, the neg might argue that the plan replaces a local program in area Y that already works well.

The word PIC

A word PIC does the whole plan but drops or replaces one word in the plan text, or in the aff's speeches. The neg argues that the word itself does harm. For example, the word may describe a group of people in a way the neg says is demeaning, or carry a frame the neg says leads to bad results.

The net benefit is usually a kritik-style argument: an argument that the aff's language or way of thinking causes harm. See Kritiks. Some teams run the same idea as a kritik with an alternative that rejects the word. That version is often called a PIK (plan-inclusive kritik). The debate is similar either way.

How a PIC competes

A counterplan must compete: the judge must have a reason to choose it instead of the plan, not with it. Most counterplans compete through a net benefit: doing the counterplan alone is better than doing both. See Competition.

PICs are hard to permute. A permutation (perm) is the aff's test: "Do both the plan and the counterplan." See Permutations. With a PIC, "do both" means the whole plan, because the counterplan is already part of the plan. So the perm puts back the left-out part, and the left-out part is the source of the net benefit. If the neg wins that the part causes harm, the perm links to the net benefit almost by definition.

Word PICs raise a further question. The plan and the counterplan do the same thing. Only the words differ. Functional competition asks whether the two policies differ in what they do. Textual competition asks whether the texts differ in their words. A word PIC differs in text, but often not clearly in function. Some judges want functional competition and doubt word PICs. Other judges accept them, especially when the net benefit is about the effects of language. Teams should argue which standard applies.

Common aff answers

The 2AC usually mixes several answers.

  • Perm do both. As above, this is hard, but the aff can still argue that the net benefit is tiny, so the perm's risk is small.
  • Perm do the counterplan. The aff argues that the plan text already allows the counterplan's version. This works best when the plan text is broad. Many judges doubt it when the plan clearly includes the left-out part.
  • Solvency deficit. The left-out part is key. Area Y may have the worst harm, or part Z may be the step the rest needs. For a word PIC, the aff may argue the word has a set meaning, such as a legal term, so changing it changes what the plan does. See Solvency deficits.
  • Answer the net benefit. No link: the left-out part does not cause the harm. For a word PIC: the aff used the word in a different sense, or the replacement word has the same problem. Impact defense works too.
  • PICs bad theory. The aff argues that PICs, or this PIC, are unfair. See below.

PICs bad, in brief

Theory is an argument about the rules of the round. See Theory. Both sides have real arguments here. The full debate is on Common theory arguments.

  • Arguments against PICs (usually the aff's): they take almost all of the aff's ground. The aff must defend every small piece of its plan. The debate becomes about a detail, not the main idea.
  • Arguments for PICs (usually the neg's): the aff chose every part of its plan, so every part should be tested. Real policy fights often turn on details. A specific PIC can show deep research.

Many teams also argue a narrower version, such as "word PICs bad" or "PICs need a specific solvency card." If the aff wins theory, the usual remedy is to reject the counterplan, not the team. Teams can argue for more. See Reasons to reject.

How to judge a PIC

  1. Theory first, if it was extended. Decide it as argued. If the counterplan is rejected, compare the plan with the status quo. The net benefit still counts as a disadvantage (DA) against the plan.
  2. Competition. Does the perm avoid the net benefit? With a PIC it usually does not, unless the net benefit is weak.
  3. Solvency. How much does the left-out part matter to the case?
  4. Weigh. Compare that solvency deficit with the net benefit.

For a word PIC, you may also need to decide whether words count as much as actions. Use the framework the teams argued, not your own view.

Example in flow shorthand

A plan-minus PIC. The plan funds program X everywhere. The PIC leaves out area Y. The 1NC comes first, then each 2AC answer with the 2NR and 2AR replies. Key: CP = counterplan, Solv = solvency, NB = net benefit, adv = advantage, arg = argument, -> = leads to.

1NC
PIC: do plan, but not in area Y
Solv: CP solves the rest of the
  case
NB: plan in area Y replaces Y's
  local program (Analyst 21)

2AC 1: PICs bad: steal aff
  ground; reject the team
2NR: Tests every part of plan;
  reject the arg at most
2AR: PICs make aff defend any
  tiny piece; reject the team

2AC 2: Deficit: area Y has the
  worst harm
2NR: Y has own program; plan
  adds little
2AR: Y program is small; half
  the adv is in Y

2AC 3: Perm do both
2NR: Perm = whole plan, so
  still in Y -> links
2AR: (no answer)

2AC 4: No link: plan adds to
  Y's program
2NR: Plan's rules override Y's
  own rules (Analyst 21)
2AR: Override is rare; link is
  tiny

How to read it: the 2NR answered all four 2AC answers. The 2AR did not answer the 2NR's reply on the perm, so the perm is gone, and the counterplan competes if the net benefit is real. The judge decides theory first. If the aff wins "reject the team," the aff wins. If the judge only rejects the counterplan, the plan is compared with the status quo, and the net benefit still counts as a DA. If the counterplan stands, the judge weighs two things. The deficit: is area Y half the advantage, or does Y's own program cover it? The net benefit: does the plan override Y's program, or is that rare?

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a PIC with no net benefit tied to the left-out part. Fix: find a harm that only that part causes.
  • Neg: a word PIC with no reason the word matters. Fix: explain what the word does and why dropping it helps.
  • Aff: relying only on "perm do both." Fix: also answer the net benefit and prove the left-out part matters.
  • Aff: one line of PICs bad, then a long 2AR on it. Fix: develop theory in the 1AR if you plan to win on it.
  • Aff: ignoring a word PIC because "it is just a word." Fix: answer the link and the impact like any other argument.
  • Judges: assuming PICs are always fair, or always unfair. Fix: decide theory as argued in the round.
