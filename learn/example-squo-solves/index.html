The "status quo solves" argument (SQ solves)

Plain version. The status quo is the world as it is now, without the plan. "Status quo solves" is a negative argument that says the problem is already being fixed. A new law, a current program or a trend is taking care of it. If that is true, the plan adds little or nothing, so there is less reason to vote for it. The affirmative usually answers that the current fix is too slow, too small, or blocked. This argument comes back every year, whatever the topic.

The shape

"Status quo solves" is an on-case argument: the neg makes it on the case sheet, next to the aff's own arguments. It does not need a counterplan or a disadvantage. It needs one thing: proof that something now in place fixes the harm the aff names.

The neg usually points to one of three things.

SourceWhat the neg says (generic)
A new law or program"A law passed last year already funds program X."
A trend"More people move into the field each year, so the gap is closing."
Other actors"Some states and private groups already do what the plan does."

What it attacks

The argument can hit two parts of the case. Neg teams should say which one they mean.

Inherency

Inherency is the aff's proof that something in the status quo blocks a fix, so the harm will last without the plan. See Inherency. "Status quo solves" says the block is gone. The law changed, or the people in charge now act. If the neg wins this fully, the aff has no reason to change anything.

The advantage and solvency

Solvency is proof that the plan fixes the harm. See Solvency. "Status quo solves" does not say the plan fails. It says the good result happens anyway. So the plan's advantage is non-unique: the plan does not cause the good result, because the status quo causes it too. Many policy-style debaters call this "uniqueness" on the case. See Uniqueness.

In practice, most neg teams use it to shrink the advantage, not erase it. "The status quo solves 80% of the harm" leaves the aff with 20%. That smaller advantage is then easier to outweigh with a disadvantage.

How the aff answers

The 2AC usually makes several of these answers.

  • The barrier is still there. Name the law, rule or gap that still blocks a full fix. A named barrier is stronger than "no one is doing it."
  • Too small. The current program covers only part of the harm. "It reaches 10% of the people who need it."
  • Too slow. The current fix will take years or decades. Harm happens in the meantime.
  • Wrong place or wrong people. The current fix helps a different group or area.
  • Not funded or not lasting. The program is a pilot, has no money yet, or ends soon.
  • The plan is still needed. The current fix only works with the plan's extra step.
  • Turn it against the neg's DAs. If the status quo already does what the plan does, a disadvantage that says the plan causes harm should already be happening too. That makes the DA non-unique. See below.

The tension with disadvantages

This is a common trap for the neg. Suppose the neg says, "A new law already funds program X." Then the neg also runs a spending DA: "The plan's cost forces cuts." The aff can answer: "If the law already pays for the same thing, the cuts should already be happening. Your DA is non-unique."

The neg can escape the trap. It can show that the current program is smaller or cheaper than the plan. But that admits the status quo does less than the plan, which helps the aff's answer to "status quo solves." Neg teams should plan for this before the round.

How it interacts with presumption

Presumption decides who wins when neither team has a clear reason to win. Most judges give it to the neg by default, because the aff asks for change. See Presumption.

"Status quo solves" is one of the usual ways a round can reach presumption, though presumption rarely decides a round. If the neg wins that the status quo already fixes the whole harm, the plan adds nothing. With no advantage left, the neg can argue that the judge should keep the status quo.

More often, the neg pairs it with a small disadvantage. The case advantage is now tiny, so even a small risk of the DA can outweigh it. This pairing is often called case defense plus a DA. Case defense is any argument that makes the aff's advantage smaller. See Case turns and case defense.

How to judge it

  1. Ask how much the status quo solves. It is rarely all or nothing. Compare the size of the current fix with the size of the harm.
  2. Compare the evidence. In this fight, the newer and more specific card often wins. A card that names the current program beats a card that says "things are improving."
  3. Check for the DA tension. If the aff pointed it out, decide whether the neg's DAs survive.
  4. Use the judge's own paradigm, or the framing the teams argue. A stock issues judge treats inherency as a must-win item, so a strong "status quo solves" can end the round. A policymaker judge weighs the part of the harm the plan still adds. See Judge paradigms.
  5. If nothing is left, decide presumption as argued.

How to read it: the 1AC says no program trains rural nurses. The 1NC makes two attacks. On the first, the 2AC says the new law has no funding yet. The 2NR says funds will come, but reads no card, and the 2AR points that out. On the second, the 2NR drops the 2AC answer, so the 2AR calls it conceded: the trend is too slow. The 2AC also adds a third answer: if the law already does what the plan does, the spending DA (on its own sheet) is non-unique. The 2NR says the law is small and the plan is much bigger. The 2AR replies that if the law is small, the status quo does not solve, so the neg must pick one claim. A judge would likely say the aff keeps most of its inherency. The judge must still decide how much an unfunded law will do.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: "status quo solves" with no named program. Fix: read a card that names the law or program and says it works.
  • Neg: running a DA that the status quo should already trigger. Fix: check that your case arguments and your DAs fit together.
  • Neg: going for presumption with a small advantage still on the flow. Fix: win that the status quo solves all of it, or keep a DA in the 2NR.
  • Aff: "the status quo does not solve" with no reason. Fix: say why: too small, too slow, wrong place, or not funded.
  • Aff: dropping the inherency card after the 1AC. Fix: extend the barrier in every speech.
  • Judges: treating any current program as a loss for the aff. Fix: ask how much of the harm the current program really solves.
