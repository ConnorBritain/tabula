Weighing and judging · 7 min read · intermediate

Turns case

Plain version. "Turns case" is a way to compare two arguments. It says: my harm would make your harm worse, or stop your plan from working. For example, the negative may say the plan causes a recession, and the recession would cut the very program the plan needs. If that is true, the negative wins its own harm and shrinks the affirmative's at the same time. "Outweighs and turns" means a team claims both: its harm is bigger, and it also makes the other harm worse.

What turns case means

Most weighing compares two impacts side by side: which is bigger, more likely, or sooner? See Impact calculus. Turns case is different. It says the two impacts are connected. One of them causes or worsens the other.

The usual form comes from the neg. A disadvantage (DA), the neg's argument that the plan causes something bad, can claim to "turn the case":

  • "The plan causes a recession (the DA). A recession cuts health budgets. So the plan's health program fails anyway."

If the turn is true, the aff's advantage shrinks, and the neg still has its DA impact. That is why many debaters see it as strong weighing.

The aff can make the same move the other way: "case turns the DA." For example: "The plan's health program keeps workers healthy, and that helps the economy. So the plan helps stop the recession the DA predicts."

Kritiks and turns case

A kritik (K), an argument that questions the ideas behind the aff, can also claim to turn the case. It often says the aff's way of thinking will make its own plan fail or backfire. See Kritiks.

"Outweighs and turns"

A team often claims two things at once:

  1. Outweighs: our impact is bigger, more likely, or sooner than yours.
  2. Turns: our impact also makes yours worse.

This is called "outweighs and turns." The two claims work as "even if" arguments. Even if the judge thinks the impacts are close in size, the turn tips the scale. Even if the turn fails, the impact may still outweigh.

Turns case vs a case turn

The names are close, so keep them apart.

  • A case turn is an on-case argument about the plan itself: "the plan makes the harm worse." See Case turns and case defense.
  • Turns case is a weighing argument made from another sheet, usually a DA or K: "our impact makes your harm worse."

On the flow, a turns case argument often appears on the DA sheet, but it is about the case. Many judges like it to be cross-applied to the case sheet so the link is clear.

How to make a turns case argument

  1. Win your own impact first. A turn from a DA the judge does not believe adds nothing. The turn is only as strong as the DA's chance of happening.
  2. Name the specific link to the case. "A recession cuts health budgets, and the plan depends on a health budget." A vague claim, such as "war makes everything worse," is easy to answer.
  3. Read evidence if you can. A card that connects your impact to their harm is much stronger than a claim.
  4. Make it early enough. Most judges expect a turns case argument in the block (the 2NC and 1NR) at the latest, so the aff has a chance to answer. A turns case claim first made in the 2NR is often treated as a new argument. Judges differ on how much new weighing they allow in the last speech.
  5. Tell the judge what it means. "Even if the case is real, the DA turns it, so the aff's advantage cannot outweigh."

How to answer turns case

  • No link to the case: the DA's impact does not affect the plan. "The plan is funded by a separate set-aside; a recession cannot cut it."
  • The turn depends on the DA: if the DA is unlikely, so is the turn. Attack the DA's uniqueness, link and internal link, and the turn falls with it.
  • Timeframe: the case solves first. "The plan works in year 1. The recession, if it comes, is years away."
  • Case turns the DA: argue the connection goes the other way. Be careful: this only helps if you explain why your direction is stronger.
  • Non-unique turn: the aff's harm is already being made worse by other things now, so the DA adds little.

How to judge turns case

  1. Check that the impact was won. A turn from a DA the neg lost has no weight.
  2. Check that the turn was answered or dropped. A dropped turn, explained with a reason, is strong. A turn the aff answered with a specific card may be gone.
  3. Look for the specific link. A turn that names how one impact causes the other is stronger than a general claim.
  4. Check when it was first made. If it first appeared in the 2NR, ask whether the teams argued that it was new. Follow your own paradigm on new weighing if no one argued it.
  5. Resolve turns before you weigh. If a turns case argument stands, it changes how much of the advantage is left. Settle it first, then compare what remains.

Example in flow shorthand

A state programs DA with a turns case argument. The neg says that when the federal government acts, states pull back their own care programs, and the plan's clinics need state staff.

                                             plan's clinics       yr 1; states exit    leave once states    "over time" ->

How to read it: the 1NC reads the DA: when the federal government acts, states pull back their own care programs. The 2AC says there is no link, because states keep their own programs, and that the case outweighs. The block extends the link. It adds that the DA turns case: the plan's clinics need state staff, so if states pull back, the plan fails (Analyst 22). The 1AR extends the no-link card and answers the turn with timeframe: the plan works in year 1, and states would exit only over years. The 2NR extends the link and the turn, says staff leave once states exit, and claims "outweighs and turns." The 2AR extends the no link, and says the neg's own card says states exit "over time," so the case solves first. The judge decides the link first. If there is no link, the turn falls with it. If there is a link, the judge settles the timing question before weighing.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: claiming "turns case" with no specific link. Fix: name how your impact hurts their plan or harm, with a card if you can.
  • Neg: making the turn first in the 2NR. Fix: put it in the block, so it is clearly not new.
  • Neg: relying on the turn but losing the DA. Fix: win the DA's link and impact first; the turn depends on them.
  • Aff: ignoring the turn. Fix: answer it every time, or the neg wins its impact and shrinks yours.
  • Aff: confusing it with a case turn. Fix: answer turns case where it is made, usually on the DA sheet, and cross-apply to the case.
  • Judges: weighing before resolving the turn. Fix: first decide if the turn stands, then compare what is left.
