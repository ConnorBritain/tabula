The affirmative case and stock issues · 6 min read · beginner

Inherency

Inherency says the problem will not fix itself. Something in the world now keeps it going: a law, a gap in a program, or people who do not want to act. If the problem is already being fixed, the plan adds nothing, so there is no reason to vote for it. Inherency is one of the stock issues, the basic jobs of an affirmative case. The negative's usual attack is "the status quo already solves."

What inherency means

The status quo is the world now, without the plan. Inherency is the aff's proof that the harm is built into the status quo. Something blocks the fix. Because of that block, the harm will go on unless the plan acts.

Inherency answers one question: why is this not already being done? If the aff has a good answer, the plan makes a real difference. If not, the judge has little reason to change anything.

Inherency is one of the five stock issues: harms, inherency, solvency, significance and topicality. See Stock issues.

The kinds of inherency

Teams name four common kinds. The Stock issues page lists the same four.

KindWhat blocks the fixExample (generic)
StructuralA law, rule or policy"Current law bans funding for program X."
AttitudinalThe people in charge do not want to act"Officials oppose new spending on X."
ExistentialNothing names the block, but the harm exists and nothing solves it"The problem is here, and no one is fixing it."
GapA program exists but leaves a hole"The current program covers cities, not rural areas."

Structural inherency

A structural barrier is a written rule: a law, a regulation, or a budget limit. It is the clearest kind, because the aff can point to it. The plan then changes or removes that rule.

Attitudinal inherency

An attitudinal barrier is a lack of will. The law may allow the fix, but the people who could act choose not to. Evidence shows officials or the public oppose the change.

Existential inherency

Existential inherency says: the harm exists, and nothing now solves it, so something must be blocking a fix. It does not name the barrier. Some judges accept it as enough. Others think it is weak, because it does not explain why the status quo fails, and the neg can argue the fix is coming.

Gap inherency

Gap inherency admits the status quo does something, but not enough. A current program leaves a hole, and the harm falls through it. Many modern cases use gap inherency, because there is often some program already in place.

Inherency and uniqueness

On a disadvantage, uniqueness asks whether the bad thing is already happening. Inherency asks a similar question about the case: is the good result already happening? If the status quo already solves the harm, the aff's advantage is non-unique: the plan does not cause the good result, because it happens anyway. Many policy-style debaters use the word "uniqueness" for this case argument too. See Uniqueness.

Some judges, often called stock issues judges, treat inherency as a must-win item. Others, often called policymakers, treat it as one factor in how much the plan adds. See Judge paradigms.

How to answer inherency (for the neg)

  • Status quo solves: a current law, program or trend is already fixing the harm. Read a card that names it.
  • The barrier is gone: the law the aff names has changed, or the officials who opposed the fix now support it.
  • The gap is small: the current program covers most of the problem, so the plan adds little.
  • Minor repairs: some traditional negs argue that a small change to a current program would fix the harm, without the plan. Judges differ on whether this is a fair neg position, and it can look like a counterplan. Teams should argue it if it comes up.

A note on attitudinal inherency: if the aff proves that officials strongly oppose the fix, the neg can argue they will undercut the plan, which is called circumvention (see Solvency).

How to defend inherency (for the aff)

  • Name the barrier. Read a card that says what stops the fix. A named law or a named gap is stronger than "no one is doing it."
  • Answer "status quo solves" with scope. Show the current program is too small, too slow, or in the wrong place. "It covers 10% of the problem."
  • Use a recent card. Inherency changes over time. An old card is easy to beat with a new one.
  • Check your plan text. The plan should remove the barrier your inherency names. If the barrier is a law, the plan should change that law.

How to judge inherency

  1. Find the barrier. What does the aff say stops the fix? Did the neg prove that barrier is gone?
  2. Ask how much the status quo solves. Inherency is often not all or nothing. If the status quo solves 90%, the plan adds 10%. A policymaker weighs that 10%. A stock issues judge may decide the aff lost inherency. Use the rule the teams argue for.
  3. Compare the dates and scope of the evidence. In a "status quo solves" fight, the newer and more specific card often wins.
  4. Do not require a perfect barrier. Most judges want a clear reason the status quo fails, not proof that change is impossible.

A gap inherency debate on a generic rural clinic case.

How to read it: the 1AC claims gap inherency: the federal program funds only city clinics. The neg makes two attacks. On the first, the 2AC reads the neg's own card back: the pilot has only 5 sites. The 2NR says the pilot will expand, but the 2AR points out there is no card for that. On the second, the 2NR drops the 2AC answer, so the 2AR calls it conceded. A judge would likely say the aff keeps most of its inherency, because a 5-site pilot leaves most of the gap open.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: no inherency card at all. Fix: read evidence that names the barrier, even in a short 1AC.
  • Aff: a plan that does not remove the barrier. Fix: match the plan text to the law or gap your inherency names.
  • Aff: proving attitudes so strong the plan cannot work. Fix: be ready to answer circumvention with how the plan is enforced.
  • Neg: "status quo solves" with no proof. Fix: name the current program and read a card that says it works.
  • Neg: arguing both "status quo solves" and "the problem is not real." Fix: these can work together, but explain each one clearly so the judge does not see a contradiction.
  • Judges: treating any current program as a loss of inherency. Fix: ask how much of the harm the status quo really solves.
