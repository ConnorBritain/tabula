Cross-examination (CX)

5 min read · Beginner

After each of the first four speeches (the constructives), the other team asks questions for three minutes. This is cross-examination, or cross-ex. It is a chance to understand the other team's arguments, to pin down what they will defend, and to set up your next speech. Cross-ex is not a speech. An answer in cross-ex matters for the decision only if someone uses it in a speech. Judges do listen closely, and good cross-ex often decides how a round goes.

What cross-ex is for

There are three main jobs:

  1. Clarify. "What does your plan do?" "Which card says that?" (A card is a quote used as evidence.) You cannot answer an argument you do not understand.
  2. Commit. Get the other team to say what they will and will not defend. "Does your plan cost money?" "Is your counterplan conditional?" (A counterplan is the neg's own plan. "Conditional" means the neg can drop it later in the round.)
  3. Set up. Get an answer you will use in your next speech. "So your evidence is from before the new law passed?"

A fourth job is quieter: show the judge you understand the round. Calm, sharp questions earn speaker points.

How to ask good questions

  • Ask short questions. One idea per question. Long questions get long answers.
  • Ask closed questions when you want a commitment. "Yes or no: does the plan take effect this year?" Use open questions only when you want to learn something.
  • Have a goal for each question. Know which speech will use the answer.
  • Do not argue. If the answer is weak, say "Thank you" and move on. Make the argument in your speech.
  • Control the time politely. If an answer runs long, say "Thank you, that is enough." It is your time.
  • Ask for documents and cards at the start, not halfway through.

How to answer well

  • Answer the question asked. Short, honest answers look confident.
  • Do not guess. If you do not know, say so, or say "My partner will explain that in the next speech."
  • Do not concede (admit) more than you must. "The plan does X" is fine. "The plan does X, and that might cause Y" gives the other team a link: proof that the plan causes a bad result.
  • Ask for the question again if it is unclear. "Can you say that again?" is better than answering the wrong question.
  • Stay calm and polite. Judges notice tone. Rude or evasive answers cost points.

Who asks and who answers

Usually, the debater who just spoke answers. The debater on the other team who does not speak next asks. So:

Cross-ex ofAsksAnswers
1AC2nd neg1st aff
1NC1st aff1st neg
2AC1st neg2nd aff
2NC2nd aff2nd neg

Some leagues allow open cross-ex, where either partner can ask or answer, or tag-team help from a partner. Others do not. Check the tournament's rules.

Prep time (each team's planning time) is sometimes used as extra cross-ex time, by agreement. Teams may ask questions during the other team's prep. This varies by league and by judge.

How judges use cross-ex

  • Flow key answers in the margin. In their notes (the flow), judges write "CX: plan costs $5B" next to the related argument.
  • Count an answer only if it is used in a speech. Cross-ex alone does not win an argument.
  • Watch for commitments. If a team says "the CP (counterplan) is not conditional" in cross-ex, many judges hold them to it.
  • Reward good cross-ex in speaker points. Clear questions and clear answers show skill.

Example in flow shorthand

A cross-ex that sets up a DA (disadvantage: a bad result of the plan). Then the same answers show up later in the round. "$" means money, "L" means link, and "->" means "so". The block is the neg's two speeches in a row (2NC and 1NR).

CX of 1AC (margin note):
  plan costs $ "some", but
    no number given
  funded by "normal means"
    -> no offset (no way
    to pay named)
1NC:
  DA: Spending
  L: plan costs $50B
    (Expert 20)
2AC:
  1. No L: plan is
    cost-neutral
Block:
  Extend L: 1AC said in CX
    it costs $; no offset
    named

How to read it: the aff admitted in cross-ex that the plan costs money, so the neg used that answer to beat "no link."

Common mistakes

  • Making speeches in cross-ex. Fix: ask, get the answer, and save the argument for your speech.
  • Asking questions with no goal. Fix: before cross-ex, write 3 or 4 questions tied to your next speech.
  • Never using the answers. Fix: refer to the cross-ex in your speech: "In cross-ex, they said..."
  • Answering "I do not know" too often. Fix: know your own case. If you truly do not know, point to your partner's next speech.
  • Being rude or talking over the other debater. Fix: be firm but polite. It is a conversation, not a fight.
  • Judges treating a cross-ex answer as a full argument. Fix: it counts only if a team uses it in a speech.
