Disadvantages · 6 min read · beginner

Impacts (I)

Plain version. An impact is the "so what" of an argument. It is the bad result at the end of a disadvantage, or the good result at the end of an advantage. Impacts can be large and unlikely, like a war, or small and very likely, like people losing a service. The other team can say the impact is smaller than claimed, or even that it is good. At the end, the judge compares the impacts each team is winning.

What an impact is

Every argument in policy debate ends somewhere. A disadvantage (DA) ends with a harm the plan causes. An advantage ends with a benefit the plan brings. That end point is the impact.

The impact answers the question, "Why should the judge care?" Without one, an argument has no weight. "The plan costs money" is a link. "The cost forces cuts that leave families without food" is an impact.

A terminal impact is the final harm in the chain, the one that matters on its own. For example, loss of life, or harm to people's rights. Some debaters stop at an earlier step, like "the economy slows." The other team can then ask, "So what?"

Kinds of impacts

Teams describe impacts in many ways. These are common groups. They overlap.

KindWhat it looks like (generic)
Large-scaleA war between countries, a disease that spreads widely, collapse of a key system
EconomicA recession, lost jobs, lower wages
StructuralHarm built into how a system works, such as poverty or unequal treatment, that hurts people every day
Rights and valuesLoss of freedom, privacy or dignity, which some argue matters on its own, apart from body counts
Local or smallA program ends, a town loses a service

No kind wins by default. A large-scale impact may be very unlikely. A structural impact may be certain and already happening. How teams compare them is the subject of Impact calculus. For a full weighing debate between very large impacts and structural ones, see Extinction vs structural violence.

How to answer an impact

These answers work against any impact, on a DA or an advantage.

  • Impact defense: the harm is smaller, slower or less likely than the other team says. "Recessions end. Jobs come back within a few years."
  • No terminal impact: the other team never said why the result matters. Point that out.
  • Impact turn: the "bad" result is actually good, or the "good" result is actually bad. "Cuts to that program would be good, because it wastes money." This is offense: it gives the judge a reason to vote for your side.
  • Weighing: even if the impact is true, your impact is bigger, more likely or sooner.

Impact turns: use with care

An impact turn can win a round, but it carries risk.

  • You must win that the result is truly good. A half-won impact turn often helps nobody.
  • Do not combine it with a link turn on the same DA: that is a double turn, which says the plan prevents something good; see Links and link turns.
  • Some impact turns are off limits for many judges. Arguing that a large loss of life is good, for example, will lose many judges, and some leagues or judges reject such arguments outright. Read the judge's paradigm.

Impact framing

Some teams argue about how to weigh impacts before the round gets to specific impacts. This is often called impact framing. For example, one team may argue that probability should come first, so very unlikely impacts count for little. Another may argue that magnitude should come first, so even a small risk of a very large harm wins.

These are weighing arguments, and they belong to Impact calculus. On this page, it is enough to know they exist. A judge should use the framing the teams win, not the judge's own.

How to judge impacts

  1. Find the impacts each team is still winning at the end of the round. Check what was extended in the last speeches.
  2. Ask whether each one has a terminal impact, or stops short.
  3. Check for impact turns. A won impact turn changes which side the impact helps.
  4. Compare them using the weighing the teams gave you. See Impact calculus. If no one weighed, explain in your decision how you compared them.

Example in flow shorthand

                           lives > money            people lose care the     first; no turn

How to read it: the 1NC's impact is a full chain: a trade fight causes a recession, then job loss, then health harms. The health harm is the terminal impact, the final harm to people. The 2AC gives impact defense (recessions end and jobs come back) and says the case outweighs, because lives matter more than money. The 2NR extends the chain: a long slump causes job loss and then health harms. It adds a turns-case argument: sick and poor people lose the care the plan gives. The 2AR says the neg's evidence is about deep slumps, and this one would be mild. It answers turns case by saying the plan's care starts first. The judge decides how bad the recession would be, and whether it would undo the plan's benefit.

Common mistakes

  • Both: no terminal impact. Fix: finish the chain. Say what happens to people.
  • Aff: an impact turn plus a link turn on one DA. Fix: pick one kind of turn per DA.
  • Neg: a huge impact with a weak chain. Fix: tighten the internal links, or pick a smaller impact you can prove.
  • Both: naming impacts but not comparing them. Fix: in the last speeches, say why yours matters more.
  • Judges: preferring a kind of impact by habit. Fix: compare impacts using the weighing the teams argued.
