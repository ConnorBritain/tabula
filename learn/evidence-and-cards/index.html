Evidence and cards

6 min read · Beginner

Debaters back up their claims with evidence: short quotes from experts and other sources, each called a card.

  • Parts of a card: A tag, a one-line summary written by the debater; a cite, which says who wrote it and where; and the text, which is the quote itself.
  • Reading it out loud: Debaters read only the key parts out loud.
  • What makes it strong: A card is only as strong as what the author really said, so judges and debaters can ask to read it.

The parts of a card

An invented example:

TAG:
The plan saves money within
three years.

CITE:
Analyst 21 — Pat Analyst,
budget researcher, "Cost Review
of New Programs," Policy
Journal, March 2021.
[page or URL]

TEXT:
...EARLY COSTS ARE REAL, but
our review found that programs
of this kind MAY RETURN MORE
THAN THEY SPEND BY THE THIRD
YEAR, MAINLY THROUGH LOWER
EMERGENCY COSTS...

(Capital letters show the part the debater reads out loud. In a real speech document, the file of cards a team shares, the read part is underlined and highlighted.)

PartWhat it isWhy it matters
TagThe debater's one-line claimTells the judge what the card proves. It is the debater's words, not the author's.
CiteAuthor, date, qualifications, source, page or linkLets anyone find and check the source. Shows if the author is an expert.
TextThe quote, unchangedThe real evidence. The tag must match it.

On the flow (the notes everyone takes during the round), debaters usually write the author's last name and the year: "Analyst 21."

Underlining and highlighting

A full card often has a paragraph or more of text. Debaters mark it in layers:

  • The full paragraph is kept, so the context is there.
  • Underlining marks the important parts.
  • Highlighting marks the parts the debater will actually read out loud.

Reading only the highlighted words saves time. But the highlighted words must still say what the author meant.

Cutting cards fairly

"Cutting" a card means picking the quote and marking it. Fair cutting has a few rules:

  • Do not change the author's words. No added words, no changed words, inside the text.
  • Do not cut out words that change the meaning. If the author wrote "this will not happen," the card cannot skip the word "not." If the author's next sentence says "but this view is wrong," the card should not stop just before it.
  • Keep enough context. The full paragraph should be in the document, even if only part is read.
  • Write a fair tag. The tag can be strong, but it must match what the text says. "Plan saves money" is fine if the card says so. "Plan saves billions right away" is not, if the card says "by the third year."
  • Give a full cite. Author, qualifications, date, title, source, and a page or a link.

Leagues have their own evidence rules. Many follow national rules on what counts as a full cite and what to do about evidence problems. Check the tournament's rules.

Clipping: an ethics issue

Clipping means saying you read a card's highlighted words, but skipping some of them out loud. For example, a debater reads the start and the end of the highlighted text and skips the middle, while the document shows it all as read.

Clipping is cheating, because the judge and the other team think more evidence was read than really was. Most leagues treat it as a serious ethics violation. A round can be stopped, and a team can lose, if clipping is shown.

To avoid clipping: read every highlighted word. If you run out of time, say "cut the card here" or "mark the card at '...'" so everyone knows where you stopped. Then the other team can check the document.

If you think clipping happened: do not accuse anyone during a speech. Tell the judge after the speech. Many leagues have a set process, often needing a recording. Follow the tournament's rules. See Round conduct.

Asking for evidence

  • During the round, most teams share speech documents by email or file share. You can read the cards as they are read. See Speech docs.
  • In cross-ex, you can ask, "Which card says that?" or "Can I see that card?"
  • After the round, a judge can ask to read specific cards. This is common when a card is central to the decision. The judge should read what was read in the round (the highlighted parts), not the parts that were skipped.

Judges: evidence helps, but the debaters' explanation matters most. A great card that no one explains is weaker than a good card that a debater explains well. Many judges say this in their paradigms (short notes on how they judge).

Example in flow shorthand

A debate about one card: does the evidence say what the tag says? "Adv" means advantage (a good result of the plan). "ev" means evidence, "$" means money, and "->" means "so".

1AC:
  Adv: Savings
  Plan saves $ (Analyst 21)
1NC:
  1. Analyst ev says "may"
    - not certain
  2. Ev predates new law
2AC:
  1. "May" is still a real
    chance: a study of past
    programs
  2. Law changed nothing on
    costs
2NR:
  Their own card reads "early
    costs are real" -> cuts
    happen now, before savings

How to read it: the neg attacks the card itself, and the 2NR turns a line the aff read out loud against the aff.

Common mistakes

  • A tag that overstates the card. Fix: make the tag match the text. Judges and opponents will read it.
  • Cutting out words that change the meaning. Fix: keep "not," "but," and "unless" if they matter to the meaning.
  • Thin cites. Fix: always include author, qualifications, date, source, and page or link.
  • Skipping highlighted words. Fix: read every highlighted word, or mark the card where you stopped.
  • Reading cards with no explanation. Fix: after a card, say in your own words why it matters.
  • Judges reading unread parts of a card. Fix: weigh what was read in the round, not text that was skipped.
