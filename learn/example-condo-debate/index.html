Condo with three or more advocacies

8 min read · Advanced

Conditionality, or condo, means the negative offers several options of its own, such as two counterplans and a kritik, and may drop any later.

  • The aff often argues: three or more options is unfair, because the affirmative must answer all of them in one speech.
  • The neg's view: Options are a fair way to test the plan.
  • This page: It walks through that debate, speech by speech, without taking a side.

The shape of the round

An advocacy is a position a team defends as its own, such as a plan, a counterplan or a kritik's alternative. Conditional means the neg may drop it later without penalty. See Conditionality.

A typical shape for this debate. The 1NC is the first neg speech, the 2AC the second aff speech, the block the two neg speeches in a row, and the 1AR the first aff rebuttal.

  • 1NC: two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional. It also reads a disadvantage (DA) or two, and case arguments.
  • CX of the 1NC: the aff asks, "What is the status of each counterplan and the kritik?" The neg says, "All conditional."
  • 2AC: the aff answers everything, including a short condo argument.
  • Block: the neg answers condo, often in one of the two block speeches.
  • 1AR: the aff decides whether to extend condo as a real option.
  • 2NR and 2AR: each side decides how much time to give it.

Some rounds add a counterplan in the block. Many aff teams argue this makes condo worse, because the 1AR has less time than the 2AC had.

The 2AC theory block

The 2AC must answer every option, so condo is often short here: 10 to 30 seconds, read from a prepared block. It still needs all four parts of a theory argument. See Theory.

Interpretation. The aff picks a rule. Common choices:

  • "The neg gets no conditional options."
  • "The neg gets one conditional counterplan."
  • "The neg may run options dispositionally": it may drop one only if the aff makes a perm (permutation: a test that does both the plan and the counterplan).

The choice matters. A strict rule ("no condo") is easy to violate but harder to defend. A loose rule ("one option") is easier to defend, and three options still violate it.

Violation. "They ran three conditional options: two counterplans and a kritik."

Standards. Common aff reasons:

  • Time split: each option takes a share of the 2AC's limited time. Answers to each get thinner.
  • Strategic cost: the aff may avoid strong answers, such as a DA against a counterplan, because the neg can drop it and the answer goes with it.
  • Contradictions: the options may say opposite things. For example, the kritik says state action is bad, while the counterplans use the state.
  • Depth: many options reward breadth over going deep on a few arguments.

Voter. "Reject the team. The time split already happened, so ignoring a counterplan cannot undo it." See Reasons to reject.

The neg answers

The block usually answers with a counter-interpretation, standards and a remedy argument.

Counter-interpretation. Common neg rules:

  • "Conditional options are fine if they do not contradict."
  • "The neg may run up to a set number of conditional options."
  • "Unlimited conditional options are fine."

Standards. Common neg reasons:

  • Testing the plan: a good decision compares the plan with several options.
  • Aff side bias: the aff picks the plan and has months to prepare. Options help balance that.
  • Decision skill: choosing what to go for in the 2NR is a key skill.
  • Aff tools: the aff has perms, theory and offense against each option.

Defense. "No harm in this round: the 2AC answered all three options." When this is true, it can be a strong point. The aff's best reply is proof of a specific harm in this round, such as an answer it had no time to make.

Remedy. "Reject the argument, not the team. The judge can ignore any option the aff thinks is unfair." Some neg teams also argue for judge kick: if the 2NR goes for a counterplan and the judge finds it worse than the plan, the judge should then compare the plan with the status quo. Judges differ on judge kick. See Conditionality.

The 1AR choice

The 1AR has little time and many arguments to cover. It has two main choices.

  • Extend condo as a real option. The 1AR spends real time on it: the interpretation, one or two standards with examples from this round, and the remedy. This keeps condo alive for the 2AR.
  • Extend it briefly, or let it go. The 1AR spends its time on the substance, and condo becomes a small issue.

If the 1AR does not extend condo, most judges will not let the 2AR go for it.

How the 2NR handles it

If the 1AR extended condo, the 2NR must answer it. A short answer can lose a round if the 2AR goes all in. Common 2NR moves:

  • Collapse. The 2NR kicks (drops) the extra options and goes for one. It then argues the harm was small.
  • Answer the 1AR line by line. It extends the counter-interpretation and the standards.
  • Point to the 2AC. "The 2AC answered all three. Name an answer you could not make."
  • Hold the remedy. Even if the aff wins, the judge should only reject the argument.

How the 2AR goes for it, or not

The 2AR can go for condo in three ways.

  • All in. The whole 2AR is condo. This is a big bet. It works best when the 1AR set it up well and the 2NR's answers were thin.
  • Part of the 2AR. The 2AR splits time between condo and the substance. This hedges, but each part is thinner.
  • Not at all. The 2AR drops condo and wins on the substance.

Each choice depends on the judge, on the 2NR's answers, and on how the substance looks. See Rebuttal strategy.

Example in flow shorthand

The 1NC ran two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional. Only the condo arguments are shown, one row at a time. Key: CI = counter-interpretation, CP = counterplan, K = kritik, Ext = extend, kick = drop.

ROW 1: THE INTERPRETATION
2AC: Interp: 1 condo option;
  they ran 3 (2 CPs, K)
Block: CI: condo OK if options
  don't contradict
1AR: Ext interp: 3 options split
  the 2AC 3 ways
2NR: Kicked K and CP 2; only
  1 CP left
2AR: 3 options split the 2AC
  3 ways; perms got seconds

ROW 2: CONTRADICTION
2AC: Contradiction: K says
  state bad; CPs use state
Block: K and CPs test different
  parts of the aff
1AR: K says state bad; CPs use
  state
2NR: (no answer)
2AR: Contradiction dropped: neg
  argued both sides

ROW 3: NEG FLEX
Block: Neg flex: aff side bias
1AR: Aff picks plan, but 3 is
  too many
2NR: Ext: aff side bias; 2AC
  still answered all
2AR: Side bias can't justify
  3 worlds

ROW 4: THE VOTER
2AC: Voter: reject the team
Block: Reject arg: kick fixes it
1AR: Harm is done; reject team
2NR: Reject arg at most
2AR: Reject team: time can't be
  given back

How to read it: the 2NR kicked the kritik and one counterplan and held the remedy, but it never answered the contradiction row. The 2AR extended that drop and asked the judge to reject the team. The 2NR's counter-interpretation depends on the options not contradicting, so the dropped contradiction hurts it. The judge must still decide the remedy: reject the argument, or reject the team.

How judges with different views tend to decide

Judges hold every view on condo. These are common patterns, not rules. Most judges say their view in their paradigm. See Judge paradigms.

  • Judges who accept any number of options. They usually need a clear technical win: a dropped standard, a dropped remedy, or a neg that never answered the 1AR. Even then, some will reject only the argument.
  • Judges with a soft limit, often one or two options. Three or more is where these judges become open to condo. A well-developed 1AR and 2AR can win in front of them, especially with harm from this round.
  • Judges who care about contradictions. They focus on whether the options say opposite things, more than on the count.
  • Judges who dislike condo. They still need the aff to extend and explain it. Most will not vote on a two-line argument just because they agree with it.
  • Lay and traditional judges. Some have seldom seen a condo debate. They need a plain story: what the neg did, why it was unfair or fair, and what the judge should do. Jargon loses them.
  • On the remedy. Many judges treat condo as one of the few theory arguments that can be a reason to reject the team. Others treat it like any theory argument and only reject the option.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: a 2AR all in on condo after a 1AR that barely extended it. Fix: if you may go for it, set it up in the 1AR.
  • Aff: no harm from this round. Fix: name the answers you could not make, the perms that got seconds, or the contradictions you faced.
  • Aff: an interpretation the aff cannot defend. Fix: pick a rule you can explain, such as one option, not just the strictest one.
  • Neg: a counter-interpretation the neg does not meet. Fix: if your CI is "no contradictions," make sure your options do not contradict, or answer the contradiction claim.
  • Neg: a ten-second 2NR answer to a full 1AR extension. Fix: give condo the time the 1AR gave it, and hold the remedy.
  • Judges: deciding by your own view when the teams argued it differently. Fix: decide the arguments made, and use your default only if no one argued a point.

The aff's rule

The 1NC ran two counterplans (CPs) and a kritik (K), all conditional. You flow the condo debate.

2AC:   Interp: neg gets 1
       condo CP; they ran 3
       options
Block: CI: up to 3 condo
       options is fine
1AR:   Ext interp: 3 options
       split the 2AC

In words: the 2AC says the neg gets one conditional counterplan, and the neg ran three options. The block's counter-interpretation (CI) says up to three conditional options is fine. The 1AR extends the aff's rule and says three options split the 2AC.

What is the aff's interpretation?

  • The neg gets one conditional counterplan
  • The neg may run up to three conditional options
  • Three options split the 2AC
  • Reject the team
Show the answer

Answer: The neg gets one conditional counterplan

Why: The interpretation is the rule a team wants. The aff's rule is in the 2AC's first line: one conditional counterplan. "Up to three" is the neg's counter-interpretation. "Three options split the 2AC" is a standard: a reason for the aff's rule.

The neg's rule

A different round. The 1NC ran a counterplan and a kritik, both conditional.

2AC:   Interp: no condo
       options
Block: CI: condo OK if the
       options don't contradict
1AR:   K says state bad; CP
       uses the state
2NR:   K and CP test different
       parts of the plan

In words: the 2AC says the neg gets no conditional options. The block says conditional options are fine if they do not contradict each other. The 1AR says the kritik calls state action bad while the counterplan uses the state. The 2NR says the two test different parts of the plan.

What is the neg's counter-interpretation?

  • The neg gets no conditional options
  • Conditional options are fine if they do not contradict each other
  • The kritik and the counterplan test different parts of the plan
  • The neg may run any number of options
Show the answer

Answer: Conditional options are fine if they do not contradict each other

Why: A counter-interpretation is the rule the answering team offers instead. Here the block offers it: options are fine if they do not contradict. "No condo options" is the aff's rule. "Test different parts of the plan" is the 2NR's claim that the neg meets its own rule.

What the 1AR is doing

This is the round from the last question. The neg's rule is "condo OK if the options don't contradict." The 1AR says: "K says state bad; CP uses the state."

What is the 1AR claiming?

  • That the neg meets its own counter-interpretation
  • That the neg breaks its own counter-interpretation, because its options contradict
  • That the aff's interpretation is wrong
  • That the judge should reject only the argument
Show the answer

Answer: That the neg breaks its own counter-interpretation, because its options contradict

Why: The neg's rule allows options only if they do not contradict. The 1AR says the kritik and the counterplan say opposite things about the state. If that is true, the neg does not meet its own rule. The 2NR answers that they test different parts of the plan. The judge decides which claim is better.

Who breaks which rule?

The 2AC's interpretation is "the neg gets one conditional counterplan." The block's counter-interpretation is "up to three conditional options is fine." The 1NC ran two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional.

Which is true?

  • The neg breaks the aff's rule, and meets its own rule
  • The neg breaks both rules
  • The neg meets both rules
  • The neg meets the aff's rule, and breaks its own rule
Show the answer

Answer: The neg breaks the aff's rule, and meets its own rule

Why: Two counterplans is more than one, so the neg breaks the aff's rule. Three options is within "up to three," so the neg meets its own rule. That is why the debate is a choice between the two rules, judged on the standards. Judges hold every view on how many options is too many, so teams must argue it in the round.
