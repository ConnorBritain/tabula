Rebuttal strategy (2NR and 2AR)

8 min read · Intermediate

The last two speeches, the 2NR and the 2AR, decide most rounds. By then, each team has too many arguments to cover in five minutes. So each team must choose. A good last speech picks the one or two arguments that win, explains them well, and tells the judge why they matter more than what the other team has. Going for everything usually means winning nothing.

What the last rebuttals do

The 2NR is the negative's last speech. The 2AR is the affirmative's last speech, and the last speech of the round. Each is about 5 minutes in the common national format. See Speeches, order and times.

These speeches have four jobs:

JobWhat it means
ChoosePick the arguments you will win on. Drop the rest.
ExtendCarry each chosen argument forward, with its reason and evidence, from earlier speeches.
AnswerDeal with the other team's best answers to your chosen arguments.
WeighCompare: why your winning arguments matter more than the other team's.

Most judges do not accept new arguments in the 2NR or 2AR. New explanation of an old argument is fine. A new answer to something new from the other team is usually fine too. A brand-new argument is not.

The 2NR: choosing what to go for

"Going for" an argument means making it your reason for the judge to vote neg. The 2NR has the most choices of any speech, because the neg usually starts many arguments in the 1NC.

Common 2NR choices include:

  • A disadvantage and case arguments. The DA outweighs the advantage, and the case answers make the advantage smaller.
  • A counterplan and a disadvantage. The counterplan solves the case, and the DA is the reason to prefer the counterplan to the plan. That reason is called the net benefit. See Counterplans.
  • Topicality alone. T is a question of whether the plan fits the topic. Many teams go for T by itself, because it asks the judge to vote on a different question than the case. See Topicality.
  • A kritik. A kritik (K) questions the ideas behind the aff. The 2NR goes for the kritik and its framework, with links to the aff. See Kritiks.
  • Case and presumption. The plan does not solve, so there is no reason to change. See Presumption.

How to choose:

  1. Look at what the 1AR answered badly or dropped. That is often the best place to go.
  2. Pick what you can win and weigh. An argument you are winning but cannot compare to the case is weaker than it looks.
  3. Make sure the pieces fit. A counterplan with a net benefit that the counterplan also links to is a weak pair.
  4. Think about the judge. Go for the argument this judge is most likely to understand and vote on. See Adapting to your judge.

Drop arguments cleanly. When you stop going for an argument, many teams say so: "We are not going for the counterplan." Some judges want this. It also keeps you from leaving the aff an easy turn to win on. If an argument you drop has aff offense on it (a reason to vote aff), such as a link turn (the plan actually stops the DA's harm), you still need to answer that offense, or explain why it does not matter.

The 2AR: answering the 2NR

The 2AR is the last word. It cannot add new arguments, and it can use only what the 1AR made or extended. That is why the 1AR matters so much: the 2AR can only go where the 1AR left a path.

How to build a 2AR:

  1. Find the 2NR's main argument. What did the neg go for? Start there.
  2. Pick your best answers to that argument from the 1AR. You do not need all of them. Two strong answers, well explained, beat six thin ones.
  3. Extend your case. Remind the judge what the aff wins: the advantage, and why it is real.
  4. Weigh. Compare the case to what the neg is still winning. See Impact calculus.
  5. Use "even if." "Even if the DA is true, the case outweighs, because it is more likely and comes first." This covers you if the judge does not buy your best answer.

Do not try to answer everything. If the 2NR went for one disadvantage, the 2AR does not need to spend time on arguments the neg dropped. Spend your time where the round will be decided.

Weighing: the core of both speeches

Both last speeches should spend real time on weighing. Weighing compares impacts by size, chance, timing and whether they can be undone. That idea has its own page: Impact calculus.

One strong form of weighing is turns case: the claim that your impact makes the other team's impact worse, or stops their plan from working. See Turns case.

Many good last speeches start with a short overview: two or three sentences that say what the judge should vote on and why. Then they go line by line through the key arguments. An overview gives the judge your ballot story: the simple version of why you win.

How judges read the last speeches

  1. Start with the 2NR and 2AR. Most judges list what each team went for and decide only those issues.
  2. Check that each argument was extended, with a reason, through every speech. An argument that skipped a speech may not count.
  3. Set aside new arguments, unless they answer something new.
  4. Look for the weighing. Use the comparisons the debaters made before adding your own.
  5. Do not finish the argument for a team. If a team did not explain why its argument wins, it is not the judge's job to do it.

See The ballot and the RFD.

Advanced: the 2NR and conditional arguments

In many rounds, the neg's counterplans and kritiks are conditional: the neg may drop them at any time. See Conditionality. This gives the 2NR more choices, but it raises a question. If the 2NR goes for a counterplan and the counterplan loses, can the judge still compare the plan to the status quo (the world without the plan)?

Some judges will do this if the 2NR asks them to. This is sometimes called judge kick. Other judges think the 2NR must choose one world, and they will not go back to the status quo on their own. The aff can argue against it. Judges differ, so a 2NR that wants this should ask for it out loud, and a 2AR that opposes it should say why.

Example in flow shorthand

A 2NR and 2AR on one round. The neg ran a counterplan (CP), a disadvantage (DA) and topicality (T) in the 1NC. The 2NR went for the counterplan and the DA.

Key: a perm (permutation) says "do the plan and the counterplan together." "$" means money, "->" means "leads to", and ">" means "matters more than."

1AR (aff):
  CP: perm do both. CP misses
    half the case
  DA: no link, plan's cost is
    offset; case outweighs
  T: we meet
2NR (neg):
  CP solves most of the case.
    Deficit is tiny
  Not going for T
  Perm links to the DA: plan
    still costs $
  DA: link card extended
  Small DA risk > tiny CP
    deficit -> vote neg
2AR (aff):
  Perm: do both (1AR). No link,
    so perm is safe
  No link: plan's cost is
    offset (1AR)
  Even if a small DA risk: CP
    misses half the case (1AR),
    and that is bigger than the DA

How to read it: "CP misses half the case" is a solvency deficit: a part of the case the counterplan does not solve. The 2NR drops T, so T is out of the round. It extends its DA link card, but it never answers the 1AR's claim that the cost is offset. The 2AR uses only answers the 1AR made. The judge must decide two things: whether the perm avoids the DA, and how big the deficit is. If the perm links and the deficit is small, even a small DA risk favors the counterplan.

Common mistakes

  • 2NR: going for everything. Fix: pick one or two arguments and go deep. Say what you are dropping.
  • 2NR: dropping an argument that has aff offense on it. Fix: answer a link turn or impact turn (the DA's harm is actually good) before you drop the argument, or explain why it does not matter.
  • 2AR: answering arguments the neg did not go for. Fix: spend your time on what the 2NR chose.
  • 2AR: new arguments. Fix: extend what the 1AR said. Most judges ignore brand-new 2AR arguments.
  • 1AR: dropping the answer the 2AR needs. Fix: extend at least one good answer on every sheet (each argument's own page of the flow), so the 2AR has a path.
  • Both: no weighing. Fix: say out loud why your argument matters more, with "even if" statements.
  • Judges: voting on arguments the 2NR or 2AR dropped. Fix: decide only what the last speeches went for.
