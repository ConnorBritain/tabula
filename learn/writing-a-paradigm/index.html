Writing a paradigm

6 min read · Beginner

A paradigm is a short note that tells debaters how you judge.

  • Why it helps: Debaters read it before the round so they can speak in a way you can follow.
  • Who can write one: You do not need debate experience to write one.
  • What to include: Say who you are, how fast you can follow, what you value, and what you will not accept. A few honest sentences help more than a long essay.

Why write one

Debaters plan each round around their judge. A paradigm gives them the facts they need: how fast to speak, how much to explain, and which arguments you know well. Without one, they have to guess. A wrong guess hurts both of you. They speak in a way you cannot follow, and you have to decide a round you did not fully understand.

Many tournaments ask judges to post a paradigm on the tournament's website or judge system. If yours does, write a short one before your first round. You can improve it later.

For the main types of paradigms (stock issues, policymaker, tabula rasa and others), see Judge paradigms. This page is about writing your own.

The four things debaters look for first

Most debaters skim a paradigm in a minute or two, often just before the round. They look for four things. Put them near the top.

  1. Speed. How fast can you follow? "Please speak at a normal talking pace" is clear. So is "Fast is fine if you are clear."
  2. Defaults. What do you do when the teams do not argue a point? For example: "If no one tells me how to weigh, I compare the size and the chance of each impact." New judges can say "I have no set defaults. Please tell me how to decide."
  3. Argument views. How do you feel about the big argument types: topicality, counterplans, kritiks and theory? It is fine to say "I am new to these. Explain them in plain words."
  4. Lines you will not cross. Rudeness, unsafe behavior, clipping (skipping words of evidence while claiming to read them), new arguments in the last speeches. Say what you will do: lower points, or report it.

Other useful parts

  • Your background, in one sentence. "Parent judge, second year." "Debated four years in high school. Now a coach."
  • The email chain. Whether you want to be on it, and the address to use. See Speech docs and email chains.
  • How you decide. One or two sentences: "I decide from my notes of what was said, not my own views of the topic."
  • Speaker points. What a typical score from you means, if you have a view.
  • Disclosure. Whether you give an oral decision, when the tournament allows it.

Be honest

The most useful paradigm is an honest one. If you are new, say so. If you cannot follow fast speech, say so. If you lean one way on a big question, such as kritiks or conditionality (whether the neg may drop its counterplan later), say so, and say whether you can still be persuaded.

Do not try to sound like an expert. Debaters adapt better to a true picture than to a flattering one. A parent judge who writes "I want clear, slow speeches and a simple story" will get exactly that from good teams.

Then judge the way you wrote. If your paradigm says "slow down," but you vote for the fastest team every time, debaters stop trusting it.

Keep it short and clear

  • Aim for a few short paragraphs, or a short list. A long essay buries the four key facts.
  • Use plain words. Avoid inside terms unless your readers will know them.
  • Put the most important line first. Speed often goes first.
  • Update it once a season, or when your views change. Remove lines that are no longer true.

Two sample paradigms

Both are invented. Use them as a starting point, and change them to fit you.

A new parent judge:

Background: parent judge, first
season.
Speed: please speak at a normal
talking pace. If I cannot follow,
I will say "clear" or "slow."
How I decide: from my notes of
what you said. I do not vote on
my own views of the topic. Tell
me what the most important issue
is and why you win it.
Arguments: I am new to
topicality, counterplans and
kritiks. If you run one, explain
it in plain words and tell me
why it matters.
Lines: be respectful. I will
lower points for rudeness.
Email chain: yes, please add me
(address on my judge card).

An experienced judge. It uses terms that debaters know. A new judge does not need them.

Background: debated four years;
have judged for several seasons.
Speed: fast is fine if you are
clear. Slow down on tags, plan
texts and theory.
Defaults: I weigh offense against
offense. On topicality, I lean
toward competing interpretations
unless you argue otherwise.
Arguments: open to all styles.
Explain your framework and how I
should weigh it against the other
team's.
Evidence: I read cards only when
the teams disagree about what one
says. Explanation beats a card
read with no explanation.
Lines: for clipping, slurs or
unsafe behavior, I stop the round
and follow the tournament's
rules. New arguments in the 2NR
or 2AR will not count.
Email chain: yes (address on my
judge card).

Example in flow shorthand

How one paradigm line plays out in a round. The judge's paradigm says: "I use the weighing the teams give me. If no one weighs, I compare size and chance."

The neg runs a DA (disadvantage): a chain of steps from the plan to a bad result. "Extended" means the team kept the argument going in a later speech. "->" means "leads to".

2NR:
  DA: recession -> big
    conflict risk
  Link card extended
  (no weighing)
2AR:
  Case saves lives now
    (sure, smaller)
  Chance first: DA chain is
    long; each step only
    "could"
Judge's note:
  Only the aff weighed:
    chance first. Neg did not
    answer.
  Per my paradigm, use the
    aff's weighing.
  DA = big, low chance.
  Case = smaller, sure.
  Chance first -> AFF

How to read it: only the aff weighed, so the judge used the aff's weighing (chance first), not the size-and-chance default, and voted aff.

The RFD (reason for decision) should say two things. A neg weighing argument could have changed the result. And the default would have applied if no one had weighed.

Common mistakes

  • Judges: no paradigm at all. Fix: write four or five honest lines before your first round.
  • Judges: a paradigm that is too long. Fix: put speed, defaults, argument views and lines at the top, in short form.
  • Judges: trying to sound like an expert. Fix: describe how you really judge. Debaters adapt better to the truth.
  • Judges: not judging the way you wrote. Fix: reread your paradigm before the tournament, and follow it.
  • Judges: a default that overrides the debate. Fix: if the teams argue a different standard, decide that argument first. Use your default only when no one argues the point.
  • Judges: an old paradigm. Fix: update it once a season.

What is missing?

A parent judge wrote this draft paradigm.

Background: parent judge,
second season.
Speed: normal talking pace,
please.
Arguments: I am new to kritiks.
Explain them in plain words.
Lines: be respectful. I will
lower points for rudeness.

In words: the judge is a parent in a second season, wants a normal talking pace, is new to kritiks and wants them explained, and will lower points for rudeness.

Debaters look for four things first: speed, defaults, argument views, and lines you will not cross. Which one is missing from this draft?

  • Speed
  • Defaults: what the judge does when the teams do not argue a point
  • Argument views
  • Lines you will not cross
Show the answer

Answer: Defaults: what the judge does when the teams do not argue a point

Why: The draft says nothing about what the judge does when no one argues a point, for example how to weigh. A new judge can still write a default line: "I have no set defaults. Please tell me how to decide."

The honest line

You are a parent at your third tournament. You cannot follow fast speech yet.

Which speed line should you write?

  • "Speed is fine. I can follow anything."
  • "Please speak at a normal talking pace. If I cannot follow, I will say 'slow.'"
  • Leave speed out, so debaters do not think you are new
  • "I prefer fast rounds, like experienced judges do."
Show the answer

Answer: "Please speak at a normal talking pace. If I cannot follow, I will say 'slow.'"

Why: The most useful paradigm is an honest one. Debaters adapt better to a true picture than to a flattering one. If you write "fast is fine" but cannot follow, they will speak in a way you cannot follow.

Judge the way you wrote

Your paradigm says: "If no one weighs, I compare size and chance." In the round, the neg says: "Judge, put timeframe first: our harm comes sooner." The aff never answers this.

How should you weigh?

  • Size and chance, because that is what your paradigm says
  • Timeframe first, because the neg argued it and the aff did not answer
  • By your own view of which harm matters more
  • By which team read more cards
Show the answer

Answer: Timeframe first, because the neg argued it and the aff did not answer

Why: A default is only for when no one argues the point. Here the neg argued how to weigh, and the aff did not answer. So you use the neg's weighing. A paradigm that says "I use the weighing the teams give me" tells debaters this clearly.

Write your own

Write a short paradigm for yourself in the box: four or five lines. Then compare it with the checklist.

Show a model answer and checklist

Model answer: Background: parent judge, first season. Speed: please speak at a normal talking pace. Defaults: I have no set defaults, so please tell me how to decide and why your side wins. Arguments: I am new to topicality, counterplans and kritiks, so explain them in plain words. Lines: be respectful; I will lower points for rudeness.

Did yours…

  • Did it say how fast you can follow?
  • Did it say what you do when the teams do not argue a point (your defaults)?
  • Did it say how you feel about the big argument types, such as topicality, counterplans and kritiks?
  • Did it name lines you will not cross, and what you will do about them?
  • Is it honest about your background?

Why: Debaters often skim a paradigm just before the round. Short, honest lines on the four key things help them most. You can add more later, such as the email chain, and update it once a season.
