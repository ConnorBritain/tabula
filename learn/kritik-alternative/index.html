Kritiks · 8 min read · intermediate

The kritik alternative (alt)

Plain version. The alternative, or alt, is what a kritik offers instead of the affirmative's approach. Some alts ask the judge to reject the aff's way of thinking. Others propose a different way to act. Like a counterplan, the alt has to solve something, and the negative must explain how. The aff often argues that the alt is too vague, or that it can do the plan and the alt together. Some negative teams go for the kritik without the alt, and teams still debate whether that is enough.

What the alt is for

A kritik, or K, says the aff relies on a harmful way of thinking. See Kritiks. The link shows the aff thinks that way. The impact shows why that is harmful. The alternative gives the judge a third choice, besides the aff and the present system.

In that sense, the alt works a bit like a counterplan. It is something the judge can prefer to the aff. And like a counterplan, it must compete: there must be a reason the judge cannot simply have both. See Counterplans.

Like a counterplan, a K is often conditional: the neg may drop it later in the round. Whether that is fair is a theory debate. See Conditionality.

Kinds of alternatives

KindWhat it asks forExample (generic)
RejectReject the aff, or the aff's way of thinking"Reject the aff's framing of the region as a threat."
RethinkAdopt a different way of seeing the problem first"Examine the assumptions behind the aff before acting."
Act differentlyA different action, often by a different actor"Support community groups that address the harm directly."

Many alts mix these. A "reject" alt may also say what thinking should replace the old one. A "rethink" alt may lead to a later action.

Alt solvency

Alt solvency is the question of what the alt achieves. It has two parts.

Does the alt solve the K's impact? If the K says a way of thinking causes harm, the neg should explain how the alt reduces that harm. "Rejecting the frame stops it from shaping the next policy" is an explanation. "The alt solves" is not.

Does the alt solve the aff's harms? It does not always have to. But if it does not, the neg needs another reason to prefer it. Common reasons: the K's impact is bigger than the case; the K turns the case (the aff's own way of thinking makes its advantages fail); or, under the neg's framework, the case does not count. See Framework against a kritik.

Is the alt imagined to happen?

Fiat is the agreement to imagine that the plan is passed, so the debate is about whether it should be. See Fiat. Whether the alt gets fiat is a common debate.

  • Some negs say the alt is imagined to happen, just like the plan.
  • Some negs say the alt happens here, in the round: in the judge's ballot, or in how the debaters choose to think.

Each answer raises a question. If the alt is fiated, the aff may argue that it imagines a huge number of people changing their minds at once, and call it utopian. Some judges find that unfair to the aff. Others see it as no different from imagining a plan passes. If the alt is not fiated, the aff often asks how one ballot can solve a large real-world harm. The neg then explains what the ballot does. See Role of the ballot.

The alt and the perm

The aff's most common answer to an alt is the permutation (perm): do the plan and the alt together. If the perm works, the alt is not a reason to reject the plan. The neg answers that the two cannot be combined, or that the K's links are reasons the combination is worse than the alt alone. See Permutations.

Floating PIKs

A PIK (plan-inclusive kritik) is an alt that keeps the plan but rejects some part of the aff, often its reasons or its words. In effect, it says: "Do the plan, but without the aff's harmful framing."

A floating PIK is a PIK that is not stated as one in the 1NC. It appears later, from the wording of the alt. For example, "reject the aff's frame" might later be explained as "do the plan, but reject the frame."

Teams debate whether this is fair.

  • The neg's view, often: the alt was always about the frame, not the plan. Showing that the aff can have the plan without its framing proves the framing was the problem.
  • The aff's view, often: an alt that includes the plan takes away the aff's ground. If it appears late, the aff had no chance to answer it in the 2AC.

Judges vary. Many are skeptical of a PIK that first shows up in the last neg speech. Others accept it if the 1NC alt's words fairly include it. The aff can protect itself in cross-ex by asking, "Does the alt let us do the plan?"

Going for the K without the alt

Sometimes the neg drops the alt in the last neg speech, the 2NR, and goes for the rest of the K. This is called kicking the alt. There are three common ways to argue it.

  • The links as a disadvantage. The aff's way of thinking causes harm, so the aff is worse than the present system. The judge weighs that risk against the case.
  • The K turns the case. The aff's way of thinking makes its own advantages fail. If the aff solves nothing, the judge may vote neg on presumption: when no team proves a change is better, the judge stays with the present system. See Presumption.
  • Framework. If the judge should weigh the aff's ideas first, the neg may argue that bad ideas are enough to reject the aff, with or without an alt.

The aff often answers that without an alt, the choice is the aff or the present system. The present system thinks the same way, so the link is not unique, and the case outweighs. Some judges treat a K without an alt as a disadvantage and weigh it like one. Others accept that a won framework makes the link enough. Teams should argue which applies.

How the aff answers the alternative

  • Alt fails. The alt does not solve the K's impact. Ask the neg to explain how rejecting or rethinking changes anything. If it is too vague to judge, say so.
  • Perm. Do the plan and the alt together. Say in one sentence what the perm looks like, and why it is better than the alt alone. See Permutations.
  • Alt does not solve the case. Even if the alt works, the aff's harms go on. Weigh those harms against the K's impact.
  • Theory, if it fits. Some affs argue that a vague or floating alt is unfair. Make this point early, in the 2AC.

Use more than one answer. The neg can answer a perm with links, so the aff also needs a reason the alt alone falls short.

How to judge the alt

  1. Say what the alt does, in the neg's words. If the neg never explained it, that weakens alt solvency. Do not fill in an explanation yourself.
  2. Ask what it solves. The K's impact, the aff's harms, or both?
  3. Check how it happens. Is it imagined to happen, or does it happen in the round? Use what the teams argued.
  4. Check the perm. Can the aff do both? If yes, ask why doing both is worse.
  5. If the alt was kicked, compare the aff to the present system, plus the risk from the K's links.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic security kritik. The neg's alt rejects the aff's description of a rival country as a threat.

1NC                    2AC                      Block                     1AR                     2NR
Alt: reject the        1. Alt fails: no         Alt = judge rejects       Ballot can't stop       Kick the alt. Links
   threat frame           actor, no action         the frame; ballot         a war; alt solves       are a DA: threat
   (Theorist 19)                                   models better             nothing                 talk -> conflict
                                                   thinking
                       2. Floating PIK: alt     Alt does not include      Hold them to it: no     (none)
                          could include plan       the plan; CX said so      PIK in the 2NR
                       3. Perm: do plan and     Perm links: plan needs    Perm solves: plan       Perm is moot: no
                          reject frame             threat to be urgent       works w/o the frame     alt left to perm

How to read it: the block explains the alt as the judge rejecting the frame, and says the ballot models better thinking. The 1AR answers that a ballot cannot stop a war. On the second row, the block says the alt does not include the plan, citing cross-ex, and the 1AR holds the neg to that. The 2NR makes no PIK. In the 2NR, the neg kicks the alt and goes for the links as a disadvantage. That makes the perm moot, because there is no alt left to combine with the plan. The judge now compares the aff's case to the present system, plus the risk from the threat-talk link.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: an alt you cannot explain in two sentences. Fix: say plainly what the alt does, who does it, and how it solves.
  • Neg: kicking the alt without saying what is left. Fix: tell the judge how to weigh the links without it: as a disadvantage, as a case turn, or through framework.
  • Neg: changing what the alt means in the 2NR. Fix: keep the same explanation from the block to the 2NR.
  • Aff: never asking about the alt in cross-ex. Fix: ask what it does, who does it, whether it is fiated, and whether it includes the plan.
  • Aff: only "alt fails," with no case weighing. Fix: also explain why the case outweighs the K's links, in case the alt is kicked.
  • Judges: filling in what the alt does. Fix: judge the alt the neg explained, not the one you imagine.
