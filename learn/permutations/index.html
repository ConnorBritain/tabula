Permutations (perm)

7 min read · Intermediate

A permutation, or perm, is how the affirmative tests whether the judge can simply do both the plan and the counterplan.

  • Why it matters: a counterplan is the negative's own plan. It is only a reason to vote negative if the judge cannot simply do both plans.
  • How it works: the affirmative says, "Imagine doing the plan and the counterplan together."
  • If both work: if doing both works and avoids the harm the negative points to, the counterplan does not compete. Then it is not a reason to reject the plan.

What a perm is

A perm is a test of competition. Competition asks whether the counterplan is a real choice instead of the plan, not just something to add to it. See Competition.

The perm imagines a world with the plan and all or part of the counterplan. Then it asks: is that world better than the counterplan alone? If yes, the judge loses nothing by voting for the plan.

The perm usually wins or loses on the net benefit: the reason the counterplan alone is better, often a disadvantage (DA). If the perm still causes the DA, the perm fails, and the counterplan competes. If the perm avoids the DA, the counterplan does not compete. See Net benefits.

The kinds of perm

PermWhat it imaginesHow it is seen
Perm do bothThe whole plan and the whole counterplan togetherThe standard perm. Rarely disputed as a form.
Perm do the plan and part of the CPThe whole plan plus some parts of the counterplanCommon against counterplans with several planks (separate steps).
Perm do the counterplanThe counterplan is one way to do the planOften disputed. See below.
Severance permA perm that drops part of the planIts fairness is debated.
Intrinsic permA perm that adds something in neither textIts fairness is debated.

Perm do both

This is the most common perm. It is easy to understand: the plan and the counterplan both happen. Against a counterplan that already includes most of the plan, "do both" means the plan plus the parts of the counterplan that are not the plan.

Perm do the counterplan

Here the aff argues that the counterplan is a way of doing the plan. For example, the plan text may be broad, and the counterplan may name one specific way to carry it out. The aff says a vote for the counterplan is a vote for a version of the plan, so the counterplan is not a reason to reject the plan.

Many judges are skeptical of this perm when the counterplan has a different actor or action from the plan. They see it as the aff giving up part of its plan. Other judges accept it when the plan text truly includes what the counterplan does. The words of both texts matter most here. See Counterplan text.

Severance and intrinsic perms

A severance perm drops part of the plan. For example, it leaves out the plan's actor, a part of its timing, or the certainty that the plan happens.

An intrinsic perm adds something that is in neither the plan nor the counterplan. For example, it adds a new actor or a new step to make the combination work.

A timing perm ("do the plan, then the counterplan") is a related case. Teams argue whether it severs the plan's timing, adds a step, or is fine.

Both sides have arguments about these perms.

  • Arguments against them (usually the neg's): the aff should defend the plan it read. If it can drop or add parts, the neg's links disappear and the target keeps moving. The aff can always find a perm that avoids any net benefit.
  • Arguments for them (usually the aff's): a perm is only a test, not a new plan. Some counterplans compete in narrow or artificial ways, and these perms test that. Banning them gives the neg too much.

Many judges treat severance and intrinsic perms as unfair if the neg argues the point well. The usual remedy is to reject the perm, not the team. Other judges look more closely at the specific perm. The theory is covered on Common theory arguments and Reasons to reject.

A test or an advocacy?

This is a key question about what a perm is.

The common view: a test. Many judges see the perm as a test of competition. If the aff wins the perm, the counterplan does not compete, and it drops out of the round. The judge then compares the plan with the status quo (the world without the plan). The aff does not have to defend the perm as its new plan. The DA still counts against the plan if the plan causes it.

The other view: an advocacy. Some teams argue the perm is a policy the judge can vote for. Under this view, the aff could win by showing the perm is the best option. The neg often answers that this lets the aff change its plan in the middle of the round.

Many judges treat the perm as a test unless the teams argue otherwise. Some judges and teams treat a perm as an advocacy by default. Either way, a team that wants one view should say so and explain why.

Perms against kritik alternatives

The aff can also perm a kritik (an argument that the ideas behind the plan are harmful). A kritik has an alternative: the different approach it offers instead of the plan. The perm says, "Do the plan and the alternative together." The neg often answers that the plan's way of thinking undercuts the alternative, so the two cannot be combined well.

Teams debate how far perms apply here. Some argue that an alternative that is not a policy cannot be permed the same way. Others argue that the same test of competition applies. See Kritiks and The alternative.

How to make a perm (for the aff)

  • Say the text. "Perm: do both" is a start. Saying exactly what the perm does is better.
  • Explain the net benefit. Say why the perm avoids the DA.
  • Do not just list perms. Three unexplained perms in the 2AC give the judge little to vote on. Pick the perms you can explain.
  • Extend it. A perm must be in the 1AR and 2AR to count.

How to answer a perm (for the neg)

  • The perm links. Explain why doing both still causes the net benefit.
  • The perm is not legitimate. If it severs or is intrinsic, say how, and say what the judge should do.
  • The perm does not work. Explain why the two policies get in each other's way when done together.

How to judge a perm

  1. Is the perm legitimate? Decide any theory about it first, as argued.
  2. Does the perm avoid the net benefit? This is usually the deciding question.
  3. If the perm wins, set the counterplan aside. Compare the plan with the status quo. Any DA the plan causes still counts.
  4. If the perm loses, the counterplan competes. Weigh the solvency deficit against the net benefit. See Solvency deficits.

Example in flow shorthand

Each group follows one argument through the round. Key: "CP" means counterplan, "NB" means net benefit, and "fed" means federal.

THE COUNTERPLAN
1NC: CP: states fund X
     NB: Spending DA

PERM 1
2AC: 1. Perm do both
2NR: Perm links to DA: it keeps
     the fed cost
2AR: Plan's cost is small; DA
     risk is tiny

PERM 2
2AC: 2. Perm do the CP
2NR: Severs the fed agent:
     reject the perm
2AR: (not extended)

How to read it: "perm do the CP" is out of the round, so only "perm do both" is left. The 2AR did not deny that it links to the DA. If the perm links, the counterplan competes. This flow shows no solvency deficit, so even a small risk of the DA can favor the neg. The judge decides how big that risk is.

Common mistakes

  • Aff: "perm do both" with no explanation. Fix: say how the perm avoids the net benefit.
  • Aff: a long list of perms in the 2AC. Fix: make the perms you can defend, and extend the best one.
  • Aff: treating the perm as a new plan without saying so. Fix: if you want the perm to be an advocacy, argue it openly.
  • Neg: ignoring the perm and only extending the counterplan. Fix: answer the perm directly. Explain the link to the net benefit.
  • Neg: calling every perm "severance." Fix: show which part of the plan the perm drops.
  • Judges: voting for the counterplan without checking the perm. Fix: decide competition first.

The standard perm

The plan: the federal government funds bike lanes in every city. The neg's counterplan (CP): the fifty states fund bike lanes. The net benefit is a spending DA (disadvantage): federal spending forces cuts.

Which perm (permutation) is the standard test of competition, with a form that is rarely disputed?

  • "Perm do both: the federal government and the states both fund bike lanes."
  • "Perm do the CP: state funding is one way to do the plan."
  • "Perm: the states fund bike lanes, and the federal government does nothing."
  • "Perm: both fund bike lanes, and a new federal tax pays for it."
Show the answer

Answer: "Perm do both: the federal government and the states both fund bike lanes."

Why: "Do both" imagines the whole plan and the whole counterplan together. It is the standard perm. "Do the CP" is often disputed when the actors differ. The third perm drops the plan's actor, so it is a severance perm. The fourth adds a tax that is in neither text, so it is an intrinsic perm. The fairness of those two is debated.

Name the perm

The plan: the federal government funds after-school programs. The counterplan: the states fund after-school programs. The 2AC says: "Perm: the federal government and the states both fund the programs, and a new federal board checks their work." Neither the plan nor the counterplan has a board.

What kind of perm is this?

  • Perm do both
  • A severance perm
  • An intrinsic perm
  • Perm do the counterplan
Show the answer

Answer: An intrinsic perm

Why: An intrinsic perm adds something that is in neither text. Here the perm keeps all of the plan and all of the counterplan, and the new board is the extra part. Many judges treat an intrinsic perm as unfair if the neg argues the point well, and the usual remedy is to reject the perm, not the team. Other judges look more closely at the specific perm.

Does the perm work?

CP: states fund X
NB: Spending DA (fed cost)
2AC: Perm do both
2NR: Perm links: it keeps
     the fed cost
2AR: (no answer to the link)

In words: the 2AC made "perm do both." The 2NR said the perm still has the federal cost, so it causes the spending DA. The 2AR did not answer that.

If the judge agrees that the perm still causes the DA, what follows?

  • The perm wins, because "do both" is always legitimate
  • The perm fails, so the counterplan competes, and the judge weighs the solvency deficit against the net benefit
  • The judge votes aff, because the DA links to the perm
  • The judge ignores both the perm and the counterplan
Show the answer

Answer: The perm fails, so the counterplan competes, and the judge weighs the solvency deficit against the net benefit

Why: The perm usually wins or loses on the net benefit. If doing both still causes the DA, the counterplan alone is better, so it competes. Then the judge weighs what the counterplan misses (the solvency deficit) against the DA it avoids (the net benefit).

When the perm wins

The aff wins its perm. Neither team argued that the perm is an advocacy.

Under the common view that a perm is a test, what does the judge compare now?

  • The plan against the status quo (the world without the plan), and any DA the plan causes still counts
  • The perm against the counterplan, and the aff must defend the perm as its new plan
  • Nothing, because the aff wins the round when it wins the perm
  • The counterplan against the status quo
Show the answer

Answer: The plan against the status quo (the world without the plan), and any DA the plan causes still counts

Why: Under the test view, a winning perm means the counterplan does not compete, so it drops out. The judge compares the plan with the status quo, and a DA the plan causes still counts. Some judges and teams treat a perm as an advocacy by default. A team that wants that view should argue it openly.

A perm that helps the judge

The aff has 30 seconds left in the 2AC to answer a counterplan.

Which answer gives the judge the most to vote on?

  • "Perm do both. Perm do the CP. Perm do the plan, then the CP."
  • "Perm do both: the plan and the counterplan together. Doing both avoids the DA, because the plan's cost is too small to force any cuts."
  • "Perm."
  • "The counterplan is bad."
Show the answer

Answer: "Perm do both: the plan and the counterplan together. Doing both avoids the DA, because the plan's cost is too small to force any cuts."

Why: Say what the perm does, and say why it avoids the net benefit. A list of unexplained perms gives the judge little to vote on. Pick the perms you can explain, then extend the best one in the 1AR and 2AR.
