Counterplans · 6 min read · intermediate

Counterplan text (CP text)

Plain version. A counterplan is the negative's own plan, offered instead of the affirmative's plan. The counterplan text is the exact sentence that says what it does. It names who acts and what they do. The negative reads it in its first speech and cannot rewrite it later. Most judges give the negative only what the text says, not extra steps added later.

What the text is

The counterplan text is usually one sentence. The negative writes it. It is not a quote from evidence. It is read in the first negative constructive (1NC), usually right before the evidence that says the counterplan works.

The text works like the aff's plan text. Both sides use fiat: for the debate, we imagine the text is passed and carried out. Fiat covers what the text says and no more. See Fiat.

Most teams put the text in the speech document, so the other team and the judge can read the exact words. If there is no speech document, the aff can ask for a written copy.

The parts

PartThe question it answersExample (generic)
AgentWho acts?"The fifty states"
ActionWhat do they do?"should fund program X"
DetailsHow, how much, and when?"at the level the plan sets"
Planks (optional)Are there extra steps?"and should create a shared review board"

Many texts copy the plan's words and change one thing. For example, the plan says "The federal government should fund program X." The counterplan says "The fifty states should fund program X." The one change is the reason the counterplan exists. It is where the counterplan avoids the harm the plan causes.

A plank is one extra step in a text with more than one step. Teams add planks to fix gaps in solvency (how well the counterplan solves the problem). More planks can mean more solvency, but each plank is also one more thing the aff can attack.

Why the exact words matter

The words of the text control most of the counterplan debate.

  • Solvency. The text must match what the evidence says the actor can do. If the card says the states can "run" a program, but the text says they "fund" it, the aff can point out the gap.
  • Competition. The aff tests the counterplan with a permutation ("perm"): a test of whether the plan and the counterplan can be done together. The perm uses the text, so the words decide what "doing both" means. See Permutations and Competition.
  • Net benefit. The text must avoid the harm the plan causes. If the text includes the very action that causes the harm, the counterplan has no advantage. See Net benefits.
  • Theory. Some texts raise fairness questions, such as a text where many separate governments act at once. See Theory.

Common text shapes

  • Same action, different agent. Another actor does the plan. The states counterplan is the classic example. See The states counterplan.
  • The plan minus one part. A plan-inclusive counterplan (PIC) does most of the plan but leaves out one piece. See Plan-inclusive counterplans.
  • A different action. An advantage counterplan uses a different policy to solve the aff's advantages.
  • A different process. The counterplan does the plan, but only after a step such as asking another body first. These are often debated on theory.

More shapes are on Common counterplan shapes.

Can the negative change the text?

The usual rule is no. The 1NC text is the counterplan for the whole round. If the neg rewrites it later, the aff's answers may no longer apply, and the aff has lost time.

Two practices are debated:

  • New planks in the block. Some negative teams add a plank in the second negative constructive (2NC) to answer a solvency deficit. Some judges allow this as an answer to the 2AC. Others think it is unfair, because the 1AR has little time to answer a new plank.
  • Vague words. A short text can leave details open, such as how the plan is paid for. Some judges let the neg say the details follow "normal means": the way the actor usually does such things. Others say the neg gets only what the text states.

The aff can make these points as theory arguments, or as solvency arguments ("the text does not do this, so the counterplan does not solve this part").

How to answer the text (for the aff)

  • Read it closely. Compare it with your plan, word by word. Find what it adds, drops or changes.
  • Ask in cross-examination (CX). Ask who acts, how it is paid for, whether it does any part of the plan, and what its status is (can the neg drop it later?). See Conditionality.
  • Find what it does not do. Each missing piece can be a solvency deficit. See Solvency deficits.
  • Check for parts of your plan. If the text includes most of your plan, the perm and theory debates change.

How to judge the text

  1. Write the text down, word for word if you can, or keep the speech document open.
  2. Give the negative only what the text says. Most judges do this. If the 2NR claims the counterplan does something the text does not include, treat it as a new argument, unless the teams argued that it follows from the text. Judges split on "normal means" and on new planks. See "Can the negative change the text?" above.
  3. Use the text when you judge the perm. "Do both" means the plan and the counterplan as written.

Example in flow shorthand

1NC                     2AC                     2NR                     2AR
CP text: 50 states      1. Text says "fund,"    "Fund" = normal         Text has no enforce
   fund program X          not "enforce"; no       means: states run       word; neg can't add
                           fiat for enforcement    X with own agencies     it in the 2NR
Solv: states can fund   2. Solv deficit: no     Enforcement exists      Ev is about funding,
   X (Analyst 21)          one makes states        now in state law        not enforcement
                           follow through          (Analyst 21)

How to read it: the 1NC read a text and one solvency card, which says the states can fund X. The 2AC made two answers. First, the text says "fund," not "enforce," so enforcement is not fiated. Second, nothing makes the states follow through. The 2NR answered both. It said "fund" means normal means, so the states use their own agencies, and it said the same Analyst card shows enforcement exists in state law. The 2AR replied that the text has no enforcement word, and that the card is only about funding. The judge decides two questions. Does "fund" fairly include enforcement? Does the card prove it? If yes, the deficit is small. If no, the deficit stands, and the judge weighs it against the net benefit.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a text that does not match the evidence. Fix: write the text from what your solvency card says the actor can do.
  • Neg: changing the text in a later speech. Fix: write the text you will defend before the round. Keep it in the speech document.
  • Neg: a text that includes the action that causes your net benefit. Fix: check that the text avoids the link to your DA.
  • Aff: not asking for the text. Fix: get the exact words in CX or from the speech document before you write the 2AC.
  • Aff: answering the counterplan you expected, not the one read. Fix: compare the text with your plan, word by word.
  • Judges: giving the neg steps the text never names. Fix: hold both teams to their texts, as argued in the round.
