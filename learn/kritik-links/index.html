The kritik link

9 min read · Intermediate

The link is the part of a kritik, or K, that ties it to this affirmative.

  • The question it answers: what did the aff do, say or assume that the kritik criticizes?
  • What it can link to: the plan, the reasons for the plan, the words the aff used, or the ideas under its case.
  • Strong links: links that fit this aff closely are stronger than links that fit any aff.
  • Why it matters: most judges expect a link, so a K without one usually loses.

Why the link matters

A kritik questions the ideas behind the aff. See Kritiks. Like a disadvantage, it needs a connection to the aff. A K about a harmful way of thinking only matters in this round if the aff thinks that way.

Not every team agrees. Some critical teams argue that a method or a framework can win without a link to the plan itself. For example, they may say the judge should vote for the better way of thinking about the problem. Teams should debate this, and judges should decide from what is argued.

The link also shapes the rest of the K debate:

  • It sets up the impact: the link names what the aff does, and the impact says why that is harmful.
  • It shapes the perm, the aff's claim that it can do the plan and the alternative together. The neg often argues that its links are reasons the perm fails. See Permutations.
  • It interacts with framework, the debate about what the judge weighs. Some kinds of link only count under some frameworks. See Framework against a kritik.

The four kinds of link

Kind of linkWhat it points toExample (generic)
Plan linkWhat the plan does"The plan gives an agency new power to monitor a group of people."
Advantage linkThe reasons and evidence for the plan"Advantage 2 says a rival country is a threat that must be contained."
Representation linkThe words, images or framing the aff used"The 1AC describes a group of people as a burden."
Assumption linkThe beliefs under the aff's argument"The aff assumes experts far away know best what a community needs."

Plan links

A plan link says the action itself is part of the problem. It works much like a disadvantage link. Plan links are hard for the aff to escape, because the aff must defend its plan.

Advantage links

An advantage link points to the aff's reasons. The plan might be fine on its own, but the aff's case for it relies on a harmful idea. For example, an advantage may only matter if a group is seen as a danger. The K then argues that the aff's way of describing the problem causes harm or leads it to the wrong answer.

Representation links

A representation link (often called a "reps" link) points to how the aff spoke. It asks the judge to weigh the aff's words, not only the plan's results. Whether these links count is often decided by framework. Teams that defend them say words shape how people act. Teams that answer them say the plan's results matter more than its description.

Assumption links

An assumption link points to what the aff takes for granted. This can be a belief about how change happens, who should decide, or how we know what we know. That last kind is often called an epistemology link. Epistemology means the study of how we know things.

Links of omission

A link of omission says the aff left something out: a group, a cause, a history or a point of view. Teams and judges disagree about these links.

The neg's view, often: what a case chooses to ignore shapes what it can see. If the aff claims to solve a problem but misses its root cause, the solution may fail or make things worse.

The aff's view, often: every aff leaves out almost everything. Leaving something out is not an action. A link that applies to any aff is hard to prepare for and easy to make.

Some judges accept omission links when the neg ties them to the aff's own claims, such as a solvency claim that depends on the missing part. Others want a link to something the aff did or said. Teams should argue this, and judges should decide from what is argued.

What makes a link strong

  • It is specific. It points to this aff. Quoting the 1AC, or a cross-ex answer, is stronger than "all policies do this."
  • It is explained. It says how the aff's move leads to the K's impact, not only that the aff made the move.
  • It is sized. It says how much of the aff relies on the idea. A whole advantage built on the idea is a bigger link than one phrase.
  • It comes in time. The neg often reads general link cards in the 1NC, then applies them to the 1AC in the block (the 2NC and 1NR, two neg speeches in a row). Many judges let the 2NC add links, since it is a constructive speech. New links in the 2NR usually come too late.

Some negs also link to what the aff says later in the round, such as a 2AC answer. Many judges accept this when the aff really said it, but teams can debate it.

How the aff answers a link

  • No link: the aff does not do or say what the K claims. Read the 1AC closely to check.
  • Link defense: the link is generic. It would apply to any aff, so it is not a reason to reject this one.
  • Link turn: the aff actually works against the thing the K criticizes. For example, the aff challenges the harmful idea instead of relying on it.
  • Links to the status quo: the present system already does the same thing, so rejecting the aff changes nothing. The neg often answers that a K is about what the judge should endorse, not only about what changes in the world. Judges differ on how much this answer weighs.
  • The perm: if the link is only to one part of the aff, the aff may argue the perm avoids it. The neg will say the links are reasons the perm fails.
  • Framework: some links, such as reps links, may not count if the aff wins that the judge should weigh only the plan.

The full order of a 2AC against a K is on Answering a kritik.

How to judge the link

  1. Start with framework. It tells you which kinds of link count.
  2. Ask whether the link is about this aff. Can you say in one sentence what the aff did, said or assumed?
  3. Ask how big the link is. A small link may not outweigh a large, well-proven case.
  4. Check the perm. Does the link still apply if the aff does both the plan and the alternative?

You do not need to know the theory behind the K. Decide on how well the neg explained the link to this aff.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic capitalism kritik, which criticizes market thinking, against an aff that uses grants to reduce poverty.

Key: L1 = link 1, adv = advantage, mkt = market, $ = money, CX = cross-ex, Ext = extend (carry an argument forward), "DAs to perm" = reasons the perm is worse. Names in parentheses are invented evidence authors and years.

  L1 plan: uses market
    rewards to fix harm
    (Theorist 18)
  L2 adv: growth solves
    poverty (Scholar 20)
  L3 generic: all state action
    props up mkt

2AC
  1. No link: plan is a public
     grant, not a market
  2. Link turn: plan shifts $
     to the poor
  3. Generic: links to any aff
  4. Perm: do plan and alt in
     other areas

Block
  1. Plan pays firms to act;
     CX: grants go to private
     firms
  2. Turn assumes growth is
     good; that is the link
  3. (not extended)
  4. Links are DAs to perm:
     perm still uses mkt
     rewards

1AR
  1. Grants go to public
     bodies too, not only firms
  2. Ext link turn: $ goes to
     the poor
  3. (no answer)
  4. Perm shields the link:
     plan is a small part

How to read it: on the first link, the aff says the plan is a public grant. The block uses a cross-ex answer that the grants go to private firms. The 1AR says they go to public bodies too. On the second link, the block says the aff's link turn assumes growth is good, which is the link itself. The 1AR extends the turn. The block does not extend the third, generic link, so the neg has let it go. On the perm, the block argues the links are reasons the perm fails, and the 1AR says the perm avoids the link. The neg's best path in the 2NR is the specific first link, backed by the cross-ex answer.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: only generic links. Fix: find at least one link to a line, card or answer from this aff.
  • Neg: a link with no story to the impact. Fix: say how the aff's move leads to the harm, step by step.
  • Neg: brand new links in the 2NR. Fix: put your links in the 1NC or the block, and apply them in the 2NR.
  • Aff: saying "no link" when the 1AC plainly says it. Fix: read your own 1AC. If the link is true, turn it, outweigh it, or use the perm.
  • Aff: ignoring reps links because you expect to win framework. Fix: answer them anyway. If you lose framework, they still count.
  • Judges: assuming the link from what you know of the theory. Fix: decide on what the neg showed about this aff.

The most specific link

The aff's plan pays for bus passes for low-income workers. Advantage 1 says: "The plan gets workers back into jobs, and that grows the economy." The neg reads a capitalism kritik, which criticizes market thinking.

Which link is the most specific to this aff?

  • "All state action props up the market."
  • "Advantage 1 says the plan matters because it grows the economy. That treats growth as the goal."
  • "Every aff that spends money supports capitalism."
  • "Policy debate itself is built on market thinking."
Show the answer

Answer: "Advantage 1 says the plan matters because it grows the economy. That treats growth as the goal."

Why: A specific link points to this aff: here, a line from its own advantage. The other three would fit almost any aff. Specific links are stronger, because they show that this aff thinks the way the kritik criticizes.

Spot the generic links

The aff's plan creates a public program that trains nurses in rural towns. The neg reads four links.

Which links would apply to almost any aff? Pick all that apply.

  • "Every plan uses the power of the state."
  • "The 1AC says rural towns cannot help themselves without outside experts."
  • "All reforms leave the larger system in place."
  • "In cross-ex, the aff said the program decides for each town how many nurses it needs."
Show the answer

Answer: "Every plan uses the power of the state."; "All reforms leave the larger system in place."

Why: A generic link fits any aff, so it gives the judge little reason to reject this one. The other two point to something this aff said: a line in the 1AC and an answer in cross-ex. The aff can answer a generic link with link defense: "This would apply to any aff."

Which kind of link?

The 1AC calls a group of people who move across a border "a flood." The neg says these words cause harm, no matter what the plan does.

Which kind of link is this?

  • A plan link
  • An advantage link
  • A representation link
  • An assumption link
Show the answer

Answer: A representation link

Why: A representation link, or "reps" link, points to how the aff spoke: its words, images or framing. Whether reps links count is often decided by framework. Teams that defend them say words shape how people act. Teams that answer them say the plan's results matter more than its words.

Make the link stronger

The neg's 1NC link is generic: "All policies use state power to control people." In cross-ex, the aff says its plan lets one federal agency set the rules for every town.

What is the best way for the neg block to make this link stronger?

  • Read the same generic link card again, louder
  • Apply the link to this aff: "The plan has one distant agency set rules for every town, and cross-ex confirmed it."
  • Wait until the 2NR to name the cross-ex answer for the first time
  • Drop the link and only argue the impact
Show the answer

Answer: Apply the link to this aff: "The plan has one distant agency set rules for every town, and cross-ex confirmed it."
