Speeches, order and times

6 min read · Beginner

A policy round has eight speeches, and each of the four debaters gives two.

  • Constructives: The first four speeches are constructives, where teams build their arguments.
  • Rebuttals: The last four speeches are rebuttals, where teams answer, pick their best arguments and explain why they win.
  • Cross-examination: After each constructive, the other team asks questions for three minutes.
  • Prep time: Each team also has a set amount of prep time (planning time) to use between speeches.

The order and the common times

This is the common national high school format. "Aff" (affirmative) is the team for the plan. "Neg" (negative) is the team against it. "1st aff" means the aff's first speaker.

Local leagues can use different times. For example, a state league or a novice division may change the rebuttal length or the prep time. Always check the tournament's rules.

Prep time: each team gets a set total for the whole round. 8 or 10 minutes is common. See Prep time.

Who asks in cross-ex: usually, the debater who is not about to speak asks the questions. That way, the next speaker can prepare. Some leagues allow "open cross-ex," where both partners can ask or answer. Check the rules.

What each speech should do

1AC: first affirmative constructive

The 1AC is mostly written before the round. It reads the plan (the change the aff wants) and the advantages (reasons the plan is good), with evidence. It should show the stock issues, the aff's basic jobs: there is a problem (harms), the world now does not fix it (inherency), and the plan does (solvency). See The affirmative case.

1NC: first negative constructive

The 1NC brings the neg's arguments. Each big argument gets its own sheet (a page of notes). There are two kinds:

  • Off-case arguments start on new sheets. They include topicality (the plan does not fit the topic), disadvantages (the plan causes harm), counterplans (a better plan) and kritiks (the ideas behind the plan are wrong).
  • On-case arguments answer the 1AC directly, on the case sheet.

The 1NC usually starts with a roadmap, the order it will go in: "Three off, then case." That means three off-case arguments, then answers on the case.

2AC: second affirmative constructive

The 2AC answers every neg argument from the 1NC. It also extends the case: it carries the aff's arguments forward. This speech has a lot to cover in 8 minutes. Teams often bring pre-written answers, called blocks or frontlines.

2NC and 1NR: the negative block

These two speeches come back to back, with only a cross-ex between them. Together they are called the negative block: 13 minutes of neg speaking. The partners split the arguments. For example, the 2NC takes the counterplan and the DA (disadvantage), and the 1NR takes topicality and case. They should not both answer the same arguments.

1AR: first affirmative rebuttal

The 1AR has 5 minutes to answer 13 minutes of the block. It must be efficient. It extends the aff's best answers on each sheet, and keeps the case alive. An argument the 1AR drops (does not answer) is usually lost for the aff.

2NR: second negative rebuttal

The 2NR is the neg's last speech. It should choose. Most good 2NRs go for one or two arguments, not everything. It explains why those arguments win, and why they outweigh the case. New arguments are not allowed here, except to answer new things from the 1AR.

2AR: second affirmative rebuttal

The 2AR is the last speech of the round. It answers the 2NR, extends the aff's best path to winning, and tells the judge how to weigh. It can answer the 2NR with new explanation, and it extends what the 1AR said. It cannot add brand-new arguments.

Example in flow shorthand

One topicality argument, followed through every speech that touches it. Topicality (T) says the plan does not fit the topic.

Key:

  • Int (interpretation): the neg's meaning of a topic word. >= means "at least."
  • Vio (violation): how the plan breaks that meaning.
  • Stds (standards): reasons the meaning is better. Limits means it keeps the topic small enough.
  • We meet: the plan fits their meaning anyway.
  • CI (counter-interpretation): the aff's own meaning.
  • Reasonable (reasonability): the plan is close enough to the topic.
  • Core aff: a plan at the heart of the topic.
  • Ext = extend. yr = year. > = outweighs.
1NC
  T: "substantial"
  Int: >= 10%
  Vio: plan = 2%
  Stds: limits
2AC
  1. We meet
  2. CI: "real importance"
  3. Reasonable
2NC
  No we meet: plan is 2%
  CI: no limit
  Limits > all
1AR
  Ext we meet: plan is 12%
    by yr 2
  Reasonable
2NR
  Go for T only. Plan is 2%
    at start; CI = no limit;
    vote neg
2AR
  We meet: plan is 12% once
    in place. Reasonability:
    core aff

How to read it: the 2NC took T in the block, so the 1NR did not touch it. The 2NR chose to go for T alone. The 2AR extended "we meet" and reasonability, which the 1AR had made. It added nothing new.

Common mistakes

  • The 2AC drops an off-case argument. Fix: answer every 1NC sheet in the 2AC, even briefly. A dropped argument is hard to bring back.
  • The block partners answer the same arguments. Fix: split the sheets before the 2NC. Say the split out loud in the 2NC roadmap.
  • The 2NR goes for everything. Fix: pick one or two winning arguments and explain them well.
  • The 2AR makes brand-new arguments. Fix: extend what the 1AR said, and answer the 2NR. Most judges ignore brand-new 2AR arguments.
  • The 1AR spends too long on one sheet. Fix: plan your time per sheet before you stand up, and keep to it.
  • Judges mix up who asks in cross-ex. Fix: the debater who does not speak next usually asks. If unsure, follow the tournament's rules.

What the 2AR may do

The 2NR has ended. The second aff speaker stands up to give the 2AR, the last speech of the round.

Which of these may the 2AR do? Pick all that apply.

  • Answer the 2NR, with new explanation of the aff's arguments
  • Extend an answer that the 1AR made
  • Tell the judge how to weigh the arguments
  • Read a brand-new advantage that no aff speech said before
Show the answer

Answer: Answer the 2NR, with new explanation of the aff's arguments; Extend an answer that the 1AR made; Tell the judge how to weigh the arguments

Why: The 2AR answers the 2NR, extends the aff's best path to win, and tells the judge how to weigh. It can explain old arguments in new words. It cannot add brand-new arguments, because the neg has no speech left to answer them. Most judges ignore a brand-new 2AR argument.

A missing answer in the 2AC

The 1NC read three off-case arguments: a topicality argument, a DA (disadvantage) and a counterplan. It also read answers on the case.

1NC          2AC
T            answered
DA           (no answer)
CP           answered
Case         answered

In words: the 2AC answered the topicality argument, the counterplan and the case. It said nothing about the DA.

What is the problem with this 2AC?

  • It dropped the DA, and a dropped argument is hard to bring back later
  • There is no problem, because the 1AR can answer the DA for the first time
  • It should have answered only one argument and dropped the rest
  • There is no problem, because a DA is not on the case sheet
Show the answer

Answer: It dropped the DA, and a dropped argument is hard to bring back later

Why: The 2AC must answer every argument from the 1NC, even briefly. An argument the aff does not answer is called a drop. A dropped argument is hard to bring back, so the DA now helps the neg.

Splitting the block

The 2NC and the 1NR come back to back. Together they are the negative block. In the 2NC, the second neg speaker answers the 2AC on the DA and the counterplan.

What should the first neg speaker do in the 1NR?

  • Answer the DA and the counterplan again, to make them stronger
  • Take the other arguments, such as topicality and the case
  • Read new off-case arguments that were not in the 1NC
  • Say nothing, because the 2NC already spoke
Show the answer

Answer: Take the other arguments, such as topicality and the case

Why: The two block speakers split the arguments. They should not both answer the same arguments. If the 2NC took the DA and the counterplan, the 1NR takes the rest, such as topicality and the case.

The last neg speech

It is time for the 2NR, the neg's last speech. The neg still has four arguments that it could go for: topicality, a DA, a counterplan and case answers. The judge has not decided yet.

What should a good 2NR do?

  • Go for all four arguments, a little time on each
  • Choose one or two arguments, and explain why they win and outweigh the case
  • Read new arguments, because it is the neg's last chance
  • Repeat the 1NC word for word
Show the answer

Answer: Choose one or two arguments, and explain why they win and outweigh the case

Why: The 2NR should choose. Most good 2NRs go for one or two arguments, not everything. It explains why those arguments win and why they outweigh the case. New arguments are not allowed in the 2NR, except to answer new things from the 1AR.
