T "substantial" and "its" limits

8 min read · Intermediate

Some words in a resolution, like "substantially" and "its," limit which plans count, and the negative argues the plan does not fit.

  • "Substantially": Many resolutions use this word. It sets a size limit: the plan must do enough to count, so the negative argues a small plan, such as a test program in a few cities, is too small to be topical.
  • "Its": This word says the thing being changed must belong to the government. The negative argues that a plan that changes someone else's program does not fit.
  • When it comes up: Almost every year, whatever the topic.

The made-up resolution

This page uses one made-up resolution: "The federal government should substantially increase its support for X." Two words in it set limits that the neg often tests.

  • "Substantially" is a size word: how big must the change be?
  • "Its" is an ownership word: whose support must change?

Both are topicality (T) arguments. T asks whether the plan fits the resolution. It has four parts: interpretation, violation, standards and voters. See Topicality.

T "substantial"

The interpretations

Size words are hard to define, because they have no single meaning. "Substantial," "significant" and "major" all mean something like "big," but how big? Neg interpretations usually take one of three forms.

Kind of interpretationExample (generic)Its strengthIts weakness
A number"At least a 10% increase"A clear lineSources disagree on the number
A plain meaning"Large in size, not a test or pilot"Close to common useThe line is less exact
A legal or field meaning"Without material qualification"Comes from a careful sourceMay fit a different context

A clear line is often called a bright line: the judge can tell for sure whether a plan meets it. Number interpretations give one, but the aff often says the number is arbitrary: why 10% and not 8%?

The violation

The violation says the plan is too small. Common shapes:

  • The plan is a pilot program: a test in a few places.
  • The plan is a small increase: a few percent more funding.
  • The plan affects one narrow part of the topic area.

Cross-examination (CX) is the best place to set up the violation. Ask: "How much does the plan increase support? Compared to what?"

Compared to what?

Many "substantial" debates turn on the baseline: the amount the plan is measured against. The same plan can look big or small.

  • 15% more funding for one small program may be less than 1% of all federal support for X.
  • A change that is small at the start may be large once fully in place.
  • A small dollar amount may be a big change in what the government does.

Teams argue which baseline is fair. The neg often says the whole topic area is the right measure. The aff often says the program the plan changes is the right measure. Few definitions settle this, so it is often decided on the standards.

The we meet and counter-interpretation fights

We meet. The aff says the plan meets the neg's own interpretation. For a size word, this usually means evidence about the plan's size: dollars, percent, or how many people it reaches. A we meet often depends on the baseline fight. Most judges treat a we meet as yes or no.

Counter-interpretation (CI). The aff gives its own meaning, which the plan meets. Common aff CIs:

  • "Substantial means important in context." The aff then argues the field calls this a major change.
  • "Substantial means real, not imaginary." This sets a very low bar.
  • A lower number than the neg's.

Standards. The neg usually leads with limits: if small plans count, there are thousands of possible pilots and narrow changes, and the neg cannot prepare for them. The aff answers that the neg's meaning overlimits: only a few large bills meet it, so the topic gets stale. The aff also argues precision and predictability: no expert source gives one number, so a number is not predictable. See Standards.

Reasonability. Reasonability is common in these debates. It asks only whether the aff's meaning is good enough. The aff argues that a vague word cannot support a fine line, so a plan that is reasonably big should be topical. The neg answers that "good enough" is itself vague, and that competing interpretations (the judge picks the better of the two meanings) give a clearer test. See Voters.

"Its" and other ownership limits

The interpretation

The word "its" ties the support to the actor: the federal government. A common neg interpretation: "'Its' means belonging to or controlled by the federal government. The plan must change support the federal government owns or runs."

The violation

The neg argues the plan changes something the federal government does not own or control. Common shapes:

  • The plan gives money to another body, such as a state, a company or a group, which then controls the support.
  • The plan changes a program run jointly with other actors.
  • The plan asks another actor to provide the support.

The answers

  • We meet: the federal government funds and directs the support, so it is "its" support.
  • CI: "its" means "associated with," not "owned by." Any support the federal government provides counts.
  • Grammar: the aff argues the neg reads too much into a small word. The neg answers that every word in the resolution should limit something.
  • Standards: the neg argues limits and ground. The aff argues the neg's meaning cuts out normal ways the government acts, which overlimits.

Ownership debates often overlap with effects questions. If the plan only causes others to give support, the neg may also run effects T. See Effects and extra topicality.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic T "substantially" debate. The plan funds a pilot program in five cities. The block and 1AR are left out. Each block is one part of T, with each speech's line on it, top to bottom.      cities.

     important in its context
     (Field Expert 21).
2NR: CI has no line: any pilot
     counts.

     calls this a major change.

     pilots.

2NR: (no answer)

     neg interp leaves 3 affs.

1NC: Fairness, education.
2AC: 4. Reasonability: core aff;

2AR: Reasonable: neg ran its core

How to read it: the block and 1AR are left out, so "(no answer)" means the 2NR made no answer to overlimits. The 2AR extended that drop, so the aff wins that the neg's meaning leaves very few affs. The judge decides the baseline first, then the test (competing interpretations or reasonability), then weighs dropped overlimits against the neg's limits claim.

How judges tend to decide

  • The baseline often decides the we meet. Judges look for the team that explained why its measure is the fair one.
  • Clear violations do better. Many judges find T "substantial" strong against plans that are plainly tiny, such as a test in one place. They find it weaker against plans that are only somewhat smaller than average.
  • Vague words help reasonability. Some judges think a vague word makes reasonability more persuasive. Others think competing interpretations still give the clearer test. Both views are common.
  • Ownership arguments turn on the plan text. Judges ask who funds, runs and controls the support, as the plan and evidence describe it.
  • Use the definitions read. Judges should not bring their own sense of "big" into the decision.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a size interpretation with no line. Fix: give a number or a clear test, so the judge can check the plan against it.
  • Neg: no baseline. Fix: say what the plan should be measured against, and why.
  • Aff: a we meet with no numbers. Fix: read evidence on the plan's size, and defend your baseline.
  • Aff: a counter-interpretation the plan does not meet. Fix: check your CI against your plan text before the round.
  • Neg: an "its" argument the plan text plainly meets. Fix: read who funds and runs the support before you run it.
  • Judges: deciding "substantial" by your own sense of size. Fix: use the interpretations, baselines and test the teams argued.
