Topicality · 8 min read · advanced

Effects and extra topicality (FX T and XT)

Plain version. Topicality, or T, asks whether the plan fits the resolution, the topic sentence for the season. Two special kinds of T come up often. Effects topicality says the plan does not do what the resolution asks. It only causes it later, through a chain of results. Extra topicality says the plan does what the resolution asks, and then does something more that is outside the topic. Teams argue about both, and they also argue about what the judge should do if the negative wins.

Two special kinds of violation

Every T argument has four parts: interpretation, violation, standards and voters. See Topicality. The interpretation says what a word means. The violation says how the plan falls outside that meaning. See Interpretation and violation.

Most violations say the plan does not do what a word requires. Effects T and extra T are different shapes of violation.

A made-up resolution helps. Say it reads: "The federal government should substantially increase its support for X."

Effects topicality

What it says. An effects violation says the plan's action is not itself support for X. The plan does something else, and that something else leads to more support for X later. For example, the plan changes a rule in area Y. The aff's evidence says that change will, in time, bring more support for X. The neg argues that the plan is topical only through its effects.

The interpretation. The neg's rule is usually: "The plan's mandates must be topical. The plan text itself must increase support for X, not cause an increase later." Mandates are the actions that the plan text orders.

The standards. Common neg reasons:

  • Limits: almost any action has some effect on X if the chain is long enough. If effects count, the topic has no edge.
  • Ground: the neg's core topic arguments are about direct support for X. An effects aff can avoid them.
  • Predictability: the neg prepares for plans that do X, not for every plan that might cause X.
  • Mixing burdens: if the plan is topical only because it works, then T depends on solvency. The neg says T should be settled first, by looking at the plan text, not at the end of the case debate.

How the aff answers.

  • We meet: the plan text itself increases support for X. Point to the words of the plan.
  • Counter-interpretation: effects count when the link is direct and supported by evidence, or when the field treats this action as support for X.
  • Every plan has effects: all plans work through some mechanism. The neg's line between "direct" and "effect" is arbitrary.
  • Reasonability: the plan is close to the core of the topic, so the judge should not vote on a fine line. See Voters.

How judges tend to see it. Many judges look hard at the plan text. If the topical action is in the text, they see no effects problem. If the aff must explain a long chain to show it is topical, many judges find effects T persuasive. Judges differ on where the line falls, so the line itself is what teams argue about.

Extra topicality

What it says. An extra topicality violation says the plan has a topical part and a part outside the topic. For example, the plan has two planks. Plank 1 increases support for X. Plank 2 raises a tax in area Z, which is not about X at all. The neg argues that plank 2 is extra topical.

Why it matters. The extra part often gives the aff an advantage it could not claim from the topical part alone. The neg says it could not prepare for that advantage. The extra part may also let the aff avoid the neg's core disadvantages.

The standards. Common neg reasons:

  • Limits: if the aff may add anything to a topical plank, the topic has no limit. Any idea can ride along.
  • Ground: the aff can claim advantages that come from outside the topic.
  • Predictability: the neg cannot prepare for whatever the aff chooses to attach.

How the aff answers.

  • We meet: the second plank is part of the topical action. For example, it pays for plank 1, or it carries plank 1 out. Many teams argue that funding and enforcement planks are normal parts of a plan, not extra. Others dispute this when the plank does more than pay or enforce.
  • No advantage from it: the aff claims no advantage from the extra part, so the neg lost nothing.
  • Sever it: the judge can simply drop the extra part. See the remedy section below.
  • Not a voter: extra T is a reason to cut the extra part, not a reason to vote neg.

The remedy debate

The remedy is the biggest fight in most extra T debates. A remedy is what the judge does after a team wins a theory or T argument. See Reasons to reject.

Effects T. Effects T is usually treated like any T argument. The topical part of the plan cannot be cut away from the rest, because the whole plan works only through its effects. So if the neg wins, the usual result is a neg ballot.

Extra T. Here teams argue about two main remedies.

  • Sever the extra part. The judge ignores the extra plank and any advantage that comes from it. The topical part stays, and the round goes on. Aff teams usually argue for this. They say it fixes the harm.
  • Vote neg. The judge treats extra T like T. Neg teams usually argue for this. They say severing in the middle of the round is itself unfair: the aff changes its plan, and the neg's links to the dropped part go away. They also say that if the worst result is losing one plank, the aff has no reason not to add extras.

Many judges lean toward cutting the extra part and its advantages when the aff offers that, and the neg did not lose much. Others treat extra T like T, especially when the neg shows that severing hurt its strategy. This varies a lot. Teams should argue it, and judges should decide it from what is argued.

How to judge effects and extra T

  1. Read the plan text. Most of these debates turn on what the plan says it does.
  2. Check the we meet. If the plan text does the topical action (effects T), or the second plank is part of that action (extra T), the violation fails.
  3. Compare the interpretations through the standards argued, using the test the teams argued: competing interpretations or reasonability.
  4. Decide the remedy. For extra T, ask which remedy was argued and won. If the extra part is cut, also cut the advantages that came from it.

How to read it: The block and 1AR are left out. The 1NC said plank 2 is a new tax outside the topic, that allowing add-ons removes all limits, and that the judge should vote neg. The 2AC made three answers: plank 2 only pays for plank 1 (a we meet), the judge can sever plank 2 and its advantage, and extra T is not a voter. The 2NR said plank 2 is a new tax policy, that severing is unfair because the neg loses its tax DA link, and that extra T lets the aff add anything. The 2AR said funding planks are normal, that severing fixes the harm because the aff gives up the tax advantage, and that the judge should strike plank 2, not vote neg. Every row was answered. The judge first decides the we meet. If plank 2 is extra, the judge decides between severing it and voting neg.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: running effects T against a plan whose text does the topical action. Fix: read the plan text first. Effects T needs a plan whose action is not itself topical.
  • Neg: no reason why extra T is a voter. Fix: explain why cutting the extra part does not fix the harm, for example the links you lose.
  • Aff: claiming advantages from the extra part, then offering to sever it. Fix: if you may sever, be ready to win without those advantages.
  • Aff: "every plan has effects" with no counter-interpretation. Fix: give a line for when effects count, and show the plan meets it.
  • Both: forgetting the remedy. Fix: say what the judge should do if the violation is won.
  • Judges: treating extra T as automatic. Fix: decide the we meet and the remedy as argued.
