Theory

7 min read · Intermediate

A theory argument is about the rules of the game, not about whether the plan is good or bad.

  • What it says: The other team did something unfair, or something that makes debate worse.
  • An example: The negative ran too many options and dropped them later.
  • What the judge can do: If the judge agrees, the judge may ignore that argument. In some cases, the judge may vote against the team that made it.

What a theory argument is

Policy debate has very few written rules. Speech times and prep time are fixed. Almost everything else is decided by argument in the round. That includes what a counterplan (the neg's other plan) may do, and how many options the neg may run. Theory is how teams make those arguments.

Topicality is a kind of theory argument: it is about what the aff is allowed to defend. This page covers the other kinds. See Topicality.

The parts

A theory argument has the same shape as topicality. In the table below, a conditional option is one the neg can drop later (see the next section). Clash means the teams directly answer each other.

PartWhat it saysExample (generic)
InterpretationThe rule the team thinks should apply"The neg may run at most one conditional option."
ViolationHow the other team broke it"The neg ran three conditional counterplans."
StandardsWhy the rule is good for debate"Too many options splits the 2AC's time; it hurts clash."
VoterWhat the judge should do about it"Reject the team." or "Reject the argument."

In the 2AC, theory is often very short: a few lines. If a team wants to win on it, it must spend real time on it in later speeches. A two-line theory argument rarely wins by itself unless it is dropped.

Conditionality: the main example

Conditionality ("condo") means the neg can run an argument and drop it later without penalty. The argument is often a counterplan or a kritik (an argument that questions the ideas behind the plan). If the neg drops it, the judge compares the aff's plan with the status quo (the world as it is now) instead.

This is the most common theory debate. Both sides have serious arguments.

Arguments that condo is good (usually the neg's):

  • The neg needs to test the aff from more than one angle. Real decision-makers consider several options.
  • The aff picks the plan and has prepared it for months. Options help balance that.
  • Choosing which argument to go for in the 2NR is a key skill.

Arguments that condo is bad (usually the aff's):

  • Many options split the aff's time, so each answer is thinner.
  • The neg can make contradicting arguments (for example, a kritik of state action and a counterplan where the state acts).
  • It rewards running many arguments over going deep on a few.

Many judges accept some conditionality (often one or two options) and grow skeptical as the number rises. Others accept any number. Others reject it. Read the judge's paradigm. See Conditionality.

Reject the argument vs reject the team

This is the voter question: if a team wins that something was unfair, what should happen? See Reasons to reject: the argument or the team.

  • Reject the argument: the judge ignores the unfair argument. For example, the judge ignores a counterplan that was found unfair, and decides the rest of the round normally.
  • Reject the team: the judge votes against the team that made the unfair argument.

Most judges default to reject the argument for most theory arguments. The reasoning: ignoring the argument fixes the harm. Many judges make an exception for conditionality, because the harm (a split 2AC) has already happened and cannot be fixed by ignoring the counterplan. Teams should argue which applies, and why.

Other common theory arguments

These come up every season. Each is covered on Common theory arguments.

  • Plan-inclusive counterplans (PICs) bad: the neg should not be allowed to do almost all of the plan.
  • Multiple-actor or "fifty states" fiat: can the neg fiat (imagine passed) many governments acting together? See Fiat.
  • International fiat: can the neg fiat another country?
  • Severance and intrinsic perms: limits on what the aff's permutation (perm) can include. A perm tests a counterplan by asking whether the plan and the counterplan could both be done. See Permutations.

How to answer theory

  • Counter-interpretation: offer a different rule, which you meet. For example, "The neg may run two conditional options."
  • Answer the standards: explain why your practice does not cause the harm, or why it has benefits.
  • Reject the argument, not the team: even if you lose the theory, the judge should only ignore the argument.
  • Do not ignore it. A dropped theory argument can decide the round.

How to judge theory

  1. Was it extended? Theory must be in the last rebuttal of the team that wants it. Many judges also expect it to be developed beyond one line.
  2. Who won the interpretation? Compare the interpretation and counter-interpretation, using the standards argued.
  3. What is the remedy? If the theory is won, decide whether to reject the argument or the team, as argued.
  4. Use your paradigm only to break ties. If you have a strong view (for example, on conditionality), say so in your paradigm before the round, and still decide the debate that happened.

Example in flow shorthand

A conditionality debate. The neg ran two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional. The block is the neg's two speeches in a row (the 2NC and 1NR).

Key: "interp" = interpretation, "CI" = counter-interpretation, "Ext" = extend, "flex" = flexibility, "K" = kritik, "CP" = counterplan, "arg" = argument, "perms" = permutations.

Condo bad

-- Interpretation --
2AC    Interp: neg gets 1 condo
       option
Block  CI: neg gets 3 condo
       options
1AR    Ext interp: 3 options
       split the 2AC
2NR    Testing the aff from 3
       angles is key
2AR    3 options split the 2AC 3
       ways; perms got little
       time

-- Violation --
2AC    Violation: 3 condo options
Block  Neg flex: aff picks plan;
       we need options
1AR    Contradictions: K says
       state bad, CP uses state
2NR    2AC answered all three; no
       real harm
2AR    Contradiction forced aff to
       answer both sides

-- Voter --
2AC    Voter: reject the team
Block  Reject the arg, not the
       team
1AR    Reject team: harm is done
2NR    Reject the arg: ignore the
       CPs
2AR    Ignoring the CPs can't undo
       the split 2AC

How to read it: both teams developed the theory past one line, so the judge should decide it. The key questions are whether three options is too many, and whether the remedy is to ignore the counterplans or vote aff. A good RFD (reason for decision) answers both.

Common mistakes

  • Two-line theory, then "they dropped it!" Fix: if you plan to win on theory, develop it in the block or 1AR, with real reasons.
  • No counter-interpretation. Fix: give a rule you meet. Without one, the judge has only the other team's rule.
  • Forgetting the remedy. Fix: say whether the judge should reject the argument or the team, and why.
  • Theory in the 2AR that was not in the 1AR. Fix: the 1AR must extend it, or most judges will treat it as new.
  • Judges: voting on theory they agree with but no one extended. Fix: decide only what the teams argued in the final speeches.

Find the rule

The aff reads a theory argument in the 2AC. It has four sentences.

(1) "The neg ran two conditional counterplans." (2) "The neg may run at most one conditional option." (3) "Two options split our 2AC's time." (4) "Reject the team."

Which sentence is the interpretation?

  • Sentence 1
  • Sentence 2
  • Sentence 3
  • Sentence 4
Show the answer

Answer: Sentence 2

Why: The interpretation is the rule the team thinks should apply. Sentence 2 states that rule. Sentence 1 is the violation: how the neg broke the rule. Sentence 3 is a standard: why the rule is good for debate. Sentence 4 is the voter: what the judge should do.

Find the violation

The aff argues against a counterplan (the neg's other plan). "Fiat" means we imagine a policy is passed.

(1) "The neg may fiat only one government." (2) "Their counterplan fiats fifty state governments acting together." (3) "That lets the neg avoid hard questions, so it hurts clash." (4) "Reject the counterplan."

Which sentence is the violation?

  • Sentence 1
  • Sentence 2
  • Sentence 3
  • Sentence 4
Show the answer

Answer: Sentence 2

Why: The violation says how the other team broke the rule. The rule (sentence 1) allows one government. The counterplan uses fifty, so sentence 2 shows the break. Sentence 3 is a standard, and sentence 4 is the voter.

Find the standards

You flow this theory argument from the 2AC.

2AC  Condo bad
     Interp: neg gets 1 condo
       option
     Violation: they ran 3
     Splits 2AC time
     Hurts clash
     Voter: reject the team

In words: the 2AC says the neg gets one conditional option, and the neg ran three. It says this splits the 2AC's time and hurts clash. It asks the judge to reject the team.

Which lines are standards? Pick all that apply.

  • "Neg gets 1 condo option"
  • "They ran 3"
  • "Splits 2AC time"
  • "Hurts clash"
  • "Reject the team"
Show the answer

Answer: "Splits 2AC time"; "Hurts clash"

Why: Standards say why the rule is good for debate. "Splits 2AC time" and "hurts clash" are both reasons of that kind. "Neg gets 1 condo option" is the interpretation, "they ran 3" is the violation, and "reject the team" is the voter.

What is missing?

The 2AC says: "The neg may run one conditional option. They ran three. Many options split our time and make each answer thinner."

Which part of a theory argument is missing?

  • The interpretation
  • The violation
  • The standards
  • The voter
Show the answer

Answer: The voter

Why: The 2AC gives a rule, says how the neg broke it, and says why the rule is good. It never says what the judge should do about it, such as "reject the argument" or "reject the team." That part is the voter. Without it, the judge uses a default; most judges then reject only the argument, not the team.
