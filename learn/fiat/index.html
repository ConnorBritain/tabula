Fiat

6 min read · Beginner

Fiat is a shortcut that lets the affirmative say, "Imagine the plan passes."

  • Why it matters: The round is about whether the plan should pass, not whether it would. This keeps the debate on the good and bad of the idea.
  • What it rules out: "Congress would never vote for that" is not a fair answer to a plan.
  • Where the word comes from: The Latin for "let it be done."

Why fiat exists

The resolution (the topic sentence for the season) says the government should do something. "Should" asks whether it is a good idea. If debates were about whether a plan would pass, every round would be a guess about politics. Most good ideas would lose, because they are unlikely today. Fiat removes that question, so the teams can debate the policy itself.

What fiat lets you assume

  • The plan passes. The aff (affirmative) does not have to prove that lawmakers would vote for it.
  • The plan is put into effect as written. The agent in the plan (the part of government that acts) does what the plan says.
  • The counterplan passes too. A counterplan is the neg's (negative's) other plan. The neg can use fiat for it, within limits argued in the round.

What fiat does not let you assume

  • That the plan works. Fiat passes the plan. It does not make the plan solve the problem. The aff still must prove solvency: that the plan works.
  • That there are no side effects. Fiat does not stop disadvantages (DAs): arguments that the plan causes harm. If the plan costs money or angers someone, that still counts.
  • That other people act. The aff fiats its own agent's action. It cannot fiat that other actors will cooperate, or that people will change their minds.
  • Anything outside the plan text. The aff gets what its plan says, not extra steps it adds later.

A common way to say it: fiat gets the plan passed; it does not make the plan perfect.

Implementation vs circumvention

Fiat passes the plan and puts it in effect. The agency named in the plan follows the order. But two kinds of argument are still open:

  • Implementation: the plan is in effect, but it is done badly, slowly or with too little money. That is a solvency argument.
  • Circumvention: someone works around the plan. Officials stall, people find loopholes, or a later government undoes it. Teams argue whether fiat covers this. Many judges say fiat covers the agent's first action, but not every later choice by other people.

Neither one is "it would never pass." Both ask whether the plan will work.

"It would never pass"

Under fiat, most judges will not count this answer. Some critical teams (teams that run kritiks, which question the ideas behind the plan) argue that fiat is imaginary. Some judges and teams prefer real-world prediction. Read the judge's paradigm: their written note on how they decide rounds.

Under the usual view, better neg arguments are:

  • the plan would not work (solvency);
  • passing it would cause harm (a disadvantage);
  • there is a better option (a counterplan).

A related idea. Some disadvantages are about the process of passing the plan. One example is the politics DA: the plan costs a leader political capital (the goodwill a leader needs to pass other bills). Whether fiat allows these is itself debated. Many judges accept them. Some judges think fiat means the plan passes with no political fight. See The politics DA.

Limits of fiat

Teams often argue about how far fiat can go. These are theory debates: arguments about the rules of debate, decided in the round. See Theory and Common theory arguments.

  • Multiple actors: can a counterplan fiat fifty state governments acting together?
  • International actors: can the neg fiat another country's government?
  • Private actors: can either team fiat a company or a person?
  • Object fiat: can the aff fiat the thing the plan acts on to behave a certain way?

There is no single rule. The common argument for limits: unlimited fiat makes debates unfair, because one team can imagine anything. The common argument against tight limits: testing a policy against real alternatives is educational.

How to judge fiat questions

  1. Ignore "it would never pass" unless the teams argued otherwise.
  2. Check the plan text. Fiat gives the aff what the plan says. If the aff later claims steps the plan does not include, the neg can say they are not fiated.
  3. Decide fiat limits as theory. If a team argues the other side's fiat is unfair, judge it like any theory argument. Ask what rule each side wants (the interpretation), why that rule is better (the standards), and what to do about it (the remedy).

Example in flow shorthand

Each group follows one argument through the speeches.

Key: "fed gov" = federal government, "Solv" = solvency, "Ext" = extend. "(Expert 22)" is an invented card (a piece of evidence) from a made-up source.

-- "Won't pass" --
1AC  Plan: fed gov funds
     program X
1NC  1. Congress won't pass it -
     too costly
2AC  Fiat: plan passes; "won't
     pass" is not a reason to
     vote neg
2NR  (no answer)
2AR  Fiat answers "won't pass";
     they dropped it

-- Solvency --
1AC  Solv: X cuts problem
     (Expert 22)
1NC  2. Solv: agencies won't
     carry it out
2AC  Plan text includes "and
     direct agencies"
2NR  Ext: plan funds but staff
     will stall - circumvention
2AR  Plan says "fund and direct"
     - the order is fiated

How to read it: the 1NC's first answer was a "would not pass" argument. The 2AC answered it with fiat, and the 2NR did not answer back, so it is gone. The second answer is fair: it is a circumvention argument. The 2AR replies that the plan text directs the agencies, so the agencies' first step is fiated. The judge counts that order as done. Then the judge decides whether the neg proved that staff would still stall after the order. If not, the aff keeps its solvency.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: "it would never pass." Fix: argue the plan would not work or would cause harm.
  • Aff: using fiat to dodge solvency or DAs. Fix: fiat passes the plan. It does not make it work or stop side effects.
  • Aff: fiating steps that are not in the plan text. Fix: write the plan text to include what you need.
  • Neg: assuming any counterplan actor is fine. Fix: be ready to defend your fiat in a theory debate.
  • Judges: treating fiat as a magic wand. Fix: give each team only what its text says.

"They would never pass it"

The aff's plan has the government pay for free school meals. The neg says: "Congress would never vote for this. It costs too much."

How do most judges treat this answer?

  • As a strong solvency answer, because the plan cannot work if it never passes
  • As an answer fiat sets aside, because the round is about whether the plan should pass
  • As a disadvantage, because it is about money
  • As a reason to stop the round
Show the answer

Answer: As an answer fiat sets aside, because the round is about whether the plan should pass

Why: Fiat lets the aff say, "Imagine the plan passes." So "it would never pass" is not a reason to vote neg for most judges. Some teams argue that fiat is imaginary, and some judges prefer real-world prediction, so read the judge's paradigm. A better neg answer is that the plan would not work, or that it would cause harm.

Does fiat answer this?

The same plan pays for free school meals. The neg says: "Even after the plan passes, schools have no kitchen staff. Most students will still get no meals."

What kind of answer is this?

  • A "would never pass" answer, which fiat sets aside
  • A solvency answer, which fiat does not set aside
  • A counterplan
  • Not an argument, because the plan is fiated
Show the answer

Answer: A solvency answer, which fiat does not set aside

Why: This answer accepts that the plan passes. It says the plan will not work. That is a solvency argument. Fiat gets the plan passed, but it does not make the plan work. The aff still must prove solvency.

The aff's reply

The neg says the plan will not work because the program has too little money. The aff answers: "Fiat means our plan works."

Is the aff's answer right?

  • Yes, because fiat means the plan does what the aff says
  • No, because fiat passes the plan but does not prove it works
  • Yes, but only if the aff spoke last
  • No, because the aff cannot use fiat at all
Show the answer

Answer: No, because fiat passes the plan but does not prove it works

Why: Fiat gets the plan passed. It does not make the plan perfect. "Too little money" is a solvency argument about whether the plan works. The aff must answer it with reasons or evidence, for example that the plan text gives enough money.

Which answers still count?

A plan pays for new bus routes. The neg makes four answers.

Which answers are still fair under fiat? Pick all that apply.

  • "The buses will run empty, so the plan solves nothing."
  • "The plan costs money that would go to roads, so roads get worse."
  • "The city council would vote the plan down."
  • "The buses break down a lot, so the plan works badly."
Show the answer

Answer: "The buses will run empty, so the plan solves nothing."; "The plan costs money that would go to roads, so roads get worse."; "The buses break down a lot, so the plan works badly."

Why: Fiat only sets aside the question of whether the plan would pass. The council vote is that question, so most judges do not count it. "Runs empty" and "breaks down" say the plan would not work well. Those are solvency answers. "Roads get worse" says the plan causes harm. That is a disadvantage. Fiat does not stop either kind.
