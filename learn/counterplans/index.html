Counterplans (CP)

6 min read · Beginner

A counterplan, or CP, is the negative's own plan, and the negative says: "There is a better way than your plan."

  • What it must show: The counterplan must be better than the plan, and it must be a real choice: the judge should not be able to just do both.
  • The net benefit: Most counterplans fix the same problem as the plan but avoid a harm the plan causes. That harm is called the net benefit.

The parts

The aff (affirmative) defends the plan. The neg (negative) reads the counterplan. A counterplan has four parts.

PartThe question it answers
TextWhat exactly does the counterplan do?
SolvencyDoes it solve the aff's problem, or most of it?
CompetitionWhy is it a choice instead of the plan, not a plan to do with it?
Net benefitWhat is better about the counterplan than the plan?

Text

The counterplan text is one sentence, like a plan text. It names who acts and what they do. For example: "The fifty states should each fund program X." The neg must defend this text all round, just as the aff defends its plan. See Counterplan text.

Like the plan, the counterplan uses fiat: for the debate, we imagine it is passed and carried out. See Fiat.

Solvency

Solvency means how well a policy fixes the problem. The counterplan should solve the aff's harms (the problems the plan fixes), or most of them. The neg often reads a card (a short quote from evidence) that says the counterplan's actor or action can do what the plan does.

If the counterplan solves less than the plan, the gap is called a solvency deficit. The aff will point it out. A small deficit can still be fine for the neg, if the net benefit is bigger. See Solvency deficits.

Competition

A counterplan must compete: the judge must have a reason to choose it over the plan, not just add it to the plan. There are two main ways to compete.

  • Mutual exclusivity: the plan and the counterplan cannot both happen. (This is rare. Most actions can be done together.)
  • Net benefits: doing the counterplan alone is better than doing both. This is the most common way. See Competition.

Net benefit

The net benefit is the reason the counterplan alone beats the plan, or the plan plus the counterplan. Most often it is a disadvantage (DA): a bad result that the plan causes and the counterplan avoids. For example: "The plan costs federal money and forces cuts. The states counterplan does not." Sometimes the net benefit is built into the counterplan itself. See Net benefits.

How to answer a counterplan (for the aff)

The 2AC (the aff's speech right after the neg reads the counterplan) usually makes several kinds of answers.

  • Permutation ("perm"): a test of competition. The aff says, "Do both: the plan and the parts of the counterplan that are not the plan." If doing both is possible and avoids the net benefit, the counterplan does not compete. It is not a reason to reject the plan. See Permutations.
  • Solvency deficit: the counterplan cannot solve all of the aff's harms. Explain what it misses and why that part matters.
  • Answer the net benefit: if the DA is not true, the counterplan has no advantage over the plan. Use the DA answers from Disadvantages.
  • Counterplan disadvantages: the counterplan causes its own harm.
  • Theory: an argument about the fair rules of debate. Some counterplans are argued to be unfair. A common question is whether the neg may drop the counterplan later in the round. That is called conditionality. See Conditionality and Theory.

How to judge a counterplan

  1. Does it compete? If the aff's perm is legitimate and avoids the net benefit, the counterplan is not a reason to vote neg.
  2. Does it solve? How much of the aff's advantage (the good the plan does) does the counterplan get?
  3. Compare. Weigh the solvency deficit (what the counterplan misses) against the net benefit (what the plan causes and the counterplan avoids). If the net benefit is bigger, vote neg. If the deficit is bigger, vote aff.
  4. Check the theory. If the aff argued the counterplan is unfair, decide that first, as argued.

Why a small DA can win with a counterplan. If the counterplan solves the whole advantage, the aff's case no longer outweighs anything. Then even a small risk of the net benefit can be the deciding factor.

Common counterplan shapes

These come back every season.

  • Agent counterplans: a different actor does the plan. The classic example is the states counterplan: the fifty states act instead of the federal government. See The states counterplan.
  • Advantage counterplans: a different action that solves the aff's advantages.
  • Plan-inclusive counterplans (PICs): do most of the plan, but leave out one part. See Plan-inclusive counterplans.
  • Process counterplans: do the plan, but by a different process (for example, after consulting another body). These are often debated on theory.

For these and the consult counterplan, see Common counterplan shapes.

Example in flow shorthand

A generic states counterplan, with a spending DA as the net benefit. Each group follows one argument through the round. Key: "->" means "leads to", "$" means money, "fed" means federal, "Solv" means solvency, "NB" means net benefit, "Ext" means extend (repeat it in a later speech), and "Condo" means conditionality.

THE COUNTERPLAN
1NC: CP: 50 states fund
     program X

THE PERM
2AC: 1. Perm: do both
2NR: Perm links to DA: still
     uses fed $
2AR: Perm solves best; DA link
     is tiny either way

SOLVENCY
1NC: Solv: states can run X
     (Analyst 21)
2AC: 2. Solv deficit: states
     lack $; some opt out
2NR: States have run X before
     (Expert 20)
2AR: Only some states act;
     deficit is big

THE NET BENEFIT
1NC: NB: Spending DA. Fed cost
     -> cuts
2AC: 3. No link: plan's cost
     is small
2NR: Ext DA: fed cost forces
     cuts now
2AR: DA is a tiny risk;
     deficit > DA

THEORY
2AC: 4. Condo bad
2NR: (no answer)
2AR: (not extended)

How to read it: the round turns on two questions. Does the perm still trigger the DA? And which is bigger, the solvency deficit or the risk of the DA? The 2NR did not answer conditionality, but the 2AR did not extend it either, so the judge should not vote on it. The judge compares the deficit with the risk of the DA, then decides.

Common mistakes

  • Neg: a counterplan with no net benefit. Fix: always pair the counterplan with a reason it is better than doing both.
  • Neg: a counterplan text that does not match the solvency card. Fix: write the text from what your evidence says the actor can do.
  • Aff: a perm with no explanation. Fix: say what the perm does and why it avoids the net benefit.
  • Aff: claiming a solvency deficit without saying why it matters. Fix: name the part of the advantage the counterplan misses, and weigh it against the net benefit.
  • Both: forgetting the net benefit is a DA. Fix: answer and extend it like any DA.
  • Judges: voting neg because the counterplan "sounds better." Fix: check competition first, then weigh deficit against net benefit.

Does it compete?

The plan: the federal government pays for free school meals. The neg reads a counterplan (CP): the fifty states pay for free school meals. The net benefit is a spending DA (disadvantage): federal spending forces cuts to other programs.

Plan: fed $ for school meals
CP:   50 states $ for meals
NB:   fed $ -> cuts elsewhere

In words: the plan uses federal money for school meals. The counterplan uses state money. The net benefit says federal spending forces cuts.

Does this counterplan compete with the plan, and why?

  • No, because the plan and the counterplan can both happen
  • Yes, through net benefits: doing both still spends federal money, so the counterplan alone avoids the DA
  • Yes, because the states and the federal government cannot both act
  • No, because a counterplan must use the same actor as the plan
Show the answer

Answer: Yes, through net benefits: doing both still spends federal money, so the counterplan alone avoids the DA

Why: Both policies could happen at once, so they are not mutually exclusive. But "do both" still uses federal money and still causes the cuts. The counterplan alone avoids them. That makes the counterplan alone better than doing both, so it competes through net benefits, the most common way.

No net benefit

The plan: the city builds a new park. The neg reads a counterplan: the city builds a new park and also plants trees on Main Street. The neg reads no DA and gives no reason the plan is worse.

Does this counterplan compete with the plan?

  • Yes, because it does more than the plan
  • No, because the judge can do both, and nothing makes the counterplan alone better
  • Yes, because it solves the aff's harms
  • No, because a counterplan may not include the plan
Show the answer

Answer: No, because the judge can do both, and nothing makes the counterplan alone better

Why: A counterplan must be a real choice instead of the plan. Here the judge can build the park and plant the trees. With no net benefit, doing both is at least as good as the counterplan alone. So the counterplan is not a reason to reject the plan.

Cannot both happen

The plan: the town keeps its old bridge open and repairs it. The neg's counterplan: the town tears down the old bridge and builds a new one in the same place.

How does this counterplan compete?

  • Mutual exclusivity: the plan and the counterplan cannot both happen
  • Net benefits: it avoids a DA the plan causes
  • It does not compete, because both fix the bridge
  • It competes only if the aff makes a perm
Show the answer

Answer: Mutual exclusivity: the plan and the counterplan cannot both happen

Why: You cannot keep a bridge open and tear it down at the same time. When the two policies cannot both happen, they are mutually exclusive. This is rare, because most actions can be done together.

Judging a counterplan

You judge a round with a counterplan. The aff made a perm (permutation): "do both the plan and the counterplan." The counterplan sounds like a better idea to you.

What should you decide first?

  • Vote neg, because the counterplan sounds better
  • Whether the counterplan competes: is the perm legitimate, and does it avoid the net benefit?
  • Whether the counterplan solves more than the plan
  • Which team read more cards on the counterplan
Show the answer

Answer: Whether the counterplan competes: is the perm legitimate, and does it avoid the net benefit?

Why: Check competition first. If the perm is legitimate and avoids the net benefit, the counterplan is not a reason to vote neg. Only if the counterplan competes do you weigh the solvency deficit against the net benefit. "It sounds better" is not a reason to vote.
