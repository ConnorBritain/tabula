# Marketing anti-patterns: AI tells

This file is the baseline checklist for the redraft of three sites:
tabulaspeech.com, tabuladebate.com and tabulaforensics.com.
Use it before you write a page and again before you ship it.

Research date: November 2026. Sources are numbered, for example [3].
The list is in section 6. We did not invent quotes or numbers.
Where a source gives a number, we name the source next to it.

## 1. What a tell is, and why it matters

A tell is a pattern in words or layout that readers link to a machine or a template.
One tell does not prove that a machine wrote a page.
Wikipedia's editors say this clearly: look for many signs together, not one [1].
People also used these patterns before chatbots. Models learned them from us [1][2].

So the goal is not "hide the AI".
The goal is a page that says real, specific things in a real voice.
Most tells are a symptom of a page that says nothing specific.

### Why this matters for Tabula

- **Parent judges** decide in seconds if a tool is serious. A page that looks like
  every startup template looks like it can disappear next month.
- **Coaches** send our link to other parents. They put their name behind it.
  Generic hype makes that harder.
- **School IT** reads for facts: what runs, what is stored, where data goes.
  Hype words slow them down and lower trust.
- **Debaters** know how to spot weak claims. That is their sport.

Evidence that plain words help:

- In a 1997 Nielsen Norman study, an objective version of a site scored 27% better
  on usability than a promotional version. Concise text scored 58% better.
  All changes together scored 124% better [10].
- Pangram counted signs in millions of documents. AI text had about 10 times more
  em dashes, 4 times more groups of three, and 3 times more "not only X but also Y"
  than human text. Bold text inside markdown was about 43 times more common [2].
- Since October 2024, a US FTC rule bans fake reviews and testimonials, AI-made ones
  included [12]. Invented social proof is now a legal risk, not only a style risk.

### What good human-made sites do instead

- They make specific claims with units: "8-minute speech clock", not "Fast".
- They show the real product, cropped to the part that matters [20].
- They have one voice. It sounds like one person who knows the field.
- They vary the layout to fit the content. Not every idea gets a card [5].
- They use concrete numbers only with context: "79 free pages on how to judge".
- They write short paragraphs. Each paragraph says one real thing.
- They name what the button does: "Open Tabula Debate", not "Get started" [6].

## 2. Copy tells

| Tell | Example | Why it reads as AI | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fragment chains with middle dots | "Free · No account · Saved on your device · No ads" | It is a list of tags, not a sentence. Templates use it to fill space under a button. No verb, so no claim. | Write one sentence with a verb: "It is free, and you do not need an account." Move the rest to the section that proves it. |
| Middle-dot brand eyebrow | "A Tabula product · for policy debate" | It labels the page instead of telling the reader anything. It repeats the logo. | Delete it. If the format matters, put it in the headline: "Flow and judge policy debate rounds." |
| Odd literal phrase | "It works with Wi-Fi off." | It describes a test condition, not a benefit. It is also not always true: AI features need a connection. | Say the situation the judge is in: "No Wi-Fi in the judging room? The flow, clock and ballot still work." Keep the limit honest. |
| Clipped two-sentence slogan | "Sign in once. Use every product." | Two short imperative sentences with the same shape. It is a stock tagline rhythm with no fact in it. | Say the fact once, in one sentence: "One account works in Tabula Debate and Tabula Speech Events." |
| "Your X. Your Y." pair | "Your choice. Your judgment." | A possessive pair is a common slogan template. It sounds warm but says nothing. | State the rule: "AI is off until you turn it on. The judge makes the decision." |
| Negative parallelism | "Judge the round, not the paperwork." "Learning, not leaderboards." | "Not X but Y" is about 3 times more common in AI text [2]. Wikipedia lists it as a sign [1]. Impeccable calls it forced contrast [5]. | Use it once per site at most. Elsewhere, state the positive claim directly. |
| Rule of three | "Flow, time and decide the round." "Free to use, open source, and your rounds stay on your device." | Groups of three are about 4 times more common in AI text [2]. Many triads in a row sound generated [1]. | Use two items, or four, or one. Pick the item that matters most for this reader. |
| Triplet adjectives | "Fast, simple, secure." "A calm, clear, kind room." | Three adjectives with no proof. The reader cannot check any of them. | Replace each adjective with a fact the reader can check, or cut it. |
| Em dash in every paragraph | "Tabula keeps you focused — so you can judge." | AI text has about 10 times more em dashes [2]. In one study, reader reaction to AI tells came mostly from the em dash [15]. | Use a full stop. Allow at most one em dash per page. |
| Hype verbs | "Unlock", "elevate", "empower", "supercharge", "unleash" | Overuse made these words empty [11]. They promise value but do not say what it is [5]. | Name the action: "Write the RFD next to your flow." |
| Hype adjectives | "Seamless", "effortless", "frictionless", "powerful", "cutting-edge" | Trade editors call "seamless" meaningless from overuse [19]. | Describe the step you removed: "No install. Open the link and start the clock." |
| "Built for X" | "Built for the keyboard." "Built for schools from the start." | A stock heading shape. It claims intent, not a result. | Show the result: "Mark a drop with one key." "School IT can force-install it on Chromebooks." |
| "Made for" / "Designed for" | "Made for the people who run the round." | Same template as "Built for X". | Name the people and the job: "Most judges are parent volunteers. Tabula helps them write a fair ballot." |
| Feature as headline | "Offline", "Keyboard first", "Open source" | A noun is not a benefit. The reader must guess why it matters. | Headline the outcome. Put the feature in the body as the reason. |
| Number with no unit or context | "79 pages" | Of what? For whom? A bare number looks like filler for a stat slot. | Add the noun and the reader: "79 free pages that teach a parent judge how a round works." |
| One-word stat headings | "Free", "Private", "Offline" in big type | It looks like a stat but has no number. Templates need four tiles, so writers fill them with words. | Remove the strip. Prove each fact in its own section, with detail. |
| Scene-setting opener | "In today's fast-paced world of competitive debate..." | A filler opener that delays the point. A classic chatbot habit. | Start with the job: "Tabula is a free app to flow and judge a debate round." |
| Inflated significance | "A pivotal tool that stands as a testament to fair judging" | Wikipedia flags "pivotal", "stands as", "underscores" as signs of AI [1]. | Say what it does. Let the reader decide if it is important. |
| "-ing" tails | "...ensuring every debater gets fair feedback." | Wikipedia calls these superficial analyses [1]. The tail adds a vague value claim. | End the sentence at the fact. If the value matters, prove it in a new sentence. |
| Vague social proof | "Trusted by coaches everywhere." "Judges love it." | Unnamed authorities are weasel words [1]. With no names, readers assume none exist. | Use a named quote with role and school, with consent. If you have none, say nothing. |
| Emphatic one-word sentence | "No ads. No tracking. Ever." | A dramatic beat from ad copy. It sounds like a promise a company cannot keep. | Give the rule and where to check it: "The app loads no ads and no tracking scripts. Read the code." |
| Question then answer | "No Wi-Fi in the room? It still works." | A stock hook. Used more than once, it sounds like a template. | Use it once per page at most, or write a plain statement. |
| Generic call to action | "Get started", "Your next round starts here", "Start your journey" | The button does not say what happens next [6]. | Name the result: "Open Tabula Debate", "Read the first-round guide". |
| Title Case Headings | "Built For The Keyboard" | Wikipedia lists title case in section headings as a sign [1]. | Use sentence case in all headings. |
| Bold label at start of each item | "**Keyboard first.** Move cells..." on every line | Inline-header lists are a sign [1]. Bold in markdown is about 43 times more common in AI text [2]. | Use bold rarely. Let the first words of a plain sentence carry the point. |
| Emoji and check-mark bullets | "✅ Free ✅ Offline ✅ Private" | AI text uses UI-style check marks and keycap numbers far more than people do [2]. | Use plain bullets or prose. Use a check mark only in a real comparison table. |
| Elegant variation | "judge", then "adjudicator", then "evaluator", then "user" | Swapping synonyms for one thing is a known sign [1]. It also confuses new parents. | Pick one word per thing and keep it. Our word is "judge". |
| Cheerful assistant voice | "Ready to judge like a pro? Let's dive in!" | Cheerful openers are about 2 times more common in AI text [2]. "Dive in" is chatbot vocabulary. | Write like a coach talks to a new parent: plain and calm. |
| Avoiding "is" | "Tabula serves as a flow tool." "The app boasts a timer." | Wikipedia lists "serves as", "boasts", "features" in place of "is" or "has" [1]. | Use "is" and "has". |
| Closing summary line | "In short, Tabula makes judging simple." | Section-ending summaries are a known sign [1]. They repeat what the reader just read. | Cut it. End the section on its last real fact. |
| Superlatives with no proof | "The best judging app", "world-class" | Promotional language scored worse than objective language [10]. | Remove it, or give the comparison and its source. |
| Alliterative slogan pair | "Rank fairly. Comment kindly." | Sound over meaning. Two slogans in a row make a page sound written by a template. | Say what the tool does: "Drag speakers to rank them. Prompts help you write useful comments." |
| Repeated "same" or "one" | "Same family, same account, same rules." "One account. One plan." | Anaphora (the same word at the start of each part) used for rhythm, not meaning. | Say it once, in one sentence, with the real detail. |

## 3. Design and layout tells

| Tell | Example | Why it reads as AI | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hard line break in a heading | `<h1>Judge the round,<br>not the paperwork.</h1>` | A fixed break looks right at one width only. At other widths it leaves a ragged or one-word line. | Remove `<br>` from headings. Use `text-wrap: balance` and a `max-inline-size` [7]. |
| Break that splits the thought | "Your choice. Your / judgment." | The break falls inside the second idea. The eye reads "Your" as a lost word. It looks cheap. | Rewrite to one idea per heading. Balance the lines. Check at 320, 768 and 1280 px. |
| Orphan on the last line | A paragraph ends with "it." alone on a line | A short last line makes text look unset. Templates never check it. | Use `text-wrap: pretty` on body text. It targets short last lines [8][9]. Edit the words if it still fails. |
| No line balance on headings | Two-line heading with 9 words on top and 1 below | The browser fills each line greedily [7]. | Apply `text-wrap: balance` to h1, h2, h3 and short quotes only. It works up to six lines [7]. |
| Eyebrow label on every heading | Small uppercase tracked text above every h2 | Several critics name this a top template tell [5][6]. It adds a line that often repeats the heading [5]. | Remove eyebrows. Keep at most one per page, where it adds a fact the heading does not have. |
| Pill badge above the hero | "New · Now with AI" pill over the h1 | It looks clickable and competes with the headline [5]. It is part of the stock hero [6]. | Delete it. Put real news in a dated line or a changelog. |
| Default font | Inter, Geist, Roboto or Space Grotesk on every word | Named as the generic AI default [3][4][5]. Unrelated products start to look the same. | Choose type for the product and reader. Set a clear type scale [6]. |
| Crushed tracking on big headings | Hero letter-spacing near -0.04em | Tight tracking makes letters hard to tell apart [5]. It is a template "premium" trick. | Loosen tracking. Test at the smallest size you ship. |
| Gradient text | Purple-to-blue fill on the h1 or a stat | Pure decoration [5]. Part of the "first wave" defaults [6]. | Use one solid colour. Get emphasis from size and weight. |
| Stock page order | Centred hero, pill, two buttons, logo row, three feature cards | The average of every SaaS page [3][6]. Readers know it before they read it. | Lead with the most specific thing about the product [6]. For us: a real flow sheet. |
| Symmetric three-card row | Three cards, each with an icon tile, a title, two lines | Equal cards give every point equal weight [5]. Icon tile above a title is a stock pattern [5]. | Group ideas by importance. Use a list, a table, or one large and two small items. |
| Four-square stat strip | Four boxes: "Free", "Open source", "79 pages", "Offline" | A stock hero-metric layout [5]. With words in place of numbers, it shows the slot came first and the content second. | Remove it. Put each fact in the section that proves it. Use a stat only with context [5]. |
| Bento grid | Mixed-size rounded tiles, each with one feature | A 2024-2025 template look. Tiles force every idea into the same box style. | Use bento only when the tiles are real views of the product. |
| Nested cards | Card inside a card inside a section panel | Adds depth and noise for no reason [5][6]. | Flatten. Use spacing, type and dividers. |
| Same gap everywhere | Every section has the same padding | Equal gaps hide what belongs together [5]. | Keep related items close. Put more space between groups. |
| Numbers with no sequence | "01", "02", "03" on sections that are not steps | Numbering implies order [5][6]. | Number only real steps, such as Flow, Decide, Ballot. |
| Arrow on every link | "Start →", "Learn more →", "Look up →" in a list | Template chrome [6]. Arrows stop meaning "go". | Use the arrow on one primary text link per section, or none. |
| Purple-to-blue gradient | Indigo gradient hero background | Traced to Tailwind's indigo demo colour, copied into training data [3]. Named the clichéd AI scheme [4]. | Build a palette of 4 to 6 named colours with roles [6]. Ours: Aff blue, Neg red, ink, paper. |
| Glow blobs and halos | Blurred radial blobs behind the hero | A glow adds emphasis without saying what matters [5]. | Remove. Direct attention with spacing, contrast and a clear heading [5]. |
| Glassmorphism | Blurred translucent panels on every surface | Decoration, not a fix for layering [5][6]. | Use blur only where content scrolls under a layer, such as a sticky header. |
| Dark page with neon accents | Near-black with one cyan or green glow | The default "technical" look [6]. | Keep dark mode as a theme. Do not add glow. |
| The new "tasteful" default | Cream background, serif display, terracotta or sage accent | The second-wave default after purple was banned [6][5]. | Choose colours from the product. Ours come from Aff and Neg flow colours. |
| Fake product UI | A dashboard with random charts that do not exist in the app | Readers sense it is not the product. It is filler art. | Use a real screenshot, or an exact HTML copy of the real screen with example data labelled. |
| Stock or "Corporate Memphis" art | Flat purple people with long limbs | Critics call it generic and bland [13]. It says nothing about debate. | Show a real flow, a real ballot, a real Chromebook. No people art. |
| AI-generated photos | A smiling "student at a podium" with odd hands | Parents and students spot fake people fast. It breaks trust at once. | Use no photos of people, or real photos with consent. |
| Logo wall | "Trusted by" row of school or company logos | A stock trust device [6]. Without permission it is also misleading. | Leave it out until named schools agree to be listed. |
| Fake testimonials | Quote with a stock headshot and "Sarah K., Coach" | Banned in the US when fake [12]. Easy to spot. | Use only real quotes with name, role, school and consent. |
| Fade-up on every section | Each block slides up on scroll | Motion everywhere is a first-wave tell [6]. | No scroll animation. Animate only in reply to a user action [6]. |
| Count-up stats | Numbers roll from 0 to 79 | Template motion for invented metrics [6]. | Show the number. Do not animate it. |
| Auto-scroll marquee | Logos or quotes slide across the screen | It sets the reading pace for the reader [5]. | Keep it still. |
| Pulsing dot or blinking cursor | A "live" dot on a static page | It signals activity that is not there [5]. | Animate only real state, such as a running clock in a real demo. |
| Hover lift on non-links | Cards rise and shadow on hover but do not click | It promises an action that does not exist. | Hover effects only on things that click. |
| Big radius and soft shadow on everything | 24 px corners and the same shadow on every box | The "card kit" look [5][6]. | Vary radius and elevation by role. Use rows or tables for tabular content [6]. |

## 4. Our draft's offenders

Files are in the site repo's page files. Quotes are exact.

### tabuladebate.com, `debate/pages/index.html`

1. Hero eyebrow "A Tabula product · for policy debate". Delete. Put "policy debate" in the h1.
2. Hero h1 "Judge the round,`<br>`not the paperwork." Remove the `<br>`.
   The "X, not Y" shape is our one allowed use per site, or rewrite as a plain job statement.
3. Hero line "It works with Wi-Fi off." Replace with the judge's real situation and the
   real limit: flow, clock and ballot work without a connection; AI features need one.
4. Fact line "Free · No account · Saved on your device · No ads". Replace with one sentence.
5. Steps "1 · Flow", "2 · Decide", "3 · Ballot". The order is real, so numbers can stay.
   Drop the middle dots. Make the three cards unequal: Flow is the core job.
6. Eyebrows "Flow and clock", "Ballot", "AI, if you want it", "Debate Library", "Formats",
   "For coaches". That is 9 eyebrows on one page. Remove all, or keep one.
7. h2 "Built for the keyboard." Replace with a result: what a judge can do with keys only.
8. List "Works offline. No Wi-Fi in the room? It still works." Repeats the hero claim and
   uses the question hook. Cut it here, or keep only the fact.
9. h2 "Your choice. Your judgment." Replace with the rule: AI is off until the judge turns it on.
   This heading also breaks badly at narrow widths.
10. Stat strip "Free / Offline / Private / Open source". Remove the `.facts` block.
    Each fact already has its own section.
11. Library line "{{libpages}} free pages on how policy debate works". Good: it has a noun.
    Keep it in prose. Never promote the bare number to a heading.
12. h2 "Policy today. LD and PF next." Clipped pair. Write: "Tabula supports policy debate now.
    Lincoln-Douglas comes next, then Public Forum."
13. Closing h2 "Your next round starts here." Generic. Use the button's job as the line.
14. Library list "Start →" five times, "Look up →". Remove the repeated arrows.
15. The HTML flow sheet with "Example round. Names and arguments are made up." Good label.
    Check that it matches the real app screen exactly, or replace with a real screenshot.

### tabulaspeech.com, `speech/pages/index.html`

16. Hero body "Flow, time and decide the round. Write a clear ballot. Learn as you go." Three short
    sentences, then a triad: "Free to use, open source, and your rounds stay on your device."
    Cut to two claims for the parent judge.
17. Stat strip "Free / Open source / {{libpages}} pages / Offline". The "79 pages" tile has no
    "of what". "Offline: Flow and clock work with Wi-Fi off" repeats the odd phrase. Remove the strip.
18. Product card "Works offline. LD and PF come next." Two fragments. Write one sentence.
19. Eyebrow "One account" and h2 "Sign in once. Use every product." Replace both with one plain sentence.
20. "One plan, every product." "Programs cover the whole team." Bold-label list pattern.
    Write normal sentences.
21. Eyebrow "Trust" and h2 "Built for schools from the start." Replace with a fact school IT can check.
22. Card "No ads. No tracking." with body ending "Ever." Remove "Ever.". Link to the code instead.
23. Four identical trust cards (`grid g4`). Use a short list or a two-column table: what we keep,
    what we never keep. The trust page already does this well.
24. Closing "Free. No account. Open it in your browser." Fragment chain. One sentence.

### tabulaforensics.com, `events/pages/index.html`

25. Eyebrow "A Tabula product · coming later". Delete. Say "coming later" in the body once.
26. Body "A calm way to time, rank and comment". Triad plus a mood adjective. Name the jobs.
27. h2 "Rank fairly. Comment kindly." Alliterative slogan pair. Replace with what the tool does.
28. "One email when it opens. Nothing else." Fine as a promise, but it is a fragment pair.
    Write: "We send one email when it opens, and no others."
29. "Same family, same account, same rules as Tabula Debate". Triple "same". One sentence.
30. Three equal cards for Rank board, Comment sheet, Congress grid. Show one real screen instead.

### Other pages

31. `speech/pages/plans.html`: "No card. No time limit." Fragment pair.
    "See rounds left, not confusing credits." Negative parallelism. Write the plain rule.
32. `speech/pages/plans.html`: "Cancel in one click." Keep only if it is true at launch.
33. `debate/pages/coaches.html`: h2 "Learning, not leaderboards." Second "X, not Y" on the site.
    Rewrite as the rule: "Coaches see done or not done. Tabula never ranks students."
34. `speech/pages/about.html`: h1 "Made for the people who run the round." "Made for X" template.
35. `debate/pages/school-it.html` and `speech/pages/trust.html` are our best pages.
    They name policies (`AudioCaptureAllowedUrls`), addresses and data stores. Use their voice everywhere.

### Before and after: sample rewrites

These are direction, not final copy. Check every fact against the app before you ship.

- Before: "A Tabula product · for policy debate / Judge the round, not the paperwork."
  After: "Flow and judge a policy debate round, then write a clear ballot."
- Before: "It works with Wi-Fi off."
  After: "No Wi-Fi in the judging room? The flow, the clocks and the ballot still work.
  AI drafts need a connection."
- Before: "Free · No account · Saved on your device · No ads"
  After: "It is free, and you do not need an account. Your rounds stay in your browser."
- Before: "Your choice. Your judgment."
  After: "AI is off until you turn it on. The judge makes the decision."
- Before: "Sign in once. Use every product."
  After: "One account works in Tabula Debate and, later, Tabula Speech Events."
- Before: stat tile "79 pages".
  After, in prose: "The Debate Library has 79 free pages for parent judges and new debaters."
- Before: "Built for schools from the start."
  After: "School IT can force-install Tabula on managed Chromebooks. It loads no third-party scripts."
- Before: "Rank fairly. Comment kindly."
  After: "Drag speakers into rank order. Short prompts help you write comments a speaker can use."
- Before: "Policy today. LD and PF next."
  After: "Tabula supports policy debate now. Lincoln-Douglas comes next, then Public Forum."

### Patterns we may keep, with care

- A question hook, once per page, when it names a real situation a judge is in.
- One "X, not Y" line on the whole site, if it is the clearest way to say the point.
- Numbered steps, where the steps are real (Flow, Decide, Ballot).
- One eyebrow per page, when it adds a fact the heading does not have.
- An HTML copy of the app screen, when it matches the app and carries the "example round" label.

### Shared CSS, `shared/site.css`

36. `.eyebrow` is uppercase with `.08em` tracking. After the copy fix, keep the class for one use or delete it.
37. `.facts` is the four-square stat strip. Delete the component.
38. Headings have no `text-wrap: balance`, and body text has no `text-wrap: pretty`. Add both.
39. `h1` uses `letter-spacing: -.035em`. Test at the smallest hero size. Loosen if letters touch.
40. `.foot-grid h4` uses uppercase tracked labels. Acceptable in a footer, but check the total count of uppercase labels per page.
41. `.grid.g3` and `.grid.g4` produce equal cards. Review each use. Prefer lists and tables for facts.

## 5. Reviewer checklist

Answer yes or no for each page. Every "no" needs a fix or a written reason.

1. Does the h1 say what the product does, in plain words, with no slogan?
2. Is every heading free of hard `<br>` breaks?
3. At 320, 768 and 1280 px, does each heading break between ideas, with no one-word last line?
4. Are `text-wrap: balance` (headings) and `text-wrap: pretty` (body) on?
5. Is there at most one eyebrow label on the page?
6. Is the page free of middle-dot fragment chains?
7. Does every sentence in body copy have a verb?
8. Is there at most one "not X but Y" or "X, not Y" on the whole site?
9. Is there at most one em dash on the page?
10. Is the page free of these words: unlock, elevate, empower, seamless, effortless, supercharge, pivotal, delve?
11. Is the page free of "Built for", "Made for" and "Your X. Your Y." headings?
12. Does every number have a unit and a noun, so a stranger knows what it counts?
13. Is the page free of a stat strip with one-word headings?
14. Does each headline state an outcome for the reader, not a feature name?
15. Is every claim true today, with its limits stated (for example: AI needs a connection)?
16. Are all quotes, logos and names real, with consent?
17. Is every product image a real screenshot or an exact, labelled copy of a real screen?
18. Is the page free of stock illustration, AI photos, glow blobs, glass panels and gradient text?
19. Are cards unequal where the ideas are unequal, and replaced by lists or tables where the content is a list?
20. Are numbered markers used only for real steps?
21. Is there no scroll-triggered motion, count-up or marquee?
22. Does each button say what happens when you press it?
23. Is one word used for each thing (for example, always "judge")?
24. Read aloud to a parent judge: does each paragraph say one real thing?
