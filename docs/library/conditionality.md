# Conditionality (condo)

> **Plain version.** Conditionality, or condo, means the negative may drop one of its options later in the round.
>
> - **What it means:** The negative can offer options such as a counterplan (its own plan) or a kritik (a challenge to the affirmative's ideas). If it drops one, the judge simply compares the plan with the world as it is now.
> - **The case for it:** Some people think this is fair, because it lets the negative test the plan from several sides.
> - **The case against it:** Others think it is unfair, because the affirmative must answer every option and the negative keeps only the best one. Judges differ, so teams argue it in the round.

## The three statuses

The **status** of an option tells the aff whether the neg can drop it. The aff often asks in cross-examination (CX): "What is the status of the counterplan?"

| Status | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Unconditional** | The neg must keep the option to the end of the round. |
| **Conditional** | The neg may drop the option at any time, for any reason. |
| **Dispositional ("dispo")** | The neg may drop it only under set conditions. |

Definitions of dispositional vary. A common one: the neg may drop the counterplan only if the aff makes a **permutation** (a test that does both plans). Some teams also say the neg cannot drop it if the aff answers it with offense, such as a disadvantage (DA) against the counterplan. Others do not use that limit. Because the word means different things to different teams, the aff should ask what the neg means in cross-ex.

**Kicking** is the slang word for dropping an option. "We kick the counterplan" means the neg no longer defends it.

## What happens when the neg kicks an option

When the neg drops a counterplan, the counterplan leaves the round. The judge compares the plan with the **status quo**: the world as it is now, without the plan. The neg can still win with the other arguments it extended, such as a DA or case arguments.

Arguments made *about* a dropped option can still matter. For example, if the aff made a DA against the counterplan, that DA goes away with it. But if the neg made an admission in CX, the aff may still use it.

### Judge kick

**Judge kick** is a related idea. Suppose the 2NR (the neg's last speech) goes for the counterplan. At the end, the judge thinks the counterplan is worse than the plan. Should the judge then compare the plan with the status quo instead?

- Some judges do this by default, because the counterplan was conditional all along.
- Some judges do it only if the neg asks for it, and the aff has a chance to answer.
- Some judges never do it. They think the neg must choose one world in the 2NR.

Judge kick is itself debated as theory. Read the paradigm, and argue it if it matters.

## How a condo argument is built

Condo is a **theory** argument: an argument about the rules of the round. It has the usual four parts. See [Theory](theory.md). The aff usually first makes it in the 2AC, its speech that answers the neg's first speech.

| Part | Example (generic) |
|---|---|
| **Interpretation** | "The neg may run at most one conditional option." |
| **Violation** | "The neg ran two conditional counterplans and a kritik." |
| **Standards** | "Each option splits the 2AC's time." |
| **Voter** | "Reject the team." |

The neg answers with a **counter-interpretation**: a different rule that it meets. Common ones include a set number of options, "conditional options are fine if they do not contradict each other," or "conditionality is fine for counterplans but not for kritiks."

## The arguments on both sides

This is one of the oldest debates in policy debate. Experienced coaches hold every view. This page does not take a side.

**Arguments that conditionality is good (usually the neg's):**

- **Testing the plan.** A good decision compares the plan with more than one option. The neg should be able to test it from several sides.
- **Balance.** The aff chooses its plan and has months to prepare it. The neg must react. Options help balance that.
- **Decision skill.** Choosing what to go for in the 2NR is a core skill. Condo rewards it.
- **The status quo is always an option.** The judge can always prefer the world as it is. Dropping a counterplan just returns to that.
- **Aff tools exist.** The aff can use theory, perms and offense against each option.

**Arguments that conditionality is bad (usually the aff's):**

- **Time split.** Each option takes a share of the 2AC's limited time. Answers get thinner.
- **Contradictions.** The neg can say two things that cannot both be true, such as a kritik of state action and a counterplan where the state acts.
- **Strategic cost.** The aff may avoid strong answers, such as offense against a counterplan, because the neg can kick a conditional counterplan and the answer goes with it. (Some dispo interpretations try to fix this. Ask in cross-ex.)
- **Depth.** It rewards running many arguments over going deep on a few.
- **New options later.** Counterplans added in the block (the neg's two speeches in a row) make it worse, because the 1AR (the aff's next speech) has less time than the 2AC.

## How judges differ

Condo is an area where judges' views really differ. Common patterns:

- **By number.** Many judges accept one or two conditional options without much worry. They grow more open to condo theory as the number rises. Others accept any number. A few reject any conditionality.
- **By contradiction.** Some judges care more about whether the options contradict each other than about how many there are.
- **By remedy.** For most theory arguments, many judges default to rejecting the argument, not the team. Many make an exception for condo. Their reasoning: the harm, a split 2AC, has already happened, so ignoring the counterplan does not fix it. Others treat condo like any other theory argument. See [Reasons to reject](reject-the-team.md).
- **By proof of harm.** Some judges want the aff to show harm in this round, such as answers it could not make. Others think the practice itself is enough.
- **By familiarity.** Some lay and traditional judges have seldom seen condo debates. A team arguing condo in front of them should explain it plainly, without jargon.

Most judges who hold a view on condo say so in their paradigm. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

## How to argue condo

**For the aff:**

- **Develop it.** A two-line condo argument in the 2AC rarely wins unless it is dropped. If you want to win on it, spend time on it in the 1AR and the 2AR (the aff's last speech).
- **Use this round.** Point to specific harm: answers you could not make, or contradictions you had to answer.
- **Ask for a remedy and defend it.** Say why the team, not just the argument, should lose.

**For the neg:**

- **Give a counter-interpretation you meet.** Explain why your number of options is reasonable.
- **Answer the standards.** Say why your options did not cause the harm, or why their benefits are bigger.
- **Argue the remedy.** Even if the aff wins, explain why rejecting the argument is enough.

## How to judge condo

1. **Was it extended?** It must be in the last rebuttal of the team that wants it.
2. **Who won the interpretation?** Compare the interpretation and the counter-interpretation, using the standards argued.
3. **What is the remedy?** Decide as argued. If no one argued it, use your stated default.
4. **Keep your view in its place.** If you hold a strong view, put it in your paradigm. In the round, decide the arguments the teams made.

## Example in flow shorthand

Each group is one argument, speech by speech. Key: CI = counter-interpretation. Ext = extend. Arg = argument. CP = counterplan.

```
THE RULE
2AC:   interp: neg gets no condo
       options
Block: CI: neg gets 2 condo
       options
1AR:   Ext: even 2 options split
       the 2AC
2NR:   2 options test the aff;
       2AC answered both
2AR:   split time meant thin
       perms on both CPs

THE REMEDY
2AC:   voter: reject the team
Block: reject the arg: ignore
       the CP
1AR:   harm is done; reject the
       team
2NR:   reject the arg at most
2AR:   ignoring a CP can't give
       back the 2AC's time

AFF SIDE BIAS
Block: aff picks the plan
1AR:   (no answer)
2NR:   Ext: aff side bias
       dropped
2AR:   (no answer)
```

*How to read it:* the neg ran two conditional counterplans. The 2AC argued the neg gets no conditional options. The block countered that two are fine, and that the remedy is to ignore the counterplan. The block also said the aff picks the plan, so the neg needs options (aff side bias). The 1AR did not answer that point, the 2NR extended it, and the 2AR still did not answer it. So the judge treats aff side bias as true. The judge must still weigh it against the 2AR's claim that the split time left the perms thin. Then the judge decides the remedy: ignore a counterplan, or vote aff.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: two lines of condo, then "they dropped it!"** Fix: develop it in the 1AR if you want to win on it.
- **Aff: no in-round harm.** Fix: name the answers you could not make or the contradictions you faced.
- **Neg: no counter-interpretation.** Fix: give a rule you meet, and defend it.
- **Neg: running contradictory options with no plan for the 2NR.** Fix: know which options you will keep, and how they fit together.
- **Both: forgetting the remedy.** Fix: say what the judge should do if the theory is won.
- **Judges: deciding condo by personal view when no one extended it.** Fix: decide only what the teams argued in the final speeches.
