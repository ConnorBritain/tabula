# Reasons to reject: the argument or the team

> **Plain version.** A theory argument says the other team did something unfair. If the judge agrees, the judge picks one of two main remedies. The judge can ignore the unfair argument and decide the rest of the round as normal. Or the judge can vote against the team that made it. Teams argue which choice fits. Many judges ignore the argument unless they hear a good reason to do more.

## The two main remedies

A **remedy** is what the judge does after a team wins a theory argument. The **voter** is the part of a theory argument that asks for a remedy. See [Theory](theory.md).

| Remedy | What the judge does | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Reject the argument** | Ignores the unfair argument. Decides the rest of the round normally. | The judge sets aside an unfair counterplan, then weighs the disadvantage (DA) against the case. |
| **Reject the team** | Votes against the team that made the unfair argument. | The judge votes aff because the neg's strategy was unfair. |

Teams sometimes ask for narrower remedies. For example, "reject the perm" means the judge ignores one permutation (perm: the aff's test of whether a counterplan really competes) but keeps the aff's other answers. A team may also ask the judge to let the other side make new answers to make up for lost time. These are less common, and judges decide them as argued.

## Why reject the argument

These are common reasons to reject only the argument.

- **It fixes the harm.** If an unfair counterplan is ignored, its unfair advantage is gone.
- **Proportion.** Losing the whole round for one argument may be too big a penalty.
- **Keep the debate on substance.** If theory could win rounds easily, teams might run theory to dodge the real debate.
- **It leaves the rest of the round alone.** The judge can still decide the issues both teams spent time on.

## Why reject the team

These are common reasons to reject the team.

- **The harm already happened.** Some harms cannot be undone. If a strategy split the time of the 2AC (the aff's answer to the neg's first speech), ignoring the argument later does not give the time back.
- **Deterrence.** If the worst result is losing one argument, a team has little reason not to try an unfair one. It is a free option.
- **Norms.** A ballot can signal what practices are acceptable. Some teams argue the judge should use it that way.

Many teams argue that the right remedy depends on the harm. If ignoring the argument fixes the harm, reject the argument. If it does not, reject the team.

## Common defaults

Judges' defaults are habits, not rules. Read the paradigm. If the teams argue the remedy, the judge should decide it from the arguments.

- **Most theory arguments.** Many judges default to rejecting the argument. Examples include arguments against a certain kind of counterplan or a certain kind of fiat (what a team may imagine is passed).
- **Conditionality.** Many judges treat condo as one of the few theory arguments that can be a reason to reject the team, because the time split cannot be undone. Others treat it like any other theory argument. See [Conditionality](conditionality.md).
- **Perm theory.** If a perm is found unfair, such as a severance perm (one that drops part of the plan), many judges reject the perm and nothing more. See [Permutations](permutations.md).
- **Topicality.** Topicality (T) works differently. If the plan is not topical, rejecting the "argument" means rejecting the plan. With no plan left, the aff has little to win on. That is why T is usually a reason to vote neg. See [Voters](topicality-voters.md).

## Reverse voting issues (RVIs)

A **reverse voting issue** (RVI) is a claim that a team should win because the other side's theory or topicality argument was wrong. It is usually made by the aff. For example: "The neg ran T and lost it. That wasted our time. Vote aff."

- **Arguments for RVIs:** theory takes time to answer. Without some risk, a team can run many theory arguments for free and force the other side to spend time on them.
- **Arguments against RVIs:** a team should not win just for being fair. Beating a theory argument means you met the rule, not that you deserve the round.

Many judges do not vote on RVIs, especially on topicality. Some judges are open to them in rare cases, such as a theory argument run in bad faith. A team that wants an RVI should explain it clearly.

## How to argue the remedy

**If you want the team rejected:**

- **Name the harm.** Say what happened in this round that cannot be fixed by ignoring the argument.
- **Explain the remedy.** Say why rejecting only the argument would leave the harm in place.
- **Extend it.** The remedy must be in your last rebuttal (the 2NR for the neg, the 2AR for the aff), with a reason, not just a word.

**If you want only the argument rejected:**

- **Say "reject the argument, not the team."** Then give the reason: ignoring the argument fixes the harm.
- **Do not concede the voter.** Even if you think you win the theory, answer the remedy too. It is your safety net.

## How to judge the remedy

1. **First decide the theory.** Did a team win that something was unfair?
2. **Then decide the remedy, as argued.** Which team explained better what should happen?
3. **If no one argued the remedy,** use your stated default. Many judges reject the argument in this case.
4. **Ask for a reason, not a word.** A dropped line such as "reject the team" still needs a reason. Many judges will not vote on a voter that was never explained.

## Example in flow shorthand

Each group is one argument, speech by speech. The block is the neg's two speeches in a row. The 1AR is the aff's next speech. Key: Intl fiat = imagining another country acts. CP = counterplan. Kick = drop. Arg = argument.

```
THEORY: INTL FIAT BAD
2AC:   neg can't fiat other
       nations
Block: real choice: nations do
       act
1AR:   neg can fiat any nation;
       aff can't answer it
2NR:   kick the CP; go for the
       DA alone
2AR:   norm: running it should
       cost them the round

THE REMEDY
2AC:   voter: reject the team
Block: reject the arg: ignoring
       the CP fixes it
1AR:   reject the team: sets a
       norm
2NR:   CP is gone; nothing left
       to reject
2AR:   reject the team: they ran
       an unfair CP
```

*How to read it:* the neg ran a counterplan in which another country acts. The 2AC argued that this kind of fiat is unfair, and asked the judge to reject the team. The block said nations really do act, and that ignoring the counterplan is enough. In the 2NR, the neg kicked the counterplan and went for the DA alone. So the theory question is now only about the remedy. If the judge rejects the argument, the counterplan is already gone, and nothing changes: the judge weighs the DA against the case. If the judge rejects the team, the aff wins. The 2AR extended the 1AR's norm argument. The judge decides whether that reason is enough to vote on.

## Common mistakes

- **Both: no remedy at all.** Fix: say what the judge should do if the theory is won.
- **Aff: "reject the team" with no reason.** Fix: explain why ignoring the argument would not fix the harm.
- **Neg: winning the theory answers but conceding "reject the team."** Fix: always argue the remedy too.
- **Both: asking for an RVI with no explanation.** Fix: if you want one, say why beating the argument should win the round.
- **Judges: treating your default as a rule.** Fix: decide the remedy from the arguments made. Use your default only if no one argued it.
