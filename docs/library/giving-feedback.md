# Oral critiques and feedback

> **Plain version.** Good feedback is kind, specific and short, and names one or two things to keep doing and one or two to change.
>
> - **Two kinds:** After a round, judges often give the debaters feedback. Some is spoken in the room, which is called an oral critique, and some is written on the ballot.
> - **Telling who won:** Whether you may tell the teams who won depends on the tournament's rules.
> - **Why it works:** Each debater knows what to practice next.

## Two kinds of feedback

**Oral feedback** is what you say in the room after the round. It may include the decision and the reason for it. It may also include advice for each debater. It is often called an **oral critique**, or "giving an oral."

**Written feedback** is what you type or write on the ballot. It has the RFD (reason for decision) and, on many ballots, a comment box for each debater. Debaters and coaches read it after the tournament, often more than once. See [The ballot and the RFD](ballot-and-rfd.md).

Both matter. The oral feedback helps while the round is fresh. The written feedback lasts.

## Disclosing the decision: follow the tournament's rule

To **disclose** means to tell the teams who won, in the room, after the round. Tournaments have different rules:

- Some tournaments ask judges to disclose after every round.
- Some ask judges **not** to disclose in early rounds, and to disclose only in later rounds.
- Some leave it to each judge.
- Some ask judges to give advice but keep the decision secret until results are posted.

Check the judge instructions, or ask at the judge table. If you are not sure, do not disclose. You can still give general advice without saying who won.

**Speaker points** are usually not announced in the room. Follow the tournament's rule.

**Submit your ballot first, or right away.** Many tournaments ask judges to enter the decision before giving any oral feedback. This keeps the decision honest and keeps the schedule moving.

## When to give an oral critique

Give an oral critique when the tournament allows it and there is time. Keep it short: 3 to 5 minutes for the decision, and a few more for advice, is common. The next round often depends on rooms and judges being free.

If you are running late, give one sentence each and put the rest in writing. "I will put more detailed advice on the ballot" is a fine thing to say.

## How to give an oral critique

1. **Give the decision first,** if you are allowed to disclose. "I voted affirmative." Do not make the teams wait through a long speech to learn the result.
2. **Give the reason.** Name the main issue and why one team won it. Use the debaters' own arguments.
3. **Say what would have changed your mind.** This is the most useful part for the team that lost.
4. **Give each debater one or two specific notes.** Talk to the winning team too. Everyone can improve.
5. **Stop and offer to answer questions.**

If you are not allowed to disclose, skip steps 1 to 3. Give each debater specific advice and stop.

## What makes feedback useful

**Specific beats general.** "Good job" teaches nothing. "Your 2AC answers were numbered, so I could flow every one" teaches a debater what to keep.

**Tie advice to a moment.** "In the 1AR you spent 3 minutes on topicality and 30 seconds on the disadvantage. The disadvantage decided the round." The debater can see the choice and fix it.

**Give a next step.** "Next time, compare the two impacts out loud in your last speech" is a clear action.

**Be kind.** Talk about the speech, not the person. Say "the 2NR did not answer the year-3 savings point," not "you were lazy." Many debaters are young and nervous. Feedback that feels like an attack is hard to use.

**Keep it about arguments and speaking.** Comment on clarity, pace, volume, choices and explanation. Never comment on a debater's looks, clothing, accent, body or identity. See [Round conduct, safety and evidence ethics](round-conduct.md).

**Be honest when you were confused.** "I did not understand how the counterplan was different from the plan" is useful. It tells the team what to explain better.

## Answering questions after the round

Debaters and coaches may ask questions. Most are polite and want to learn.

- **Answer briefly and calmly.** Explain what you saw on your flow.
- **You do not have to defend every line.** "That is how I saw the round" is a fine answer.
- **Do not change the decision.** Once the ballot is in, the decision stands, unless the tournament's rules allow a change.
- **End it if it turns hostile.** Say "Thank you, I have explained my decision," and leave. You can report rude behavior to tournament staff.

Arguing with a judge after the round is sometimes called **postrounding**. A short, polite question is normal. A long argument is not. You do not have to take part in one.

## Written ballot comments

Written comments are read later, without your tone of voice. So write with extra care.

- **Use full sentences.** Short notes like "bad 1AR" can sound harsher than you meant.
- **Use the same shape as an oral critique:** one or two things that worked, one or two things to change.
- **Write to each debater by speaking position,** for example "1A" or "2N," and match your notes to their speeches.
- **Keep the RFD and the advice separate.** The RFD explains the decision. The comments help the debater grow.
- **Check your tone.** Read it once as if you were 15 and had just lost.

## Example in flow shorthand

A judge's notes for comments, one block per debater. "1A" means the first aff speaker, "2N" the second neg speaker, and so on. "T" is topicality, "DA" is a disadvantage, and "CX" is cross-ex.

```
1A (1AC, 1AR)
  Keep: clear 1AC; good pace
  Change: 1AR: 3 min on T,
    30 s on DA. Split time by
    what matters
2A (2AC, 2AR)
  Keep: numbered 2AC answers
  Change: 2AR: compare
    impacts out loud
1N (1NC, 1NR)
  Keep: specific DA link card
  Change: 1NR: explain the
    card, do not only re-read
    it
2N (2NC, 2NR)
  Keep: strong CX on the plan
  Change: 2NR: answer the
    yr-3 savings point; it was
    dropped
```

*How to read it:* each debater gets one thing to keep and one thing to change, tied to a named speech; the judge turns each into a sentence or two.

## Common mistakes

- **Judges: disclosing when the tournament says not to.** Fix: read the judge instructions. If unsure, give advice but not the decision.
- **Judges: long speeches before the decision.** Fix: give the decision first, then the reason.
- **Judges: only general praise.** Fix: name one specific thing to keep and one to change, tied to a speech.
- **Judges: personal comments.** Fix: talk about arguments and speaking only, never looks or identity.
- **Judges: arguing back when a team disagrees.** Fix: explain once, kindly, and stop. The decision stands.
- **Debaters: arguing with the judge.** Fix: ask one short question to learn, then thank the judge. Save the rest for your coach.
- **Judges: skipping written comments after an oral.** Fix: write a short version too. The team will read it later.
