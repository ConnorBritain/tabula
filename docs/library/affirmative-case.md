# The affirmative case

> **Plain version.** The affirmative case is the first speech of the round, the 1AC. In it, the affirmative team says what it wants to do and why. The "what" is the plan: one sentence that says who acts and what they do. The "why" is a set of advantages: good results the plan causes. Each advantage shows a real problem, shows the problem will not fix itself, and shows the plan fixes it. The rest of the round is mostly about whether the case holds up.

## The parts

Most 1ACs are written before the tournament and read word for word. A typical 1AC has these parts, in roughly this order.

| Part | What it does | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Good results of the plan, each with its own harm and impact | "Advantage 1: Public health." |
| **Plan text** | One sentence: who acts, and what they do | "The federal government should fund program X." |
| **Solvency** | Proof the plan works | "Experts say funding X cuts the problem in half." |

Some teams read the plan first; some read it after the advantages. Both are fine.

### Plan text

The **plan** is the aff's promise. It names an **agent** (who acts, often the federal government) and an **action** (what they do). It should fit inside the resolution.

The plan text matters more than it looks. It is what the neg reads for topicality. It is what counterplans compete with. And it is what the aff must defend all round. A vague plan can dodge links, but it invites "you do not solve" and topicality attacks.

Under [fiat](fiat.md), the aff does not have to prove the plan would pass. It only has to prove the plan would be good if it did.

### Advantages

An **advantage** is a reason the plan is good. Each advantage is a small story with three beats:

1. **Harm:** something bad is happening now.
2. **Inherency:** it will keep happening, because nothing in the status quo stops it.
3. **Solvency:** the plan stops it, or makes it smaller.

Most advantages also have an **impact**: why the harm matters. It can be large (conflict between countries) or close to home (people lose health care). Many advantages have **internal links**, the steps between the problem and the impact. See [Advantages and internal links](advantages.md).

A 1AC usually has one to three advantages. Fewer, well-proven advantages often beat many thin ones.

### Harms, inherency and solvency in the 1AC

These three ideas come from the **stock issues**, the classic checklist for a case. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md). A complete 1AC proves all three. If any one is missing, the neg has an easy attack:

- No harm? Then the plan fixes nothing that matters.
- No inherency? Then the status quo already solves.
- No solvency? Then the plan does not work.

## Other kinds of affirmatives

Most high school affs defend a plan, as above. Some affirmative teams instead defend a different kind of advocacy. For example, they may criticize a way of thinking, or make a statement about the topic without a government plan. These are often called **kritikal affirmatives** or "K affs." They are common in some circuits and rare in others. The neg often answers them with a framework argument. See [Kritikal affirmatives](kritikal-affirmatives.md) and [Framework](framework.md).

## How to answer the case (for the neg)

The neg's arguments directly against the aff's advantages are called **on-case** arguments. They go on the case sheet. The common ones:

- **Harm defense:** the problem is smaller than the aff says.
- **Inherency:** the status quo is already fixing it. (See [Inherency](inherency.md).)
- **Solvency:** the plan does not work, or works only a little. (See [Solvency](solvency.md).)
- **Impact defense:** the impact would not happen even if the harm is real.
- **Case turns:** the plan makes the problem worse. (See [Case turns and case defense](case-turns.md).)

The neg also has **off-case** arguments, which get their own sheets: disadvantages, counterplans, topicality and kritiks.

Good neg teams do not ignore the case. Even small case defense makes the aff's advantage smaller, which helps any DA outweigh it.

## How to judge the case

At the end of the round, ask:

1. **How much of each advantage is left?** Did the neg reduce the harm, the solvency, or the chance of the impact? Advantages rarely go to zero, but they can shrink a lot.
2. **Did the aff extend it?** An advantage the 2AR does not talk about is usually gone. The aff must extend its case into the last speech.
3. **What is it weighed against?** Compare what is left of the advantage with the neg's best argument (a DA, a K, a counterplan's net benefit). See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic advantage about a public health program.

```
1AC                       1NC                       2AC                       2NR                       2AR
Adv 1: Public health
H: 1M lack care now       1. SQ solves: new          New program is too        Ext SQ solves: program    Program covers only
   (Analyst 21)              program started            small; covers 10%          will grow                 10%; no ev it grows
I: untreated illness      2. Impact D: most          Untreated illness is      Most still recover, so    Their card is about
   -> deaths                 recover w/o care           often permanent           the impact is small       minor illness; ours
                                                        (Author 22)                                         is about serious
Plan: fed gov funds
   clinics in area X
Solv: clinics cut gap     3. Solv: no doctors        Plan funds training;      Training takes years      Plan pays now; gap
   by half (Expert 22)       to staff clinics           staff in 2 yrs                                       closes in 2 yrs
```

*How to read it:* the neg made three on-case attacks. The aff answers each one in the 2AC and extends those answers in the 2AR. The key clash is inherency: is the status quo program big enough to solve? The aff says it covers only 10%, and the neg has no card that it will grow. A judge would likely give the aff most of its advantage, then weigh it against the neg's other arguments.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: a plan text that does not match the advantages.** Fix: make sure the plan does what the solvency cards say it does.
- **Aff: dropping the case in the 2AR.** Fix: extend at least one advantage, with its harm, solvency and impact, and weigh it.
- **Aff: too many thin advantages.** Fix: two strong advantages beat four weak ones.
- **Neg: no case arguments at all.** Fix: even two or three solvency or inherency answers make the advantage smaller.
- **Neg: arguing the plan "would never pass."** Fix: under fiat, most judges will not count this. Argue that it would not work, or would cause harm.
- **Judges: giving the aff full credit for an advantage the neg shrank.** Fix: weigh only the part of the advantage that is left.
