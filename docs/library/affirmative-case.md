# The affirmative case

> **Plain version.** The affirmative case is the 1AC (first affirmative constructive), the round's first speech: what the affirmative wants to do, and why.
>
> - **The what:** The plan: one sentence that says who acts and what they do.
> - **The why:** A set of advantages, good results the plan causes. Each shows a real problem, shows the problem will not fix itself, and shows the plan fixes it.
> - **Why it matters:** The rest of the round is mostly about whether the case holds up.

## The parts

Most 1ACs are written before the tournament and read word for word. The aff proves each part with **cards**: short quotes from experts or studies, with the author and year. A typical 1AC has these parts, in roughly this order.

| Part | What it does | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Advantages** | Good results of the plan, each with its own harm and impact | "Advantage 1: Public health." |
| **Plan text** | One sentence: who acts, and what they do | "The federal government should fund program X." |
| **Solvency** | Proof the plan works | "Experts say funding X cuts the problem in half." |

Some teams read the plan first; some read it after the advantages. Both are fine.

### Plan text

The **plan** is the aff's promise. It names an **agent** (who acts, often the federal government) and an **action** (what they do). It should fit inside the **resolution**, the topic statement that every team debates.

The plan text matters more than it looks. It has three jobs:

- The neg reads it to test **topicality**: does the plan fit the topic?
- **Counterplans** (the neg's own, different plans) compete with it.
- The aff must defend it all round.

A vague plan can dodge **links** (the neg's claims that the plan causes a specific harm). But it invites "you do not solve" and topicality attacks.

Under [fiat](fiat.md) (the rule that we imagine the plan passes), the aff does not have to prove the plan would pass. It only has to prove the plan would be good if it did.

### Advantages

An **advantage** is a reason the plan is good. Each advantage is a small story with three beats:

1. **Harm:** something bad is happening now.
2. **Inherency:** it will keep happening, because nothing in the **status quo** (the world now, without the plan) stops it.
3. **Solvency:** the plan stops it, or makes it smaller.

Most advantages also have an **impact**: why the harm matters. It can be large (conflict between countries) or close to home (people lose health care). Many advantages also have **internal links**: the steps between the problem and the impact. See [Advantages and internal links](advantages.md).

A 1AC usually has one to three advantages. Fewer, well-proven advantages often beat many thin ones.

### Harms, inherency and solvency in the 1AC

These three ideas come from the **stock issues**, the classic checklist for a case. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md). A complete 1AC proves all three. If any one is missing, the neg has an easy attack:

- No harm? Then the plan fixes nothing that matters.
- No inherency? Then the status quo already solves.
- No solvency? Then the plan does not work.

## Other kinds of affirmatives

Most high school affs defend a plan, as above. Some affirmative teams defend a different kind of advocacy. For example, they may criticize a way of thinking, or make a statement about the topic without a government plan. These are often called **kritikal affirmatives**, or "K affs." They are common in some circuits (groups of tournaments) and rare in others. The neg often answers them with a **framework** argument: a claim about what the aff should have to defend. See [Kritikal affirmatives](kritikal-affirmatives.md) and [Framework](framework.md).

## How to answer the case (for the neg)

The neg's arguments directly against the aff's advantages are called **on-case** arguments. They go on the **case sheet**: the page of notes for the aff's case. The common ones:

- **Harm defense:** the problem is smaller than the aff says.
- **Inherency:** the status quo is already fixing it. (See [Inherency](inherency.md).)
- **Solvency:** the plan does not work, or works only a little. (See [Solvency](solvency.md).)
- **Impact defense:** the impact would not happen even if the harm is real.
- **Case turns:** the plan makes the problem worse. (See [Case turns and case defense](case-turns.md).)

The neg also has **off-case** arguments. Each one gets its own sheet:

- **Disadvantages** (DAs): the plan causes something bad.
- **Counterplans:** a different plan is better.
- **Topicality:** the plan is off the topic.
- **Kritiks** (Ks): a challenge to the ideas behind the plan.

Good neg teams do not ignore the case. Even small case defense makes the aff's advantage smaller. That helps any DA outweigh it.

## How to judge the case

At the end of the round, ask:

1. **How much of each advantage is left?** Did the neg reduce the harm, the solvency, or the chance of the impact? Advantages rarely go to zero, but they can shrink a lot.
2. **Did the aff extend it?** To **extend** an argument is to carry it forward into later speeches. An advantage the 2AR (the aff's last speech) does not talk about is usually gone. The aff must extend its case into the last speech.
3. **What is it weighed against?** Compare what is left of the advantage with the neg's best argument: a DA, a K, or a counterplan's **net benefit** (the extra reason the counterplan is better). See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic advantage about a public health program. The aff's 1AC comes first. Then each neg attack is shown with the four speeches that argue it.

Key: H = harm, I = impact, Solv = solvency, SQ = status quo, D = defense, Ext = extend, ev = evidence, w/o = without, "->" = leads to. "(Analyst 21)" is an invented card: author and year.

```
1AC  Adv 1: Public health
  H: 1M lack care now
     (Analyst 21)
  I: untreated illness
     -> deaths
  Plan: fed gov funds
     clinics in area X
  Solv: clinics cut gap
     by half (Expert 22)

ATTACK 1 (inherency)
1NC  SQ solves: new
     program started
2AC  New program is too
     small; covers 10%
2NR  Ext SQ solves:
     program will grow
2AR  Program covers only
     10%; no ev it grows

ATTACK 2 (impact)
1NC  Impact D: most
     recover w/o care
2AC  Untreated illness is
     often permanent
     (Author 22)
2NR  Most still recover,
     so impact is small
2AR  Their card is about
     minor illness; ours
     is about serious

ATTACK 3 (solvency)
1NC  No doctors to staff
     clinics
2AC  Plan funds training;
     staff in 2 yrs
2NR  Training takes years
2AR  Plan pays now; gap
     closes in 2 yrs
```

*How to read it:* the key clash is inherency. The aff says the status quo program covers only 10%, and the neg has no card that it will grow. A judge would likely give the aff most of its advantage, then weigh it against the neg's other arguments.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: a plan text that does not match the advantages.** Fix: make sure the plan does what the solvency cards say it does.
- **Aff: dropping the case in the 2AR.** Fix: extend at least one advantage, with its harm, solvency and impact, and weigh it.
- **Aff: too many thin advantages.** Fix: two strong advantages beat four weak ones.
- **Neg: no case arguments at all.** Fix: even two or three solvency or inherency answers make the advantage smaller.
- **Neg: arguing the plan "would never pass."** Fix: under fiat, most judges will not count this. Argue that it would not work, or would cause harm.
- **Judges: giving the aff full credit for an advantage the neg shrank.** Fix: weigh only the part of the advantage that is left.
