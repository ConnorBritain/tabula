# Inherency

> **Plain version.** Inherency says the problem will not fix itself.
>
> - **What keeps it going:** something in the world now: a law, a gap in a program, or people who do not want to act.
> - **Why it matters:** if the problem is already being fixed, the plan adds nothing, so there is no reason to vote for it. Inherency is one of the stock issues, the basic jobs of an affirmative case.
> - **How to answer it:** the negative's usual attack is "the status quo already solves."

## What inherency means

The **status quo** is the world now, without the plan. **Inherency** is the aff's proof that the harm is built into the status quo. Something blocks the fix. Because of that block, the harm will go on unless the plan acts.

Inherency answers one question: **why is this not already being done?** If the aff has a good answer, the plan makes a real difference. If not, the judge has little reason to change anything.

Inherency is one of the five **stock issues**: harms, inherency, solvency, significance and topicality. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).

## The kinds of inherency

Teams name four common kinds. The [Stock issues](stock-issues.md) page lists the same four.

| Kind | What blocks the fix | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Structural** | A law, rule or policy | "Current law bans funding for program X." |
| **Attitudinal** | The people in charge do not want to act | "Officials oppose new spending on X." |
| **Existential** | Nothing names the block, but the harm exists and nothing solves it | "The problem is here, and no one is fixing it." |
| **Gap** | A program exists but leaves a hole | "The current program covers cities, not rural areas." |

### Structural inherency

A **structural** barrier is a written rule: a law, a regulation, or a budget limit. It is the clearest kind, because the aff can point to it. The plan then changes or removes that rule.

### Attitudinal inherency

An **attitudinal** barrier is a lack of will. The law may allow the fix, but the people who could act choose not to. Evidence shows officials or the public oppose the change.

### Existential inherency

**Existential** inherency says: the harm exists, and nothing now solves it, so something must be blocking a fix. It does not name the barrier. Some judges accept it as enough. Others think it is weak, because it does not explain *why* the status quo fails. The neg can also argue the fix is coming.

### Gap inherency

**Gap** inherency admits the status quo does something, but not enough. A current program leaves a hole, and the harm falls through it. Many modern cases use gap inherency, because there is often some program already in place.

## Inherency and uniqueness

Policy-style debaters often use another word for this idea: **uniqueness**.

- On a **disadvantage** (a neg argument that the plan causes something bad), uniqueness asks whether the bad thing is already happening.
- Inherency asks a similar question about the case: is the good result already happening?

If the status quo already solves the harm, the aff's **advantage** (its reason the plan is good) is **non-unique**. The plan does not cause the good result, because it happens anyway. Many policy-style debaters use the word "uniqueness" for this case argument too. See [Uniqueness](uniqueness.md).

Some judges, often called **stock issues judges**, treat inherency as a must-win item. Others, often called **policymakers** (judges who weigh costs against benefits), treat it as one factor in how much the plan adds. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

## How to answer inherency (for the neg)

- **Status quo solves:** a current law, program or trend is already fixing the harm. Read a **card** (a quote from a source, with author and year) that names it.
- **The barrier is gone:** the law the aff names has changed, or the officials who opposed the fix now support it.
- **The gap is small:** the current program covers most of the problem, so the plan adds little.
- **Minor repairs:** some traditional negs argue that a small change to a current program would fix the harm, without the plan. Judges differ on whether this is a fair neg position. It can look like a **counterplan** (the neg's own, different plan). Teams should argue it if it comes up.

A note on attitudinal inherency. Say the aff proves that officials strongly oppose the fix. Then the neg can argue they will undercut the plan. This is called **circumvention** (see [Solvency](solvency.md)).

## How to defend inherency (for the aff)

- **Name the barrier.** Read a card that says what stops the fix. A named law or a named gap is stronger than "no one is doing it."
- **Answer "status quo solves" with scope.** Show the current program is too small, too slow, or in the wrong place. "It covers 10% of the problem."
- **Use a recent card.** Inherency changes over time. An old card is easy to beat with a new one.
- **Check your plan text.** The plan should remove the barrier your inherency names. If the barrier is a law, the plan should change that law.

## How to judge inherency

1. **Find the barrier.** What does the aff say stops the fix? Did the neg prove that barrier is gone?
2. **Ask how much the status quo solves.** Inherency is often not all or nothing. If the status quo solves 90%, the plan adds 10%. A policymaker weighs that 10%. A stock issues judge may decide the aff lost inherency. Use the rule the teams argue for.
3. **Compare the dates and scope of the evidence.** In a "status quo solves" fight, the newer and more specific card often wins.
4. **Do not require a perfect barrier.** Most judges want a clear reason the status quo fails, not proof that change is impossible.

## Example in flow shorthand

A gap inherency debate on a generic rural clinic case. A **pilot** is a small trial program. Each neg attack is shown with the speeches that argue it.

Key: Inh = inherency, SQ = status quo, Ext = extend (carry forward), Ev = evidence, fed = federal. "(Ev 23)" is an invented card.

```
1AC  Inh: fed program
     funds city clinics
     only (Analyst 21)

ATTACK 1
1NC  SQ solves: new pilot
     adds rural sites
     (Ev 23)
2AC  New program is a
     pilot, 5 sites only
     (same Ev 23)
2NR  Ext SQ solves: pilot
     will expand next yr
2AR  Pilot = 5 sites;
     no card it grows

ATTACK 2
1NC  Attitude shift:
     officials support
     rural care
2AC  Ev says "consider,"
     not "fund"
2NR  (dropped)
2AR  Conceded: no change
     in policy
```

*How to read it:* the 2AC turned the neg's own card against it, and the neg dropped the second answer. A 5-site pilot leaves most of the gap open, so the aff likely keeps most of its inherency.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: no inherency card at all.** Fix: read evidence that names the barrier, even in a short 1AC.
- **Aff: a plan that does not remove the barrier.** Fix: match the plan text to the law or gap your inherency names.
- **Aff: proving attitudes so strong the plan cannot work.** Fix: be ready to answer circumvention with how the plan is enforced.
- **Neg: "status quo solves" with no proof.** Fix: name the current program and read a card that says it works.
- **Neg: arguing both "status quo solves" and "the problem is not real."** Fix: these can work together, but explain each one clearly so the judge does not see a contradiction.
- **Judges: treating any current program as a loss of inherency.** Fix: ask how much of the harm the status quo really solves.
