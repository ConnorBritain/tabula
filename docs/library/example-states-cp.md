# The states counterplan (50 states CP)

> **Plain version.** Many plans have the federal government act; the states counterplan says to let all fifty states do it instead.
>
> - **The neg's case:** The states can solve the same problem without a harm the federal plan causes.
> - **The aff usually answers:** the states cannot do everything the federal government can. Teams also argue whether it is fair to imagine all fifty states acting at once.
> - **When it comes up:** Almost every year, whatever the topic.

## The shape

The states counterplan is an **agent counterplan**: the same action, done by a different actor. It has the usual four parts of a counterplan. See [Counterplans](counterplans.md).

| Part | What it usually looks like (generic) |
|---|---|
| **Text** | "The fifty states should each fund program X." |
| **Solvency** | Evidence that states can run programs like X. |
| **Competition** | A net benefit the plan causes and the states avoid. |
| **Net benefit** | A harm tied to *federal* action, such as cost or federal power over states. |

### The text

The text usually copies the plan and changes the actor. Some texts add details, such as "acting together" or a shared agreement among the states, to answer the aff's solvency arguments. Some also add territories or other local governments. Each word affects the perm and the solvency debate. See [Counterplan text](counterplan-text.md).

### The net benefit

The net benefit is the reason the states alone are better. It must be something the plan causes because the *federal* government acts. Common generic shapes:

- **Federalism-style net benefits.** Federal action in this area takes power from the states, or weakens their role as places to try new ideas. The neg argues that this harm matters.
- **Spending net benefits.** The plan costs federal money and forces cuts. The states counterplan does not use federal money. See [The spending DA](example-spending-da.md).
- **Politics net benefits.** Passing the plan costs a federal leader political support. The states counterplan avoids that fight. Whether fiat (imagining a policy passes) allows this is itself debated. See [The politics DA](example-politics-da.md).

## The fiat theory question

**Fiat** means that, for the debate, we imagine a policy is passed. The states counterplan fiats all fifty states acting the same way at once, called **uniform fifty-state fiat**. The neg often says this tests a core question: why must the federal government act? The aff often says no single actor can make fifty states act at once, so it is too much fiat. See [Fiat](fiat.md) and [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md) for the full arguments on both sides.

## Common aff answers

The 2AC usually mixes several kinds of answers.

- **Perm do both.** A **perm** (permutation) is the aff's test of whether the counterplan competes. Here, the plan and the states act together. The aff then argues the perm avoids the net benefit, or that the net benefit is small. See [Permutations](permutations.md).
- **Solvency deficits.** The states miss part of the case. See [Solvency deficits](solvency-deficits.md).
- **Answer the net benefit.** Non-unique, no link, no impact. If the harm is not real, the states have no edge.
- **The counterplan links too.** State action can cause a similar harm, such as state budgets cutting other programs.
- **Theory.** Uniform fifty-state fiat is unfair, as above.

### Common solvency deficits

These are generic. Each needs evidence and an impact tied to the aff's advantage.

- **Federal-only tools.** Some powers belong only to the federal government, such as setting one rule for things that cross state lines.
- **Money.** States may lack the funds to run a large program well. Fiat makes them act; it does not give them more money.
- **Signal.** Other countries, or other actors, may respond to federal action and not to state action.
- **Durability.** Fifty separate laws can be changed one state at a time.
- **Conflict with federal law.** Federal law or the courts may limit what states can do in some areas.

### Answers to a federalism-style net benefit

- **Non-unique.** The federal government already acts in this area, so one more step changes little.
- **No link.** The plan works with the states and does not take their power.
- **Impact defense.** The harm is smaller or less likely than the neg says.

## How to judge a states counterplan

1. **Theory first, if argued.** If the aff extended fifty-state fiat theory, decide it. If the aff wins, set the counterplan aside, as argued. Then compare the plan with the status quo, and still count the net benefit as a DA.
2. **Competition.** Does the perm avoid the net benefit?
3. **Solvency.** How much of the case do the states solve?
4. **Weigh.** Compare the solvency deficit with the net benefit.

## Example in flow shorthand

Each block is one part of the counterplan, with each speech's line on it, top to bottom. Key: "CP" means counterplan, "fed" means federal government, "Solv" means solvency, "NB" means net benefit, "N/U" means non-unique, and "adv" means advantage. "Reject arg" means reject the argument (set the CP aside), not the team. Names in parentheses are invented cards.

```
CP TEXT AND THEORY
1NC: CP: 50 states fund program
     X.
2AC: 1. 50-state fiat bad: no one
     can make all 50 act.
2NR: Tests if fed is key; core of
     the topic; reject arg at
     most.
2AR: No one decides for all 50;
     reject the CP.

SOLVENCY (Solv)
1NC: States can run X
     (Analyst 21).
2AC: 2. Deficit: only fed can set
     one rule across state lines.
2NR: States have run X alone
     (Analyst 21).
2AR: Fed tool is key to half the
     adv.

NET BENEFIT (NB)
1NC: Fed action takes power from
     states.
2AC: 3. Perm do both.
2NR: Perm still takes state
     power.
2AR: Perm leaves state programs
     in place.

NB: UNIQUENESS
2AC: 4. N/U: fed already acts in
     this area.
2NR: Plan is a new step beyond
     today.
2AR: (no answer)
```

*How to read it:* the 2AC made four answers, and the 2NR answered all four. The 2AR did not answer that the plan is a new step beyond today. So the neg wins that the plan adds new federal power. On theory, both final speeches asked only to reject the counterplan, not the team. So the judge decides theory first. If the counterplan is rejected, the judge compares the plan with the status quo, and the federalism DA still counts against the plan. If the counterplan stands, the judge asks whether the perm still takes state power. If it does, the judge weighs the deficit (the federal tool, which the 2AR says is key to half the advantage) against the net benefit.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: a net benefit that the states also cause.** Fix: pick a net benefit tied to *federal* action only.
- **Neg: solvency evidence about one state, used for all fifty.** Fix: find evidence about state action in general, or be ready to defend the jump.
- **Aff: deficits with no impact.** Fix: tie each deficit to a part of your advantage, and say how big it is.
- **Aff: "the states won't do it."** Fix: fiat makes them act. Argue what they cannot do well, not whether they will act.
- **Aff: one line of theory, then a long 2AR on it.** Fix: develop the theory in the 1AR if you plan to win on it.
- **Judges: assuming the states counterplan is always fair, or always unfair.** Fix: decide the theory as argued in the round.
