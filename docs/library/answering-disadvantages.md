# Answering a disadvantage (the 2AC block)

> **Plain version.** When the negative reads a disadvantage, the affirmative must answer it in the next aff speech, the 2AC. A good answer has a few short, numbered points. Some points show the disadvantage is weak. One or two points, if you can, turn it into a reason to vote for the plan. The last point says why the plan's good outweighs any risk left. Then, in the 1AR, the aff picks the best points and explains them.

## What a "block" is

A **disadvantage** (DA) is a neg argument that the plan causes something bad. See [Disadvantages](disadvantages.md) for its four parts: uniqueness, link, internal link and impact.

The **2AC** (second affirmative constructive) is the first aff speech after the neg reads its DAs. A **2AC block** is a short list of answers to one DA, often written before the round. "Block" here just means a prepared set of answers. (It is not the same as the "neg block," the 2NC and 1NR back to back.)

Most teams number their answers: "1. Non-unique. 2. No link. 3. Link turn..." Numbers help the judge flow, and help both teams refer to each answer later.

## Step 1: flow the DA carefully

Write each part of the DA on its own line in the 1NC column: uniqueness, link, internal link, impact. Note the author and year of each card. See [How to flow](how-to-flow.md).

Listen for what is missing. Many 1NC DAs skip a step, or read a link about the topic, not the plan. A missing step is a free answer.

## Step 2: find the weakest part

Ask yourself four questions, one per part.

| Part | Question | Possible answer |
|---|---|---|
| Uniqueness | Is the harm already coming? | Non-unique (N/U) |
| Link | Does the plan really do this? | No link, or link turn (L/T) |
| Internal link | Does each step follow? | No internal link (IL) |
| Impact | Is the harm as bad as they say? | Impact defense, or impact turn |

You do not need to attack every part. But a block that answers only one part is easy for the neg to beat.

## Step 3: choose your offense

**Offense** is an answer that, if you win it, makes the DA a reason to vote aff. **Defense** only makes the DA smaller. Defense alone rarely brings a DA to zero, and some judges think almost every DA keeps some risk. So most blocks try to include offense.

There are two kinds of offense:

- **Link turn:** the plan does the opposite, and prevents the harm.
- **Impact turn:** the harm is actually good.

**Pick one kind per DA, never both:** a link turn plus an impact turn is a **double turn**, which says the plan prevents a good thing; see [Links and link turns](links.md).

Pair offense with the answers that support it. Non-unique plus a link turn is a strong pair: the harm is coming, and the plan stops it. See [Uniqueness](uniqueness.md).

## Step 4: add defense

Add answers that make each part weaker:

- **Non-unique:** the harm is already happening.
- **No link:** the plan does not do what the DA says. Point to the plan text.
- **No internal link:** a step does not follow, or a step is missing.
- **Impact defense:** the harm is smaller, slower or less likely.

Check that your answers do not contradict each other. "No link" and a link turn can sit together (the plan does not cause the harm, and if anything it helps). But be ready to explain how they fit.

## Step 5: weigh the case

End with at least one weighing answer.

- **Case outweighs:** even if the DA is true, the advantage is bigger, more likely or sooner. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).
- **Case turns the DA:** the plan's benefit makes the DA's harm less likely.
- **DA does not turn case:** answer any claim that the DA undoes your advantage.

Weighing matters most when the neg also has a counterplan. Then the DA is the **net benefit**: the reason the counterplan is better than the plan. If the counterplan solves your advantage, "case outweighs" no longer helps much, and your DA answers carry more weight. See [Counterplans](counterplans.md).

## Step 6: theory, if it applies

Some DAs raise a fairness question. For example, some teams argue that a DA based on the fight to pass the plan does not fit fiat (the idea that the plan is imagined to pass). Put a short theory answer in the block if you plan to use it. See [Fiat](fiat.md) and [The politics DA](example-politics-da.md).

## Step 7: budget your time

The 2AC answers many arguments, often several DAs plus a counterplan and the case. Common habits:

- **Put your best answers first.** If you run out of time, the strongest ones are on the flow.
- **Keep each answer short.** A clear tag and a card, or a clear analytic (an argument without evidence).
- **Give more time to the DA the neg is most likely to go for.** That is often the net benefit to a counterplan.

## In the 1AR: extend what wins

The **1AR** (first affirmative rebuttal) answers the whole neg block in a short speech. Common rules:

- **Extend your offense if the neg dropped it or answered it badly.** A dropped link turn is a big gain.
- **You do not have to extend every answer.** Extend the ones you are winning. Let go of weak ones on purpose.
- **Keep weighing.** The 2AR needs a reason that the case outweighs whatever risk is left.

## How to judge it

If you are judging, read an answered DA in this order.

1. **Find what was extended.** Count only the answers the aff extended in the 1AR and 2AR, and only the parts of the DA the neg extended in the block and 2NR.
2. **Sort offense from defense.** A won link turn or impact turn is a reason to vote aff. Defense only lowers the risk of the DA.
3. **Check dropped answers.** A dropped answer counts if the team that made it extends it and explains why it matters.
4. **Ask how much risk is left.** Judges differ on whether strong defense can bring a DA to zero. Say which view you used.
5. **Use the teams' weighing.** Compare the DA risk that is left to the case, using the weighing the teams argued. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

For how to answer the DA shapes you will meet most often, see [Common DA shapes](common-disadvantages.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic trade-off DA: the plan pulls agency staff away from disaster work. Each part shows the speeches that argue it, top to bottom. The 1NR is the second half of the neg block.

Key: N/U = non-unique. L/T = link turn. Impact D = impact defense. Ext = extend (carry forward). ev = evidence. Names in parentheses are invented authors.

```
DA: AGENCY TRADE-OFF

UNIQUENESS
1NC: Agency on track for
     disaster prep
2AC: 1. N/U: agency already
     behind (Analyst 22)
1NR: Ext U: newer ev says on
     track (Analyst 23)
1AR: Ext 1: their ev is one
     region only

LINK
1NC: Plan moves staff to
     new program
2AC: 2. No link: plan funds
     new hires
1NR: Hiring takes years;
     staff move now
1AR: Plan text: hires in
     year 1
2AC: 3. L/T: new program
     trains staff for
     disaster work too
1NR: (no answer)
1AR: Ext 3: L/T dropped
     -> DA is aff offense

IMPACT
1NC: Weak disaster response
2AC: 4. Impact D: staff gaps
     take years to hurt
     response
1NR: One big storm in year 1
     is enough (Expert 21)
1AR: (not extended)
2AC: 5. Case outweighs
1NR: DA comes faster
1AR: Case harm is now too
```

*How to read it:* answers 1 and 3 work as a pair: the harm is coming, and the plan stops it. Answer 4 says the harm is slower, not that disaster response is unimportant, so the link turn keeps its value. The 1NR never answers the link turn, so the 1AR extends it as aff offense. The 1AR lets answer 4 go, by choice.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: only defense.** Fix: add a link turn or an impact turn, so the DA can become a reason to vote aff.
- **Aff: double-turning.** Fix: pick link turns or impact turns on each DA, not both.
- **Aff: answers with no numbers.** Fix: number each answer, so the judge and the 1AR can find it.
- **Aff: no weighing.** Fix: end every block with "case outweighs" or a case-turns-the-DA answer.
- **Aff: extending everything in the 1AR.** Fix: extend the answers you are winning, and the offense the neg dropped.
- **Neg: ignoring a link turn.** Fix: answer every turn in the block. A dropped turn can flip the DA.
