# Round conduct, safety and evidence ethics

> **Plain version.** Debaters argue hard, but they must treat each other with respect. If anyone in the round feels unsafe, the round can stop, and the judge should get tournament staff. Evidence must be honest: no made-up quotes, no changed words, and no claiming to read words that were skipped. If someone says the evidence is dishonest, follow your tournament's procedure. You do not have to handle a serious problem alone.

## Respect in the round

Debate is a contest of ideas. Strong disagreement is the point. Personal attacks are not. Most tournaments expect everyone in the room, debaters and judges, to:

- **Talk about arguments, not people.** "Their link card is old" is fine. Insults about a person are not.
- **Avoid slurs, threats and mocking** of anyone's identity, background, body, accent or ability.
- **Be polite in cross-ex.** Questions can be sharp. Shouting over someone or refusing to let them answer is not.
- **Respect time and space.** Do not use the other team's materials, and do not interrupt speeches.
- **Treat the judge and the other team fairly,** before, during and after the round.

Judges can respond to rude behavior. Common steps are a calm reminder in the round, lower speaker points, and a note on the ballot. See [Speaker points](speaker-points.md). For serious problems, get tournament staff.

**Arguments about conduct are different from conduct itself.** Some teams make arguments about language or ideas, such as a kritik. Those are part of the debate, and the other team can answer them. Some teams also argue about in-round conduct as part of a kritik or a theory argument. But many leagues treat unsafe behavior as something to stop and report, not something to win on the flow.

## Safety

Every tournament has its own safety rules. Many share a few common ones:

- **Keep the room door open,** or follow the rules about adults and students alone in a room.
- **Online rounds:** keep cameras and chats to what the tournament allows. Do not record without the tournament's permission.
- **Know where the tournament staff are.** The central office is often called the **tab room**. It runs the schedule and handles problems.

### If a debater feels unsafe

Any debater can say they feel unsafe, at any point in the round. When that happens:

1. **Stop the round.** Stop the timers. Do not ask the debater to argue or prove the problem first.
2. **Check that everyone is safe.** If there is an emergency, call for help the way the tournament directs, or call emergency services.
3. **Get tournament staff.** Go to the tab room, or contact them the way the tournament says. Let staff decide what happens next.
4. **Write down what happened,** in plain facts, while it is fresh: the time, what was said or done, and who was there.
5. **Do not decide the round on your own** until staff tell you how to proceed.

Judges, you are not expected to investigate or fix the problem. Your job is to stop the round, keep people safe and bring in the people whose job it is.

## Evidence ethics

Evidence in debate must be honest. The common problems are:

- **Fabrication:** a quote or a source that does not exist.
- **Changed text:** words added to or changed inside a quote.
- **Miscut evidence:** words left out that change the author's meaning, such as skipping "not," or ending a quote right before "but this view is wrong."
- **Clipping:** claiming to read highlighted words out loud that were skipped. See [Evidence and cards](evidence-and-cards.md).
- **A missing or false cite:** no way to find or check the source.

Not every weak card is an ethics problem. A card that is old, short or overstated by its tag is usually a matter for debate: the other team can point it out in a speech. An ethics problem is about honesty: did the team present the evidence truthfully?

## Ethics challenges: follow your tournament's procedure

Leagues handle evidence challenges in different ways. Do not invent your own rule. Read the tournament's rules, or ask the tab room.

Some things are common across many leagues:

- **Raise it at the right time.** A debater who thinks there is a problem usually tells the judge between speeches, not in the middle of a speech.
- **Some leagues use a formal challenge.** In many of them, a formal challenge stops the round. The judge then decides whether the violation happened. The result is often a loss for the team found at fault, and sometimes a loss for the team that made the challenge if it is not upheld. The details differ, so check the rules.
- **Some leagues let teams debate it instead.** A team can make the problem an argument in its speech and let the judge weigh it.
- **Proof matters.** A judge needs evidence of the problem, such as the original source, the speech doc, or a recording where one is allowed. A clipping claim is hard to prove without a recording.
- **Staff can help.** If you are not sure what to do, stop the round and ask the tab room before you decide.

If you are a judge and you see a problem no one raised, such as clear clipping, follow the tournament's procedure. Many tournaments want judges to contact the tab room rather than act alone.

## Judge conduct

Judges set the tone. A fair judge:

- **pays attention** to every speech, and keeps phones and other work away;
- **stays neutral** in words during the round (some judges keep a neutral face too);
- **gives feedback about arguments and speaking,** never about looks or identity. See [Oral critiques and feedback](giving-feedback.md);
- **follows the tournament's rules** on disclosure, time and safety;
- **reports problems** instead of ignoring them.

## Example in flow shorthand

A judge's notes when an evidence question comes up during a round.

```
When           What happened                  What the judge did
After 2AC      Neg says Writer 19 card        Stopped timers. Asked: debate
               skips "not" -> miscut          it, or formal challenge?
               Neg: formal challenge          Checked the tournament rules:
                                              challenge ends the round
               Aff gives source; neg shows    Read full source paragraph.
               the cut line                   "Not" was cut out -> meaning
                                              changed
               Decision                       Upheld challenge, per rules.
                                              Told tab room. Wrote facts.
```

*How to read it:* after the 2AC, the neg said the aff's Writer 19 card had cut out the word "not." The judge stopped the timers and asked whether the neg wanted to debate it or make a formal challenge. The neg chose a formal challenge, and the judge checked the tournament's rules, which said a challenge ends the round. The aff gave the source, and the neg showed the line that was cut. The judge read the full source paragraph and found that cutting "not" changed the meaning. The judge upheld the challenge as the rules directed, told the tab room, and wrote down the facts.

## Common mistakes

- **Judges: asking an upset debater to prove they are unsafe before stopping.** Fix: stop the round first, then get tournament staff.
- **Judges: making up a rule for an ethics challenge.** Fix: read the tournament's rules or ask the tab room.
- **Debaters: accusing someone in the middle of a speech.** Fix: raise it with the judge between speeches, calmly.
- **Debaters: calling a weak card an ethics violation.** Fix: if the card is old or overstated, answer it in your speech. Save challenges for honesty problems.
- **Debaters: not marking cards you cut short.** Fix: say "mark the card at..." out loud. See [Speech docs](speech-docs.md).
- **Everyone: treating unsafe behavior as an argument to win.** Fix: arguments get debated. Unsafe behavior gets stopped and reported.
