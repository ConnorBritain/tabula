## Whose reasons are these?

The neg runs a kritik. The aff says the judge should weigh the plan's results. The neg says the judge should weigh the aff's ideas first.

Which of these are reasons teams give for the aff's view, weigh the plan? Pick all that apply.

- [x] "We prepared to defend our plan. If anything we said can be judged, the target moves every round."
- [ ] "The aff chose its words, so it should defend them like any other part of its case."
- [x] "If the plan's results do not count, our advantages count for nothing."
- [ ] "How we describe a problem decides which answers seem possible."

**Why:** The first is predictability, and the third is aff ground. Both are common reasons to weigh the plan. The second and fourth are common neg reasons to weigh ideas first. Both lists have strong supporters among coaches and judges. The judge decides which side argued better in the round.

## Answer the limits claim

The aff says: "There are countless ways to criticize a speech's words. Weighing words first gives the neg too many options."

Which answer does the neg usually give to this claim?

- [ ] "Limits do not matter in debate."
- [x] "The kritik must still link to this aff. That link is the limit."
- [ ] "The aff should have read fewer words."
- [ ] "The plan does not really pass."

**Why:** The aff's claim is about limits. The neg's usual answer is that the kritik must still link to what this aff did, so the debate stays tied to the 1AC. The aff can reply that links can be to any phrase. The judge weighs that exchange as argued.

## Answer "ideas shape policy"

The neg says: "Ideas shape policy. How we describe a problem decides which answers seem possible. So weigh ideas first."

Which answer does the aff usually give to this claim?

- [x] "That ideas shape policy does not prove ideas must come first."
- [ ] "Ideas are never important."
- [ ] "The neg read the kritik too fast."
- [ ] "The judge already knows which side is right."

**Why:** A good framework answer takes on the other side's standard directly. The aff can grant that ideas matter and still argue that this does not make them come before the plan's results. The neg will then explain why it does. Neither side's view is the "real" rule.

## A middle ground with no answer

You flow this on the framework sheet.

```
Block
  CI: weigh plan, but the K
    can turn it
1AR
  (no answer to the CI)
2NR
  Ext CI: under plan FW, K
    still turns case
```

In words: in the block, the neg offers a middle ground: weigh the plan, but the kritik can show the plan's results get worse. The 1AR does not answer it. The 2NR extends it.

How do many judges treat a middle ground that the other side never answers?

- [x] They use it to decide what counts
- [ ] They ignore it, because only the aff can offer a framework
- [ ] They vote neg at once, because a framework was dropped
- [ ] They use their own preferred framework instead

**Why:** If one team offers a middle ground and the other does not answer, many judges will use it. That does not end the round. Under this middle ground, the plan's results still count, and the judge must still decide whether the kritik turns the case.

## Win either way

The aff says: "Even if you weigh ideas first, our case outweighs the kritik." The neg says: "Even if you weigh only the plan, the kritik turns the case."

What are both teams trying to do?

- [ ] Drop framework from the round
- [x] Win even if they lose the framework debate
- [ ] Make the judge decide framework last
- [ ] Agree on one framework

**Why:** These are "even if" arguments. Each team plans to win under both frameworks. If one team does win under both, framework may not decide the round. That is common.
