# Judge paradigms

> **Plain version.** A paradigm is a judge's way of deciding a round. Some judges want the affirmative to prove a set of basic points. Some judges act like lawmakers who weigh costs and benefits. Some try to start with no views at all, and let the debaters set the rules. Many judges post a short paradigm online before a tournament. Debaters read it to learn what this judge cares about, and adjust their speeches to fit.

## Why paradigms exist

Policy debate has no single rulebook for how to decide. Judges come from different places: former debaters, coaches, parents, teachers, people from the community. Each brings a view of what a good round looks like. A paradigm makes that view public, so debaters can plan for it.

Few judges fit one type exactly. Most mix two or three. The types below are a starting map, not boxes.

## The common types

### Stock issues judge

The aff must win each **stock issue**, the basic points of a case: harms (a real problem), inherency (the problem is not solved now), solvency (the plan fixes it), significance (it is big enough) and topicality (the plan fits the topic). If the neg wins any one of them, the aff loses. This judge often values clear structure and the classic burdens of the aff. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).

*What helps with this judge:* label each stock issue. On the neg, pick one stock issue and win it clearly.

### Policymaker

The judge acts like a lawmaker choosing between policies. The question is: "Is the plan better than the status quo (the world now), or better than the neg's counterplan (the neg's own plan)?" The judge weighs costs and benefits. A disadvantage (a bad result of the plan) with a big impact can outweigh an advantage (a good result). This is a very common view in national circuit debate, the fast, national-level tournament scene.

*What helps:* compare impacts directly. Explain risk, size and timing. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

### Tabula rasa ("blank slate")

The judge tries to come in with no fixed views and lets the debaters decide what matters. If a team argues that the judge should vote on a certain issue, and the other team does not answer, the judge goes along. Many tabula rasa judges still have some limits, for example around safety, or new arguments in the last speeches.

*What helps:* tell the judge how to decide, explicitly. Do not assume a default.

### Games player

The judge sees debate as a game with rules that the debaters can argue about. Fairness to both sides matters a lot. Theory arguments, about the rules of debate, are often taken seriously. One example is conditionality: whether the neg may drop its counterplan later. See [Theory](theory.md).

*What helps:* explain why your arguments are fair, and why the other team's choices made the game unfair, with specific harm.

### Critic of argument / kritikal judge

The judge looks at the quality of the arguments and the ideas behind them, not only the plan's results. These judges are often open to kritiks (arguments that criticize the ideas behind the other side) and to affirmatives that do not defend a plan in the usual way. Many care about what the round teaches. See [Kritiks](kritiks.md).

*What helps:* explain your framework and why it is the right way to judge this round. See [Framework](framework.md).

### Hypothesis tester

The judge treats the resolution (the topic statement for the season) like a scientific claim that the aff must prove. The neg can test it in many ways, and can run several arguments that do not fit together, because each tests the claim. This view is less common today, but its ideas show up in debates about conditionality.

*What helps:* the aff should defend the resolution as a whole. The neg can test it from several sides.

### Lay or community judge

A parent, teacher or community member, often with little or no debate background. Lay judges usually want clear speaking, a moderate pace, plain words and a story that makes sense. They are a large part of many circuits, and they are an important audience. Debate is, after all, practice in convincing real people.

*What helps:* slow down. Explain terms. Give fewer, better arguments. Tell the judge in plain words why you win.

## How to read a paradigm

Look for:

- **Speed:** how fast the judge can follow. "Spreading" is very fast speaking, so "No spreading" means a slower pace.
- **Defaults:** what the judge does if no one argues the point. For example, "I default to competing interpretations on T" means that on topicality (T), the judge picks the better definition of the topic.
- **Presumption:** how the judge votes when no one wins clearly. Some judges give it to the neg, some to the side with less change, and some let the teams argue it.
- **Argument views:** how the judge feels about kritiks, counterplans, theory, topicality.
- **Pet peeves:** rudeness, stealing prep (planning while the prep clock is off), clipping (skipping words of evidence while claiming to read them), unclear speaking.
- **Evidence:** whether the judge reads cards after the round, and wants to be on the email chain.

Then plan: which arguments to run, how fast to go, and what to explain.

## How to write a paradigm

A good paradigm is short, honest and specific. Include:

1. **Your background** in a sentence (for example, "parent judge, third tournament" or "debated four years in high school").
2. **Speed** you can follow.
3. **Defaults** on key questions (T, theory, kritiks, counterplans).
4. **What you value** in a speech.
5. **Lines you will not cross** (safety, respect).

See [Writing a paradigm](writing-a-paradigm.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

The same final issue, seen by two judges with different paradigms. "SQ" means the status quo, and "->" means "so".

```
2NR:
  Inherency: SQ already funds
    half the plan. No barrier
    left
2AR:
  Yes, SQ funds half. Plan
    funds the other half ->
    real gain
Stock issues judge:
  Half the harm is solved
    now; no clear barrier ->
    inherency lost -> vote NEG
Policymaker judge:
  Plan still helps the other
    half -> net gain -> vote
    AFF
```

*How to read it:* both teams agree on the facts, but the stock issues judge needs a clear barrier and votes neg, while the policymaker sees a net gain and votes aff. This is why reading the paradigm matters.

## Common mistakes

- **Debaters: not reading the paradigm.** Fix: check it before the round, and ask the judge one or two quick questions if allowed.
- **Debaters: treating a paradigm as a full rulebook.** Fix: it is a guide. Still argue how the judge should decide.
- **Judges: a paradigm that is too long or vague.** Fix: list speed, defaults and pet peeves in a few lines.
- **Judges: voting on a default after the teams argued against it.** Fix: if the debaters argued a different standard, decide that debate first.
- **Everyone: thinking lay judges are "worse."** Fix: lay judges test a skill that matters: clear persuasion of a real audience.
