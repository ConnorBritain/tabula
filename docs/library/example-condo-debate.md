# Condo with three or more advocacies

> **Plain version.** Sometimes the negative offers several options of its own, such as two counterplans and a kritik. It says it may drop any of them later. This is called conditionality, or condo. The affirmative often argues that three or more options is unfair, because it must answer all of them in one speech. The negative answers that options are a fair way to test the plan. This page walks through that debate, speech by speech, without taking a side.

## The shape of the round

An **advocacy** is a position a team defends as its own, such as a plan, a counterplan or a kritik's alternative. **Conditional** means the neg may drop it later without penalty. See [Conditionality](conditionality.md).

A typical shape for this debate:

- **1NC:** two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional. It also reads a disadvantage (DA) or two, and case arguments.
- **CX of the 1NC:** the aff asks, "What is the status of each counterplan and the kritik?" The neg says, "All conditional."
- **2AC:** the aff answers everything, including a short condo argument.
- **Block:** the neg answers condo, often in one of the two block speeches.
- **1AR:** the aff decides whether to extend condo as a real option.
- **2NR and 2AR:** each side decides how much time to give it.

Some rounds add a counterplan in the block. Many aff teams argue this makes condo worse, because the 1AR has less time than the 2AC had.

## The 2AC theory block

The 2AC must answer every option, so condo is often short here: 10 to 30 seconds, read from a prepared block. It still needs all four parts of a theory argument. See [Theory](theory.md).

**Interpretation.** The aff picks a rule. Common choices:

- "The neg gets no conditional options."
- "The neg gets one conditional counterplan."
- "The neg may run options dispositionally": it may drop one only if the aff perms.

The choice matters. A strict rule ("no condo") is easy to violate but harder to defend. A loose rule ("one option") is easier to defend, and three options still violate it.

**Violation.** "They ran three conditional options: two counterplans and a kritik."

**Standards.** Common aff reasons:

- **Time split:** each option takes a share of the 2AC's limited time. Answers to each get thinner.
- **Strategic cost:** the aff may avoid strong answers, such as a DA against a counterplan, because the neg can drop it and the answer goes with it.
- **Contradictions:** the options may say opposite things. For example, the kritik says state action is bad, while the counterplans use the state.
- **Depth:** many options reward breadth over going deep on a few arguments.

**Voter.** "Reject the team. The time split already happened, so ignoring a counterplan cannot undo it." See [Reasons to reject](reject-the-team.md).

## The neg answers

The block usually answers with a counter-interpretation, standards and a remedy argument.

**Counter-interpretation.** Common neg rules:

- "Conditional options are fine if they do not contradict."
- "The neg may run up to a set number of conditional options."
- "Unlimited conditional options are fine."

**Standards.** Common neg reasons:

- **Testing the plan:** a good decision compares the plan with several options.
- **Aff side bias:** the aff picks the plan and has months to prepare. Options help balance that.
- **Decision skill:** choosing what to go for in the 2NR is a key skill.
- **Aff tools:** the aff has perms, theory and offense against each option.

**Defense.** "No harm in this round: the 2AC answered all three options." When this is true, it can be a strong point. The aff's best reply is proof of a specific harm in this round, such as an answer it had no time to make.

**Remedy.** "Reject the argument, not the team. The judge can ignore any option the aff thinks is unfair." Some neg teams also argue for **judge kick**: if the 2NR goes for a counterplan and the judge finds it worse than the plan, the judge should then compare the plan with the status quo. Judges differ on judge kick. See [Conditionality](conditionality.md).

## The 1AR choice

The 1AR has little time and many arguments to cover. It has two main choices.

- **Extend condo as a real option.** The 1AR spends real time on it: the interpretation, one or two standards with examples from this round, and the remedy. This keeps condo alive for the 2AR.
- **Extend it briefly, or let it go.** The 1AR spends its time on the substance, and condo becomes a small issue.

If the 1AR does not extend condo, most judges will not let the 2AR go for it.

## How the 2NR handles it

If the 1AR extended condo, the 2NR must answer it. A short answer can lose a round if the 2AR goes all in. Common 2NR moves:

- **Collapse.** The 2NR kicks the extra options and goes for one. It then argues the harm was small.
- **Answer the 1AR line by line.** It extends the counter-interpretation and the standards.
- **Point to the 2AC.** "The 2AC answered all three. Name an answer you could not make."
- **Hold the remedy.** Even if the aff wins, the judge should only reject the argument.

## How the 2AR goes for it, or not

The 2AR can go for condo in three ways.

- **All in.** The whole 2AR is condo. This is a big bet. It works best when the 1AR set it up well and the 2NR's answers were thin.
- **Part of the 2AR.** The 2AR splits time between condo and the substance. This hedges, but each part is thinner.
- **Not at all.** The 2AR drops condo and wins on the substance.

Each choice depends on the judge, on the 2NR's answers, and on how the substance looks. See [Rebuttal strategy](rebuttal-strategy.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

The 1NC ran two counterplans and a kritik, all conditional. Only the condo rows are shown.

```
2AC                  Block                1AR                  2NR                  2AR
Interp: 1 condo      CI: condo OK if      Ext interp: 3        Kicked K and CP 2;   3 options split
   option; they ran     options don't        options split        only 1 CP left       the 2AC 3 ways;
   3 (2 CPs, K)         contradict           the 2AC 3 ways                            perms got seconds
Contradiction: K     K and CPs test       K says state bad;    (no answer)          Contradiction
   says state bad;      different parts      CPs use state                             dropped: neg argued
   CPs use state        of the aff                                                     both sides
                     Neg flex: aff        Aff picks plan,      Ext: aff side        Side bias can't
                        side bias            but 3 is too         bias; 2AC still      justify 3 worlds
                                             many                 answered all
Voter: reject        Reject arg: kick     Harm is done;        Reject arg at most   Reject team: time
   the team             fixes it             reject team                               can't be given
                                                                                       back
```

*How to read it:* the 2AC argued the neg gets one conditional option, but the neg ran three. The block said condo is fine if the options do not contradict, that the remedy is to reject the argument, that the kritik and counterplans test different parts of the aff, and that aff side bias justifies options. The 1AR extended the interpretation, the contradiction and the voter, and answered side bias. In the 2NR, the neg kicked the kritik and one counterplan, and argued the 2AC still answered everything. The 2NR held the remedy: reject the argument at most. It did not answer the contradiction row. The 2AR extended that drop: the neg argued both sides, because the kritik said state action is bad while the counterplans used the state. The 2AR also said the time split left the perms only seconds, and asked the judge to reject the team. The 2NR's counter-interpretation depends on the options not contradicting, so the dropped contradiction hurts it. The judge must still decide the remedy: reject the argument, or reject the team.

## How judges with different views tend to decide

Judges hold every view on condo. These are common patterns, not rules. Most judges say their view in their paradigm. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

- **Judges who accept any number of options.** They usually need a clear technical win: a dropped standard, a dropped remedy, or a neg that never answered the 1AR. Even then, some will reject only the argument.
- **Judges with a soft limit, often one or two options.** Three or more is where these judges become open to condo. A well-developed 1AR and 2AR can win in front of them, especially with harm from this round.
- **Judges who care about contradictions.** They focus on whether the options say opposite things, more than on the count.
- **Judges who dislike condo.** They still need the aff to extend and explain it. Most will not vote on a two-line argument just because they agree with it.
- **Lay and traditional judges.** Some have seldom seen a condo debate. They need a plain story: what the neg did, why it was unfair or fair, and what the judge should do. Jargon loses them.
- **On the remedy.** Many judges treat condo as one of the few theory arguments that can be a reason to reject the team. Others treat it like any theory argument and only reject the option.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: a 2AR all in on condo after a 1AR that barely extended it.** Fix: if you may go for it, set it up in the 1AR.
- **Aff: no harm from this round.** Fix: name the answers you could not make, the perms that got seconds, or the contradictions you faced.
- **Aff: an interpretation the aff cannot defend.** Fix: pick a rule you can explain, such as one option, not just the strictest one.
- **Neg: a counter-interpretation the neg does not meet.** Fix: if your CI is "no contradictions," make sure your options do not contradict, or answer the contradiction claim.
- **Neg: a ten-second 2NR answer to a full 1AR extension.** Fix: give condo the time the 1AR gave it, and hold the remedy.
- **Judges: deciding by your own view when the teams argued it differently.** Fix: decide the arguments made, and use your default only if no one argued a point.
