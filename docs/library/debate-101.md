# Debate 101: what policy debate is

> **Plain version.** Policy debate is a game between two teams of two. Each year has one topic, called the resolution. One team, the affirmative (aff), proposes a plan that fits the topic. The other team, the negative (neg), argues the plan is a bad idea, or that the aff did not do its job. Each person gives two speeches, and asks and answers questions. At the end, the judge decides which team did the better debating, writes why, and gives each debater points.

## The resolution

The **resolution** is the topic: one sentence for the whole season. It usually says that a government should make a big change in one area. The aff must fit its plan inside that sentence. The neg can argue that the plan does not fit. That fight is called **topicality**.

## The two teams

**The affirmative ("aff")** says yes to the resolution. It reads a **plan**: one sentence that says who does what. Then it gives reasons the plan is good. These reasons are called **advantages**.

**The negative ("neg")** says no. It does not have to defend one fixed thing. It can:

- argue the plan does not work, or does not solve a real problem;
- argue the plan causes something bad (a **disadvantage**, or DA);
- offer a different idea that is better (a **counterplan**, or CP);
- argue the plan is not on the topic (**topicality**, or T);
- question the ideas behind the plan (a **kritik**, or K).

Teams switch sides from round to round. Over a tournament, each team argues both sides.

## The speeches

There are eight speeches and four cross-examinations (question times). Each debater gives two speeches:

- one **constructive**, to build arguments;
- one **rebuttal**, to answer what is left. In the last two speeches, the rebuttal also picks the best arguments and explains why they win.

The common high school format:

| Speech | Who | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1AC: first affirmative constructive | 1st aff speaker | 8 min |
| Cross-ex of the 1AC | neg asks, aff answers | 3 min |
| 1NC: first negative constructive | 1st neg speaker | 8 min |
| Cross-ex of the 1NC | aff asks | 3 min |
| 2AC: second affirmative constructive | 2nd aff speaker | 8 min |
| Cross-ex of the 2AC | neg asks | 3 min |
| 2NC: second negative constructive | 2nd neg speaker | 8 min |
| Cross-ex of the 2NC | aff asks | 3 min |
| 1NR: first negative rebuttal | 1st neg speaker | 5 min |
| 1AR: first affirmative rebuttal | 1st aff speaker | 5 min |
| 2NR: second negative rebuttal | 2nd neg speaker | 5 min |
| 2AR: second affirmative rebuttal | 2nd aff speaker | 5 min |

Times can differ. Five-minute rebuttals are standard, but some leagues use 6. Check the tournament's rules.

**The "negative block."** The 2NC and 1NR come back to back, with only a cross-ex between them. That is 13 minutes of neg speaking in a row. The neg usually splits the work: the 2NC takes some arguments, and the 1NR takes the rest. Then the 1AR has 5 minutes to answer all of it. That is why the 1AR is often called the hardest speech.

**The aff speaks first and last.** The aff has to prove its case, so it gets the first word and the last word.

## Cross-examination ("cross-ex" or "CX")

After each constructive, the other team asks questions for 3 minutes. Cross-ex is for:

- making sure you understand an argument;
- pinning down what the other team will and will not defend;
- setting up your own next speech.

Cross-ex is not a speech. What a debater says in cross-ex matters only if someone uses it in a later speech. But judges do listen, and good cross-ex often shapes the round.

## Prep time

**Prep time** is planning time. Each team gets a fixed amount for the whole round, often 8 or 10 minutes. A team uses it before any of its own speeches. The judge, or a timekeeper, keeps track. When a team's prep time is gone, it must speak without more prep.

## Evidence and cards

Debaters read **evidence**: short quotes from experts, called **cards**. A card has three parts:

1. a **tag**: one sentence, written by the debater, that says what the card proves;
2. a **cite**: who wrote it, when, and where;
3. the **text**: the quote, with the key parts read out loud.

A card is only as good as what it really says. A judge can ask to see a card after the round. Most rounds today share **speech documents** (files of the cards each speaker reads), so the judge can read along.

## Flowing

The **flow** is the notes of the round. Debaters and judges write each argument in a column for its speech. They draw lines to show which answer goes with which argument. The flow shows what each team said. It also shows what the other team **dropped** (did not answer).

See [How to flow](how-to-flow.md).

## Who wins: the ballot and the RFD

At the end, the judge fills out a **ballot**. The ballot has:

- the **decision**: which team won;
- the **RFD** (reason for decision): a short explanation of why;
- **speaker points**: a score for each debater.

**How judges decide.** Most judges ask: "Based only on what was said in this round, which team's arguments were better?" The judge does not add their own arguments. If one team makes an argument and the other team does not answer it, the judge usually treats that argument as true.

Judges have different views about which kinds of arguments matter most. A judge's view is called their **paradigm**. Many judges post a paradigm online before the tournament. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

**A simple way to decide**, for a new judge:

1. Look at the last two speeches (the 2NR and 2AR). What did each team say is the most important issue?
2. For each issue, which team had the better explanation and the better answers?
3. Which issue matters most? The teams should tell you how to compare. That is called **impact calculus**. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).
4. Vote for the team that won the most important issue. Write that in your RFD.

## Speaker points

Each debater gets points for how well they spoke and argued. Most leagues use a scale near 25 to 30, with tenths allowed. Points reward clear speaking, smart choices, good cross-ex and respect for others. They are separate from the win. A team can lose the round and still get high points. See [Speaker points](speaker-points.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A very short round, on one argument only. The aff says the plan saves money. The neg says the plan costs more than it saves.

Key: "Adv" = advantage. "$" = money. ">" = more than. "->" = leads to. "yr" = year. "#1" = answer number 1. "Ext" = extend: carry an argument forward into a later speech.

```
1AC
  Adv: plan saves $
1NC
  Cost > savings (Analyst 21)
2AC
  1. Savings come in yr 2
     (Expert 22)
  2. Cost is 1-time only
2NR
  Ext cost. Their #1 card only
  says "may" -> no savings
2AR
  Ext #2: cost is 1-time,
  savings repeat. 2NR dropped it.
```

*How to read it:* the 2AC made two answers. The 2NR answered the first one, but never answered the point that the cost happens only once. A judge could vote aff, because the 2AR extended a point the neg dropped.

## Common mistakes

- **New judges vote on what they believe, not on what was said.** Fix: decide only from arguments made in the round. Your own view of the policy does not count.
- **Debaters treat cross-ex as a speech.** Fix: ask short questions, and use the answers in your next speech.
- **Debaters bring up new arguments in the 2NR or 2AR.** Fix: the last two speeches extend and compare. Most judges ignore brand-new arguments there, unless they answer something new from the other team. (New answers in the 1NR and 1AR are normal.)
- **Judges forget to time prep.** Fix: start the prep clock when a team stops to prepare. Stop it when they are ready to speak.
- **Writing an RFD that only says who won.** Fix: name the issue that decided it, and why that team won it.
