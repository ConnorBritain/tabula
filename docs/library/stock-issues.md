# Stock issues

> **Plain version.** The stock issues are a checklist for the affirmative. To win, the affirmative must show five things. There is a real problem (harms). The problem will not fix itself (inherency). The plan fixes it (solvency). The problem is big enough to matter (significance). And the plan fits the topic (topicality). Some judges decide the whole round on this checklist: if the negative wins any one item, the negative wins.

## The five stock issues

| Stock issue | The question | Neg attack, in plain words |
|---|---|---|
| **Harms** | Is there a real problem now? | "The problem is not real, or not bad." |
| **Inherency** | Will the problem last without the plan? | "The status quo is already fixing it." |
| **Solvency** | Does the plan fix the problem? | "The plan does not work." |
| **Significance** | Is the problem, or the fix, big enough to matter? | "The change is too small to care about." |
| **Topicality** | Does the plan fit the resolution? | "The plan is off the topic." |

A common memory aid: the aff must prove a **significant harm**, that is **inherent**, which the plan **solves**, with a **topical** plan.

### Harms

Harms are the bad things happening now. The aff shows who is hurt, how, and how many. Harms are often stated as the **impact** of an advantage. See [Harms](harms.md).

### Inherency

Inherency says the harm is built into the status quo. Something keeps it from being fixed. There are a few kinds:

- **Structural:** a law or rule blocks the fix.
- **Attitudinal:** the people in charge do not want to fix it.
- **Existential:** the harm exists and nothing now solves it, so something must be blocking a fix.
- **Gap:** the current law or program leaves a hole, and the harm falls through it.

Inherency is why the aff needs a plan. If the world is already solving the problem, the plan adds nothing. See [Inherency](inherency.md).

### Solvency

Solvency says the plan works. It is usually proven with expert evidence that the plan's action reduces the harm. Solvency does not need to be total. Fixing half of a big problem can still be worth it. See [Solvency](solvency.md).

### Significance

Significance says the harm, or the plan's effect on it, is large enough to matter. It can be measured in numbers (how many people) or in quality (how serious, such as loss of life or rights). Many judges fold significance into harms and solvency. Traditional judges often treat it as its own item. See [Significance](significance.md).

### Topicality

Topicality says the plan fits inside the resolution. It is the only stock issue usually argued as its own off-case position, on its own sheet. See [Topicality](topicality.md).

## Why stock issues matter

**For judges.** Some judges, often called **stock issues judges**, use this checklist as their decision rule. The aff must win all five. If the neg clearly wins one, the neg wins the round, even with no disadvantage. This view is common among traditional and lay judges, and on circuits with many parent and community judges.

**For debaters.** Even judges who do not use the checklist strictly still expect the aff to prove each item. A neg that wins "the status quo solves" or "the plan does not work" has made the aff's advantage tiny, and that makes every other neg argument stronger. So stock issues are useful in every round.

**Adapt.** If a judge's paradigm says "stock issues," make your arguments in those words. Say "they lose inherency, so you vote neg," not only "their advantage is non-unique."

## How to answer stock issue attacks (for the aff)

- **Harms:** read more or better evidence about the harm, and explain who is hurt and how.
- **Inherency:** show why the status quo is not solving. Point to the barrier.
- **Solvency:** explain how the plan works, step by step. Read solvency cards that match the plan text.
- **Significance:** put a number or a clear quality on the harm, and say why even partial solvency matters.
- **Topicality:** see [Topicality](topicality.md) for the full answer.

## How to judge with stock issues

1. **Decide which rule applies.** If the teams argue that stock issues are the rule, use them. If they argue for weighing advantages against disadvantages, do that. If no one says, use your paradigm.
2. **Go through the five items.** For each one, did the aff prove it? Did the neg clearly beat it?
3. **Be careful with "clearly."** Under a strict checklist, a small doubt on solvency is not the same as no solvency. Many stock issues judges ask whether the neg *won* the issue, not whether it raised a question.
4. **If no one wins clearly,** some judges use **presumption**: the tie goes to the neg, because the aff must prove change is good. See [Presumption](presumption.md).

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: assuming inherency is obvious.** Fix: read a card that names the barrier, and extend it when the neg says the status quo solves.
- **Neg: raising a stock issue without proving it.** Fix: "they do not solve" needs a reason and, ideally, a card.
- **Neg: going for five stock issues in the 2NR.** Fix: pick the one or two you are clearly winning, and explain why each one alone is a reason to vote neg.
- **Both: forgetting the judge's paradigm.** Fix: read it. A stock issues judge wants to hear those words.
- **Judges: treating any doubt as a loss for the aff.** Fix: ask which team *won* the issue as argued.
