# Framework vs a K aff: a timeless example

> **Plain version.** This page walks through one common kind of round: the aff argues for a better way to study the topic, not a government plan.
>
> - **The neg's case:** The aff should have defended a plan. A shared topic makes debate fair and useful.
> - **The aff's case:** The neg's rule leaves out its ideas and its people.
> - **How it ends:** Each speech narrows the debate, until the judge must decide which model of debate is better.

## The setup

The aff runs a **kritikal affirmative** (K aff): an aff built on a critique of ideas, without a government plan. See [Kritikal affirmatives](kritikal-affirmatives.md). In this example, the 1AC argues that debates about the topic usually treat a group of people as numbers. Its **advocacy** (its statement of what it stands for) is to study the topic through that group's own experience.

The neg answers with **framework**, also called **T-USFG**: the aff must defend government action that fits the resolution. The full arguments on both sides are on [Framework against a kritikal affirmative](framework-vs-k-aff.md). This page shows how they play out across one round.

## The 1NC

A typical 1NC framework argument is short. It has:

- **Interpretation:** the aff must defend a topical action by the federal government.
- **Violation:** the aff does not.
- **Standards:** limits, ground and predictability, which add up to fairness; and clash, which leads to education and skills.
- **Voters:** fairness and education.

Many negs also read case arguments, or presumption (the claim that, with no good reason to vote aff, the judge should vote neg), in the 1NC. This page follows only the framework sheet.

## The 2AC choices

The 2AC has to decide what kind of answer to build. There are two main approaches, and many affs use both.

**The counter-interpretation approach.** The aff offers its own model, such as "the aff must be in the topic's direction." It then defends that model with standards: the topic's subject still limits debate, and the aff's model gives better education. This approach meets the neg on its own ground.

**The impact turn approach.** The aff argues the neg's model is harmful. It excludes some ideas and people. It trains students to see problems from the government's view. This approach says that even if the neg's model is more limited, it costs too much.

The 2AC usually also answers the neg's standards one by one. For example, it may say fairness only matters because it serves education, or that the neg had general arguments ready, such as case answers.

## The block choices

The **neg block** is the 2NC and 1NR, two neg speeches in a row. On framework, the block usually does three things.

- **Answers the counter-interpretation.** It says the aff's model has no real limit, because almost any aff can claim to be "in the direction" of the topic.
- **Weighs the neg's impacts.** It says fairness comes first, because the game needs it, or that clash is what makes debate teach anything.
- **Answers the impact turns.** It reads a **topical version of the aff** (TVA): a plan that keeps the aff's main ideas. It may add **switch-side debate** (SSD): the aff could make its argument on the neg, as a kritik.

## The 1AR choices

The 1AR is short. It must keep the aff's best path alive. Most 1ARs extend the counter-interpretation, weigh the impact turn against fairness, and answer the TVA. A 1AR that drops a key neg argument, such as SSD or clash, gives the neg an easy extension.

## The 2NR choices

The 2NR picks one main story.

- **Fairness.** The game must be fair to be worth playing. The ballot can protect fairness in this round, even if it cannot fix wider problems.
- **Clash.** Debate needs a stable point of disagreement to test ideas. Without it, neither team learns from the other.

Either way, the 2NR uses the TVA and SSD to shrink the aff's impact turns. It also extends anything the 1AR dropped.

## The 2AR choices

The 2AR also picks a story.

- **Counter-interpretation:** "Our model still limits debate, and it gives better education. The neg had ground."
- **Impact turn:** "Even if the neg's model is more limited, exclusion outweighs. The TVA does not solve, because a plan drops our method."

Most 2ARs combine the two, with an "even if" between them.

## Example in flow shorthand

The framework sheet, from the 2AC to the 2AR, one row at a time. The 1NC is described above. Key: CI = counter-interpretation, TVA = topical version of the aff, SSD = switch-side debate, Ks = kritiks, X = a stand-in for the topic area.

```
ROW 1: THE CI
2AC: CI: aff must be in the
  topic's direction
Block: CI: no limit; any aff
  claims it
1AR: Limit is the topic's
  subject area
2NR: Neg can't prep every
  "direction" aff
2AR: Neg had generic Ks and
  case answers

ROW 2: FAIRNESS
2AC: Fairness is not an impact
  by itself
Block: Fairness first: the game
  needs it
1AR: Exclusion outweighs a game
  rule
2NR: Fairness: only thing a
  ballot can fix
2AR: Ballot can fix our
  exclusion too

ROW 3: IMPACT TURN, TVA, SSD
2AC: Impact turn: their model
  excludes
Block: TVA: a plan for X keeps
  the core; SSD: say it on the
  neg
1AR: TVA drops the core; method
  is the aff
2NR: TVA solves; SSD is dropped
  by the 1AR
2AR: TVA still drops the core
  (no SSD answer)

ROW 4: CLASH
2AC: Clash: we meet it; neg had
  answers
Block: Clash: skills from
  testing a plan
1AR: (no answer)
2NR: Dropped: clash is needed
  to learn
2AR: Our clash is deeper, about
  method
```

*How to read it:* on row 3, the 1AR answers the TVA but not SSD. The 2NR points that out, and the 2AR still gives no answer to SSD. On row 4, the 1AR does not answer the block's clash argument, and the 2NR extends it as dropped. The 2AR then gives a new answer about clash. Most judges would treat that answer as too late, so the neg is ahead on clash.

## How judges tend to decide it

Judges come to this debate with different views. Most still try to decide it on the flow. Here is how the round above might look to three kinds of judge.

- **A judge who leans toward framework** may see the dropped clash argument and the dropped SSD as decisive. SSD shrinks the aff's exclusion claim. Clash then gives the neg an impact the aff never answered in time.
- **A judge who leans toward K affs** may focus on the third row. If the TVA truly drops the aff's method, the impact turn may still stand. That judge may also see SSD as only defense, which does not erase the aff's offense.
- **A judge who tries to stay neutral** will weigh what each team won. The aff dropped two neg arguments (SSD and clash). The aff still has a live impact turn and a live counter-interpretation. The decision rests on whose weighing was clearer in the last speeches.

A **lay judge**, a judge with little debate background, may find this round hard to follow. Both teams should explain in plain words what each model of debate looks like, and why it matters. The team that does this better often wins with a lay judge.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: dropping arguments in the 1AR.** Fix: answer the TVA, SSD and clash, even briefly. A dropped argument is hard to recover in the 2AR.
- **Aff: new answers in the 2AR.** Fix: make the answer in the 1AR. Most judges will not count a brand new 2AR answer.
- **Neg: a TVA that does not fit the aff.** Fix: write a TVA that keeps the aff's main ideas, and explain how.
- **Neg: no answer to the counter-interpretation's limit claim.** Fix: answer it directly, and say which affs the neg still could not prepare for under that limit.
- **Neg: going for every standard in the 2NR.** Fix: pick fairness or clash as the main story, and weigh it against the aff's impact turn.
- **Both: no "even if."** Fix: say why you win even if the other side wins its best argument.
- **Judges: deciding before the round starts.** Fix: decide the round on the flow, and say in the RFD which argument decided it.
