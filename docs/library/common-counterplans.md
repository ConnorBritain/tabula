# Common counterplan shapes

> **Plain version.** A counterplan, or CP, is the negative's own plan, offered instead of the affirmative's plan. The topic changes every year, but a few kinds of counterplan come back every year. Some change who acts. Some take a different action. Some change how the plan is passed. Some do almost all of the plan but leave out one part. Each kind has its usual answers, and each raises its own fairness questions.

## The basics, in brief

A counterplan must do two things. It must **solve** the aff's problem, or most of it. And it must **compete**: the judge must have a reason to choose it *instead of* the plan, not just add it to the plan. Most counterplans compete through a **net benefit**, a reason the counterplan alone is better than doing both. Often the net benefit is a disadvantage the plan causes and the counterplan avoids. See [Counterplans](counterplans.md) and [Competition](competition.md).

The aff's main tool is the **permutation** ("perm"): a test that asks whether the plan and the counterplan can be done together. See [Permutations](permutations.md).

## The shapes at a glance

| Shape | What it changes | Usual way it competes |
|---|---|---|
| **States** | Who acts: the fifty states, not the federal government | A net benefit tied to federal action |
| **Agent** | Who acts: another branch or body | A net benefit tied to the plan's actor |
| **Advantage** | What is done: a different action | A net benefit the plan causes |
| **Process** | How the plan is passed or put in place | Certainty, timing, or a net benefit from the process |
| **Consult** | Asks another party first; acts only if it agrees | Certainty and timing, with a relations net benefit |
| **PIC** | Does the plan minus one part | The left-out part is the net benefit |

## States

**The shape.** All fifty states do what the plan does. The federal government does nothing.

**Competition.** A net benefit that comes from federal action, such as federal cost, or federal power over the states.

**Usual aff answers.** Perm do both. Solvency deficits: only the federal government has some tools, states may lack money, and other actors respond to federal action. The states also link to the net benefit.

**Theory, in one line.** Teams debate whether the neg may fiat (imagine into action) fifty governments at once. See [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md).

Full page: [The states counterplan](example-states-cp.md).

## Agent

**The shape.** A different actor does the plan. For example, a different branch of government, or a different agency, acts instead of the one in the plan. The states counterplan is one kind of agent counterplan.

**Competition.** A net benefit tied to the plan's actor. For example, the plan's actor would spend its support or power, and the counterplan's actor would not.

**Usual aff answers.** Perm do both. The other actor cannot do it as well, or its action is easier to undo. The other actor links to the net benefit too.

**Theory, in one line.** Some aff teams argue that changing only the actor is unfair, and some neg teams say it tests whether the plan's actor is key. See [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md).

## Advantage

**The shape.** A different action that solves the aff's advantages. If the aff says the plan helps a problem, the counterplan fixes that problem another way. It may have several parts, one for each advantage. The [counterplan text](counterplan-text.md) lists each part.

**Competition.** Usually a net benefit: a disadvantage the plan causes and the counterplan does not. Without one, the perm usually solves. See [Net benefits](net-benefits.md).

**Usual aff answers.** Solvency deficits are the main answer: the different action misses part of the advantage. Perm do both. The counterplan links to the net benefit too.

**Theory, in one line.** Advantage counterplans draw less theory than the others. Some aff teams argue against counterplans with many parts the neg can drop one by one. See [Conditionality](conditionality.md).

## Process

**The shape.** The counterplan does the plan, or something close, but changes the process. For example, it adds a review step, a vote, a different way of passing, or a delay. The exact words of the [counterplan text](counterplan-text.md) decide what the step is, and what the perm can copy.

**Competition.** Often through **certainty** or **timing**. The plan happens for sure and at once. The counterplan happens later, or only if a step succeeds. Some process counterplans instead claim a net benefit from the process itself. The perm often gets that kind of benefit too. See [Net benefits](net-benefits.md).

**Usual aff answers.** Perm do both, or perm do the counterplan. Solvency deficits from delay or a failed step. Theory.

**Theory, in one line.** Many teams argue process counterplans are unfair because they do the whole plan, and others say they test how the plan should be done. See [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md).

## Consult

**The shape.** A kind of process counterplan. The government asks another party, such as a close ally or an international body, about the plan. It does the plan only if that party agrees.

**Competition.** Through certainty and timing: the plan acts at once and for sure, and the counterplan waits for an answer. The net benefit is usually better relations with the party that is asked.

**Usual aff answers.** Perm do both. The party may say no, or consultation takes too long. Theory.

**Theory, in one line.** Teams debate whether competing through certainty or timing is fair. See [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md).

Full page: [The consult counterplan](example-consult-cp.md).

## PIC (plan-inclusive counterplan)

**The shape.** The counterplan does most of the plan but leaves out one part. That part might be one place, one group, one action, or even one word in the plan text.

**Competition.** The left-out part is the source of the net benefit. The neg says that part of the plan causes harm, so doing the plan without it is better.

**Usual aff answers.** Perm do both. The left-out part is key to solving the case. The net benefit is small or untrue. Theory.

**Theory, in one line.** "PICs bad" is a common theory argument: some judges think PICs steal the aff's ground, and others think they test every part of the plan. See [Common theory arguments](common-theory.md).

Full page: [Plan-inclusive counterplans](example-pic.md).

## How to judge any common counterplan

1. **Theory first, if argued.** Decide any theory the teams extended into the last speeches.
2. **Competition.** Does the perm avoid the net benefit, as argued?
3. **Solvency.** How much of the case does the counterplan solve?
4. **Weigh.** Compare what the counterplan misses (the solvency deficit) with the net benefit. See [Solvency deficits](solvency-deficits.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic advantage counterplan with a spending net benefit. The middle speeches are left out to save space.

```
1NC                     2AC                     2NR                     2AR
CP: agency runs a       1. Solv deficit: CP     Training covers both    Training is slow;
   short training          misses the second       advantages (Analyst     adv 2 needs the
   program instead         advantage               22)                     new program now
NB: Spending DA -       2. Perm do both         Perm still costs the    Plan cost is small;
   plan costs $40B                                 full $40B               DA risk is tiny
                        3. No link: plan is     Ext link: $40B from     (no answer)
                           paid by normal          a fixed budget
                           means
```

*How to read it:* on solvency, the 2AC says the counterplan misses the second advantage. The 2NR says the training program covers both advantages. The 2AR says training is slow, and the second advantage needs the new program now. On the perm, the 2AC says do both. The 2NR says the perm still costs the full $40 billion, so it still links to the DA. The 2AR says the plan's cost is small, so the DA risk is tiny. On the link, the 2AC says "normal means" (the usual way the government pays for new programs) pays for the plan. The 2NR says the money comes from a fixed budget. The 2AR does not answer that. The judge must weigh the deficit on the second advantage against the risk of the spending DA.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: a counterplan with no net benefit.** Fix: pair every counterplan with a reason that doing both is worse.
- **Neg: a process or consult counterplan with no answer to the perm.** Fix: explain why doing both loses the certainty, timing or net benefit.
- **Aff: the same answers to every counterplan.** Fix: find the weak point for this shape. For an advantage counterplan, it is usually solvency. For a PIC, it is usually why the left-out part matters.
- **Aff: theory in one line, then a long 2AR on it.** Fix: develop theory in the 1AR if you plan to win on it.
- **Both: forgetting the net benefit is a DA.** Fix: answer and extend it like any DA.
- **Judges: treating one shape as always fair or always unfair.** Fix: decide the theory as argued in the round.
