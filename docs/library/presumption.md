# Presumption

> **Plain version.** Sometimes neither team gives the judge a good reason to vote for it. Presumption is the rule for that case. It says which team wins when nothing else decides the round. Most judges start by giving presumption to the negative, because the affirmative is asking for a change and has to prove the change is good. Some judges move presumption to the affirmative when the negative offers its own change, such as a counterplan. Teams can argue about this in the round.

## The basic idea

In a policy round, the aff asks the judge to support a change: the plan. The neg opposes it. If the aff cannot show that the plan does any good, why vote for it? The world without the plan, called the **status quo**, is already here. Changing it takes effort and carries risk.

That is the usual reason presumption goes to the neg. The aff carries the **burden of proof**: the duty to show its plan is a good idea. If the aff fails, the neg wins, even if the neg did not prove anything bad about the plan.

A simple comparison is a trial. The person accused does not have to prove they are innocent. The side making the claim has to prove it. In debate, the side asking for change is usually the side making the claim.

## When presumption comes up

Presumption matters only when the round has no clear winner on the issues. That happens most often when:

- **The neg wins that the plan does not solve.** If the plan does nothing, the aff has no advantage. The neg may have no disadvantage left either. Then neither side has a reason to vote for it, and presumption decides.
- **Every argument cancels out.** Each team wins **defense** against the other: reasons the other side's argument is wrong or small. But no one has **offense** left. Offense is an argument that gives the judge a reason to vote for your side, not just a reason the other side is wrong.
- **No stock issue is clearly won by either side** before a judge who treats stock issues as must-wins. If the aff clearly loses one, that is a plain neg win, not presumption. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).

In most rounds, presumption never decides anything. One team usually has at least some reason to vote for it. A small risk of an advantage, or a small risk of a disadvantage, is often enough to beat presumption.

## The common views

Judges and coaches do not all agree on presumption. Here are the common views, described neutrally.

### Neg presumption under the status quo

This is the most common default. If the neg defends the status quo, presumption goes to the neg. The aff must give a reason to change. Many judges use this default unless the teams argue for something else.

### Presumption and counterplans

A **counterplan** is a policy the neg offers instead of the plan. See [Counterplans](counterplans.md). When the neg goes for a counterplan, it is no longer defending the status quo. It is also asking for a change.

Some judges think this shifts presumption. A common version says presumption goes to **the side that asks for less change**. If the counterplan is a bigger change than the plan, presumption may go to the aff. If the counterplan is smaller, the neg may keep it. Other judges keep neg presumption no matter what, because the aff still has the burden of proof. Teams can argue about this, and should if it might decide the round.

### Judges who flip presumption

Some judges say in their paradigm that they "flip presumption" in certain cases. The most common is that presumption goes to the aff when the neg's last speech goes for a counterplan. Others say presumption goes to the less-change side. Some say they rarely vote on presumption at all, because they think there is almost always some risk of offense. Read the judge's paradigm. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

### Presumption in other kinds of rounds

Presumption also comes up when the aff does not defend a usual plan, such as some kritikal affirmatives (aff cases that question ideas instead of defending a government plan). A neg team may argue that the aff does not do anything a ballot can support, so the judge should vote neg on presumption. The aff answers by explaining what its method does and why that is a reason to vote for it. This debate is advanced, and judges differ a lot on it. See [Kritikal affirmatives](kritikal-affirmatives.md).

## How to use and answer presumption

**For the neg:**

- **Go for presumption only when you have won "no solvency"** or have taken out all of the aff's offense. Say it plainly: "The plan does not solve. There is no reason to change. Vote neg on presumption."
- **Back it up with a reason** the status quo is the safe default: change has costs and risks.
- **Keep a little offense if you can.** A small disadvantage risk gives the judge a reason to vote neg that does not depend on presumption alone.

**For the aff:**

- **Keep some offense alive.** Even a small, real advantage beats presumption for most judges.
- **Argue that any risk is enough.** "Even a small chance the plan helps is a reason to try it."
- **If the neg goes for a counterplan,** you can argue that presumption shifts to you, because the counterplan is also a change, and a bigger or riskier one.
- **Do not leave "no solvency" unanswered.** That is the most common way the aff ends up losing on presumption.

## How judges decide presumption

1. **First, check for offense.** Does either team have a reason to vote for it that survived the round? If yes, weigh it. Presumption does not come into play. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).
2. **If no one has offense, look for presumption arguments.** Did the teams argue who gets presumption? If so, decide that argument from the flow.
3. **If no one argued it, use your default,** and say so in your RFD (reason for decision). Most judges default to the neg. Some shift it when the neg goes for a counterplan.
4. **Explain it clearly.** "Neither team had offense left. The plan does not solve, so there was no reason to change. I vote neg on presumption."

## Example in flow shorthand

A case debate where the neg wins that the plan does not solve.

Key: Adv = advantage, SQ = status quo, Ext = extend (carry a point forward), "->" = "leads to", ">" = "beats."

```
1AC (aff):
  Adv: plan funds new clinics ->
    better care
1NC (neg):
  1. No solvency: the program is
    too small (Analyst 21)
  2. SQ already funds clinics
2AC (aff):
  Funds go to clinics (Expert 22)
  SQ funds are capped
2NR (neg):
  Ext #1: the program is too
    small; Expert 22 does not say
    how much
  Ext #2: no added clinics ->
    no adv
  No DA in 2NR. No solvency ->
    vote neg on presumption
2AR (aff):
  Some funds reach clinics ->
    some risk of better care.
    Any risk > presumption
```

*How to read it:* the 2NR has no disadvantage left, so it asks the judge to vote neg on presumption. The 2AR says some funds still reach clinics, so there is some risk of better care, and any risk beats presumption. The judge must decide: did the aff keep any real chance of an advantage? If yes, the aff wins. If not, the neg wins on presumption.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: going for presumption with offense still on the flow.** Fix: first win that the aff has no advantage left, then ask for presumption.
- **Neg: saying "presumption" without a reason.** Fix: explain why the status quo is the safe default when the plan does nothing.
- **Aff: dropping "no solvency."** Fix: always answer solvency attacks. Keep some chance that the plan helps.
- **Aff: missing the shift when the neg goes for a counterplan.** Fix: argue that the counterplan is also a change, and say who should get presumption.
- **Judges: voting on presumption when one team has a small risk of offense.** Fix: weigh the risk first. Presumption is for rounds where nothing else decides.
- **Judges: using a default without saying so.** Fix: name your default in your paradigm and in your RFD.
