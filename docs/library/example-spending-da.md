# The spending / trade-off DA

> **Plain version.** The spending disadvantage says a plan's cost has to come from somewhere.
>
> - **Why it fits any plan:** Almost every plan costs money or takes effort.
> - **The harm:** If the budget is tight, the plan's cost forces cuts to another program. People who rely on that program are hurt.
> - **The aff usually answers:** the budget is not that tight, the plan is cheap or saves money, or the plan's good outweighs the cuts.

## The generic shape

A **disadvantage** (DA) is a neg argument that the plan causes something bad. It has four parts. See [Disadvantages](disadvantages.md). Here is how the spending DA fills them in.

| Part | The spending DA says | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Uniqueness** | The budget is under control now | "The agency's budget is fixed for this year." |
| **Link** | The plan costs money | "The plan costs $20 billion from that budget." |
| **Internal link** | New costs force trade-offs | "To pay for the plan, other programs lose funds." |
| **Impact** | The cut program mattered | "Food aid is cut, and families go hungry." |

## Common versions

The same idea comes in a few forms.

- **Trade-off DA:** money or attention moves from one program to another inside the same budget or agency. "The plan pulls funds from disaster prep."
- **Deficit DA:** the plan adds to government debt, and debt harms the economy or forces later cuts.
- **Political trade-off:** the plan uses up lawmakers' time or support, so another bill fails. This version overlaps with [The politics DA](example-politics-da.md).

All three share one point: resources are limited, and the plan uses some of them.

## The usual 2AC answers

The **2AC** (second affirmative constructive) is the aff's first chance to answer. Common answers, by part:

- **Non-unique:** spending is rising anyway. Other bills, called **thumpers**, already add big costs. If those did not cause cuts, the plan will not either.
- **No link: the plan is cheap.** Show a cost that is small next to the budget.
- **No link: normal means.** Many plans say they are funded through "normal means," meaning however the government usually pays for new programs. The aff may argue this means new money, not cuts. The neg may argue normal means includes cuts. Teams debate what normal means includes.
- **Link turn: the plan saves money.** The plan pays for itself over time, for example by preventing costly harms.
- **No internal link:** the government can borrow, raise money or ask for more. A new cost does not have to force a cut.
- **No impact:** the program that would be cut is not that important, or would be cut anyway.
- **Case outweighs:** the plan's benefit is bigger than the harm of the cut.

Remember: do not link turn and impact turn the same DA. "The plan saves money" plus "the cut program is bad" says the plan prevents a good cut. That is a **double turn**. See [Links and link turns](links.md).

## How the neg extends it

In the neg block (the 2NC and 1NR), the neg usually:

1. **Defends uniqueness** with newer evidence, and answers each thumper.
2. **Proves the link size.** The bigger the cost, the more likely the cut.
3. **Explains the internal link.** Why must the cost come out of this program? Laws that require offsets (paying for new costs with cuts) are a common reason.
4. **Weighs.** Spending DAs often argue that the cuts are near-certain and fast, while the aff's benefit is slower or less sure.

The spending DA is also a common **net benefit** to a counterplan: a cheaper counterplan that solves the case avoids the cost. See [Counterplans](counterplans.md).

## How judges weigh it

- **Uniqueness and timing carry the most weight.** Budgets change every year. Judges look closely at the date of uniqueness evidence, if the teams raise it.
- **The internal link is the usual weak point.** Many judges ask: why this cut, and not borrowing or a different cut? A neg that explains this well often wins the DA.
- **Probability over size.** Spending impacts are often smaller but more likely than other DA impacts. They tend to win on probability and timeframe, not magnitude. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).
- **Some judges find generic spending DAs weak** unless the link is specific to the plan's cost. Others are happy to vote on them if the aff does not answer well. Both views are common.

For other DA shapes like this one, see [Common DA shapes](common-disadvantages.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic trade-off version. The middle speeches are left out to save space. Each block is one part of the DA, with each speech's line on it, top to bottom. Key: "N/U" means non-unique, "IL" means internal link, "$" means money, "$20B" means $20 billion, "=" means "is", and "->" means "leads to". Names in parentheses are invented cards.

```
DA: SPENDING TRADE-OFF

UNIQUENESS
1NC: Agency budget is fixed this
     year (Analyst 21).
2AC: 1. N/U: budget grows every
     year anyway.
2NR: Fixed this year; growth is
     next year.
2AR: Plan starts next year, when
     $ grows.

LINK
1NC: Plan costs $20B from same
     budget.
2AC: 2. No link: plan is paid by
     normal means, not cuts.
2NR: Normal means = this budget;
     no new $ in plan text.
2AR: Normal means: new programs
     get new $.

INTERNAL LINK
1NC: Other programs lose funds.
2AC: 3. No IL: agency can ask for
     more.
2NR: Requests take a year; cuts
     come first.
2AR: (no answer)

IMPACT
1NC: Food aid cut -> hunger.
2AC: 4. Case outweighs.
2NR: DA is sure and fast; case is
     slow.
2AR: Case helps more people.
```

*How to read it:* the 2AR does not answer "cuts come first" on the internal link. The judge must decide two things. When does the plan start? And what does "normal means" include?

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: no reason why this program gets cut.** Fix: explain the internal link, such as a law that requires offsets.
- **Neg: old budget evidence.** Fix: update uniqueness often. Budget facts change every year.
- **Aff: forgetting thumpers.** Fix: name other recent costs that did not cause the cut.
- **Aff: double-turning.** Fix: do not argue both "the plan saves money" and "the cut would be good."
- **Both: arguing "normal means" with no detail.** Fix: say what normal means includes, and why.
- **Judges: assuming every plan triggers cuts.** Fix: ask whether the neg proved this plan's cost forces this cut.
