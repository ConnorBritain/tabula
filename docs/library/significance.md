# Significance

> **Plain version.** Significance asks whether the problem, or the plan's fix, is big enough to matter.
>
> - **Why it matters:** A plan that fixes a tiny problem may not be worth the change.
> - **How to show it:** The affirmative uses numbers, such as how many people are hurt, or shows how serious the harm is, such as loss of life or rights.
> - **Where it fits:** Significance is one of the stock issues, the basic jobs of an affirmative case.
> - **How judges decide:** Many judges treat it as part of harms and solvency. Traditional judges often check it on its own.

## What significance is

**Significance** is about size. It asks: is this worth acting on? It has two sides:

- **Significance of the harm:** is the problem large or serious?
- **Significance of the solvency:** does the plan change enough of it to matter? (**Solvency** means the plan works.)

A huge problem that the plan barely touches may not be significant. A modest problem that the plan solves fully may be.

Significance is one of the five **stock issues**: harms, inherency, solvency, significance and topicality. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).

## Two ways to show significance

| Kind | What it measures | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Quantitative** | A number: people, money, events | "10,000 serious accidents a year (Analyst 21)." |
| **Qualitative** | How serious the harm is | "Each accident can mean a death or a lasting injury." |

"(Analyst 21)" names a **card**: a short quote from a source, with the author and year. The cards on this page are invented.

### Quantitative significance

A number makes significance easy to see. The aff should give a clear figure and a source. Bigger is not always better: a careful, believable number is stronger than a huge one with a weak source.

### Qualitative significance

Some harms matter even when the number is small. Loss of life, loss of a basic right, or unfair treatment by the government can be significant for even a few people. The aff explains why the kind of harm matters, not just the count.

Many cases use both. "10,000 accidents a year, and each one risks a life."

## Where significance fits

Significance overlaps with other ideas. That is why judges treat it in different ways.

- **With harms.** Many debaters prove significance inside the harm: "1 million people lack care" is both a harm and its size. See [Harms](harms.md).
- **With solvency.** The plan's effect must be large enough to count. "The plan cuts accidents by 40%" is a claim about significant solvency. See [Solvency](solvency.md).
- **With impact calculus.** Impact calculus is how judges weigh one side's results against the other's. In a policymaker round, significance is part of **magnitude**: how big an impact is when weighed against a **disadvantage** (a neg argument that the plan causes something bad). See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

**Traditional and stock issues judges** often expect significance to be named and proven as its own item. **Policymaker judges** (who weigh costs against benefits) usually fold it into weighing. If a judge's **paradigm** (their written view of how they judge) says "stock issues," label significance clearly. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

### Significance is not topicality's "substantial"

Some resolutions (topic statements) use a word like "substantially." That word is about the **plan**: does the plan make a large enough change to fit the topic? That is a **topicality** question, argued on its own **sheet** (page of notes). Significance is about the **harm** and the plan's effect on it, on the case sheet. The two can sound alike, so keep them apart. See [Topicality](topicality.md).

## How to answer significance (for the neg)

- **The harm is small:** the number is lower than the aff claims, or most cases are minor.
- **The fix is small:** the plan solves only a sliver of the harm, so the change is not worth it.
- **The number is weak:** the card is old, vague, or from a biased source.
- **No quality claim:** if the aff gives only a number, argue it does not show why the harm is serious.

In front of a stock issues judge, the neg can argue that a lack of significance alone is a reason to vote neg. In front of a policymaker, the neg usually uses it to make the **advantage** (the aff's reason to vote for the plan) small enough for a disadvantage to outweigh.

## How to defend significance (for the aff)

- **Give a number and a source** in the 1AC.
- **Add a quality claim**, so a smaller number still matters.
- **Show that partial solvency is still large.** "Even if we solve only 20%, that is 2,000 lives a year."
- **Answer the neg's evidence directly.** If they say most cases are minor, read a card about serious cases.

## How to judge significance

1. **Ask what rule the teams argued.** If they treat significance as a must-win stock issue, check it on its own. If they weigh costs and benefits, treat it as part of magnitude.
2. **Look at both sides: harm and solvency.** A big harm with tiny solvency may not be significant.
3. **Be careful about setting your own number.** There is no fixed line for "big enough." Many judges decide only from the arguments made in the round.
4. **Credit qualitative claims if they were argued.** A small number of serious harms can still be significant.

## Example in flow shorthand

A significance debate on a generic safety-check case, in front of a traditional judge. Each claim is shown with the speeches that argue it.

Key: Sig = significance, Qual = qualitative, Solv = solvency, Ext = extend (carry forward), Ev = evidence, K = thousand.

```
THE NUMBER
1AC  Sig: 10K serious
     accidents/yr
     (Analyst 21)
1NC  Most accidents are
     minor (Ev 22)
2AC  Ev counts serious
     only (Analyst 21)
2NR  Ext: 10K is all
     accidents
2AR  Card says "serious"
     - read it again

THE QUALITY
1AC  Qual: each risks a
     death
1NC  (no answer)
2AC  Ext: dropped
2NR  (no answer)
2AR  Qual sig conceded

THE FIX
1AC  Solv: cuts 40%
1NC  40% is small
2AC  40% = 4K serious
     accidents a year
2NR  (dropped)
2AR  40% answer dropped;
     4K a year is
     significant
```

*How to read it:* the neg went for only the number. The 2AR answers by pointing to the word "serious" in the card. The neg also dropped the quality claim and the 40% answer, so a traditional judge would likely find the aff proved significance.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: no number and no quality.** Fix: give both in the 1AC.
- **Aff: forgetting that solvency must be significant too.** Fix: say how much of the harm the plan fixes, in numbers if you can.
- **Neg: confusing significance with topicality's "substantially."** Fix: argue the size of the plan's change on the T (topicality) sheet, and the size of the harm on the case sheet.
- **Neg: "not significant" with no reason.** Fix: show the number is small, weak, or mostly minor cases.
- **Judges: using a personal cutoff for "big enough."** Fix: decide from the arguments, and say why in your decision.
