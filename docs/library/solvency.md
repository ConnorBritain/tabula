# Solvency

> **Plain version.** Solvency means the plan works. The affirmative must show that its plan fixes the problem, or at least makes it smaller. It usually does this with evidence from an expert who supports the plan's idea. Solvency is one of the stock issues, the basic jobs of an affirmative case. If the plan does not work, there is no reason to vote for it, however bad the problem is.

## What solvency is

**Solvency** is the link between the plan and the **harm** (the problem now). The aff says, "Here is a problem, and here is our plan. The plan fixes the problem." Solvency is the proof of that last step.

Solvency is one of the five **stock issues**: harms, inherency, solvency, significance and topicality. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).

Solvency does not need to be total. A plan that fixes half of a large problem can still be worth doing. What matters is how much of the harm the plan solves, and whether that is more than the costs the neg proves.

## The parts of a solvency argument

### The solvency advocate

A **solvency advocate** is a source (an expert, a study, or a report) that says the plan's kind of action would work. The best solvency advocate supports something close to the exact plan. The aff reads it as a **card**: a short quote with the author and year. "Funding rural clinics would cut untreated illness by half (Expert 22)." The cards on this page are invented.

Many judges expect the aff to have a solvency advocate. A plan that no expert supports is easy to attack.

### Matching the plan text

The solvency evidence must match the **plan text**, the one sentence that says what the plan does. Say the card is about funding and training, but the plan only funds. Then the neg can argue the plan does not do what the card needs. See [The affirmative case](affirmative-case.md).

### The mechanism

The **mechanism** is how the plan works, step by step. Who acts? With what power? How does the action reach the people who are hurt? A clear mechanism makes solvency easy to explain and hard to attack.

### Solvency for each advantage

An **advantage** is one reason the plan is good. A case with two advantages needs solvency for both. The plan might fix one harm well and the other only a little. The neg can attack each one on its own.

## How to answer solvency (for the neg)

Neg solvency arguments are **on-case** arguments: arguments directly against the aff's case. They go on the **case sheet** (the page of notes for the case), next to the aff's solvency.

Two words help here. **Defense** is a reason the other side's argument is weaker. **Offense** is a reason to vote for your side.

- **No solvency:** the plan does not work. Read a card that says the plan's approach fails.
- **Solvency takeout on the mechanism:** a step in the plan breaks. "There are no doctors to staff the new clinics."
- **Alternate causes** (often "alt causes"): the harm has other causes the plan does not touch. "Distance is not the only reason people skip care; cost matters too." This shrinks solvency, but rarely erases it.
- **Circumvention:** the people who must carry out the plan will avoid or undercut it. Under [fiat](fiat.md) (the rule that we imagine the plan passes), the plan is imagined to pass. But some judges let the neg argue how it would be carried out after that.
- **Plan text mismatch:** the plan does not do what the solvency card needs.
- **Solvency turn:** the plan makes the harm worse. This is offense, not just defense. See [Case turns and case defense](case-turns.md).

### Solvency deficits

The neg may run a **counterplan**: its own, different plan. Then the aff argues the counterplan does not solve as well as the plan. That gap is called a **solvency deficit**. It is a different idea from case solvency. It compares the plan and the counterplan, instead of asking if the plan works at all. See [Solvency deficits](solvency-deficits.md).

## How to defend solvency (for the aff)

- **Read a strong solvency advocate.** Use a source that supports the plan's action, ideally in the same words.
- **Explain the mechanism.** Walk the judge through how the plan works.
- **Answer alt causes with "the plan still helps."** Even if other causes exist, the plan solves the part it targets.
- **Answer circumvention with enforcement.** Point to what in the plan makes it happen: funding, a deadline, or oversight.
- **Use "risk of solvency."** If the neg only casts doubt, argue that some chance the plan works is still worth more than the **status quo** (the world now, without the plan).

## How to judge solvency

1. **Ask how much the plan solves.** Most neg solvency arguments shrink the advantage. Few erase it. Weigh what is left.
2. **Check that the evidence matches the plan.** The neg may point out a gap between the plan text and the solvency card. If the aff does not answer, that matters.
3. **Watch for zero solvency.** If the neg proves the plan does nothing, the aff's advantages are gone. Some judges then vote neg on **presumption**: with no reason to change, they keep the status quo. Teams can argue whether presumption applies. See [Presumption](presumption.md).
4. **Use the paradigm the teams argue.** A **stock issues judge** asks whether the aff proved solvency at all. A **policymaker judge** (who weighs costs against benefits) asks how much the plan solves compared to the costs.

Most judges ask whether a team *won* the solvency debate, not whether any doubt was raised.

## Example in flow shorthand

A solvency debate on a generic rural clinic case. The plan funds new clinics, staff training and free care, with oversight and a deadline. Each neg attack is shown with the speeches that argue it.

Key: Solv = solvency, Ev = evidence, Ext = extend (carry forward), yrs = years.

```
1AC  Plan: fund new
     clinics, staff
     training, free care;
     oversight and a
     deadline
     Solv: new clinics cut
     untreated illness by
     half (Expert 22)

ATTACK 1 (no staff)
1NC  Rural doctor
     shortage (Ev 22)
2AC  Plan funds training;
     staff in 2 yrs
     (Author 23)
2NR  Training takes years;
     gap stays
2AR  Plan pays now; staff
     in 2 yrs

ATTACK 2 (alt cause)
1NC  Cost stops care too
2AC  Plan clinics are free
     to patients
2NR  (dropped)
2AR  Conceded: cost is
     solved

ATTACK 3 (circumvention)
1NC  States slow funds
2AC  Plan has oversight
     and deadline
2NR  Ext: oversight is
     weak (no card)
2AR  Oversight is in plan
     text
```

*How to read it:* the aff answered each attack by pointing to what the plan already does. The main clash is timing: does the staff gap last years, or close in two? A judge would likely give the aff some solvency, then weigh it against the neg's other arguments.

## Common mistakes

- **Aff: no solvency advocate.** Fix: find a source that supports the plan's kind of action, and read it in the 1AC.
- **Aff: a plan text that does less than the card.** Fix: write the plan so it does what the solvency evidence needs.
- **Aff: dropping solvency in the 2AR.** Fix: extend how the plan works, not just the harm.
- **Neg: "they do not solve" with no reason.** Fix: name the broken step, and read a card if you can.
- **Neg: arguing the plan "would never pass."** Fix: under fiat, most judges will not count this. Argue that it would not work once in place.
- **Judges: treating doubt as zero solvency.** Fix: weigh how much of the advantage is left.
