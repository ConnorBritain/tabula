# Judging your first round

> **Plain version.** You do not need debate experience to judge a fair round.
>
> - **Your job:** Listen, take notes, and decide which team made the better case, based only on what they said. Do not add your own opinions about the topic.
> - **Students will help:** Students are trained to explain things to judges like you.
> - **You are needed:** Many tournaments rely on parent and community judges, and those judges are welcome and needed.

## The round in one minute

- **Two teams of two debate.** The **aff** (affirmative) argues for a plan, a change the government should make. The **neg** (negative) argues against it.
- **They take turns giving speeches.** Each team also asks the other team questions.
- **The last two speeches matter most.** The **2NR** is the neg's last speech. The **2AR** is the aff's last speech, and it is the final speech of the round.
- **Your notes are called the flow.** You write down what each speech says, in columns.

For the full order of speeches, see [Debate 101](debate-101.md).

## What to bring

- **Paper and two pens** in different colors, or a laptop. (Tabula is free and keeps your notes on your own computer.)
- **A watch or phone timer.** Debaters usually time themselves, but you should time too.
- **Water and a snack.** Rounds take about 90 minutes.
- **Your judge code or login**, if the tournament uses an online ballot.

## Before the round

1. **Check in** at the judge table, or online. You will get your room and the two teams.
2. **Get to the room on time.** The round cannot start without you.
3. **Keep the door open,** or follow the tournament's rules about adults and students in rooms.
4. **Tell the debaters you are new**, if you like. Something like: "This is my first round. Please speak clearly, and explain your arguments." That is a normal request. Good debaters will adjust.
5. **Ask about the speech documents.** Many teams share their evidence by email or a shared folder. You can ask to be included. It helps you follow along. You do not have to read every word.
6. **Ask who is keeping time**, and how much **prep time** (planning time between speeches) each team gets.

If something goes wrong at any time, such as rude or unsafe behavior, a team that will not stop, or evidence that seems made up, report it to the tournament staff. You do not have to handle it alone. See [Round conduct, safety and evidence ethics](round-conduct.md).

## During the round

- **Take notes on each speech.** Use one column per speech. Write a few words per argument. Even rough notes help a lot. See [How to flow](how-to-flow.md).
- **Listen for what each team says is most important.** The 2NR and 2AR should tell you.
- **Stay neutral in your face.** Try not to nod or frown at arguments.
- **Do not argue with the debaters.** You can ask them to slow down or speak up. Say "clear" if a speaker is too fast to understand.
- **Do not decide early.** Rounds often change in the last speeches.

## After the round: deciding

Take a few minutes alone. Then ask yourself:

1. What was the main fight in the last two speeches?
2. Which team explained its side of that fight better, with reasons?
3. Did either team ignore an important argument from the other side?
4. Which team told you how to compare the arguments? Did that make sense?

Vote for the team that won the most important issue. If it is close, trust your notes over your feelings.

**What not to vote on:** your own view of the policy, which school the team is from, how the team is dressed, or which team you liked more as people.

### An example

Here is a tiny piece of a judge's notes at decision time. On paper, each speech gets its own column; here they are stacked to fit a phone. "$" means money, and "->" means "leads to".

```
2NR (neg): plan costs $
  -> cuts to schools now
2AR (aff): plan saves $ by yr 3
  (neg never answered)
Judge: main fight = cost.
  Aff wins cost. Vote AFF.
```

*How to read it:* the neg never answered the aff's "savings by year 3" point. So the judge voted aff, and wrote that reason in the RFD (see below).

## Filling out the ballot

The ballot has three parts. See [The ballot and the RFD](ballot-and-rfd.md) for more.

1. **The decision:** circle or click the winning team, aff or neg.
2. **Speaker points:** a score for each debater, often on a scale near 25 to 30. A solid, clear speech is about a 28, and most debaters land between 27 and 29. Save very low points for rude or unsafe behavior. See [Speaker points](speaker-points.md).
3. **The RFD** (reason for decision): two to five sentences on why you voted as you did. Name the issue and the reason.

**If you were confused,** say so kindly in your RFD, and decide from what you did understand. For example: "I did not understand how the counterplan (the neg's other idea) was different from the plan. The aff explained the plan's benefits more clearly, so I voted aff." That is useful feedback. It teaches the debaters to explain better.

**Be kind in feedback.** Comment on arguments and speaking, never on a student's looks, voice or identity.

Some tournaments ask you to tell the debaters your decision out loud. Others do not. Follow the tournament's rule.

## Common mistakes

- **Voting on your own opinion of the topic.** Decide only from what the debaters said.
- **Not taking notes.** A few words per argument is enough.
- **Deciding in the first speech.** Wait for the last two speeches. They often change things.
- **An RFD that says only "good job."** Name the issue that decided the round, and why.
- **Very low speaker points for small mistakes.** Keep low points for rude or unsafe behavior.
