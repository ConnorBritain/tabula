# Common kritik families

> **Plain version.** A kritik, or K, questions the ideas behind the affirmative's case. Many kritiks fall into a few well-known families. This page gives a short, fair summary of each: its claim, its usual link, what it offers instead, and how affirmative teams answer. Judges hold strong and different views about these arguments, and this page does not rank them.

## Before you read

**This is a debate-round summary, not a full account.** Each family draws on a large body of thought, and scholars inside each tradition disagree. The goal is to help you follow the argument in a round.

**Each core claim is stated as its advocates would put it**, and each aff answer as aff teams would put it. Neither is this library's view. The families are in alphabetical order, not ranked.

**Judges hold strong, different views.** Some know these traditions well. Some are skeptical of kritiks. Some rounds involve debaters' own experiences. Judge every round with the same care, on the argument made.

## What every family shares

Every kritik has the same basic parts. See [Kritiks](kritiks.md).

- **Link:** what the aff does, says or assumes that the K criticizes. See [The kritik link](kritik-links.md).
- **Impact:** why that thinking is harmful.
- **Alternative (alt):** what the neg offers instead. See [The alternative](kritik-alternative.md).
- **Framework:** how the judge should weigh the K against the plan. See [Framework](framework.md). Some teams add a [role of the ballot](role-of-the-ballot.md): what the vote should do.

Most aff answers are also shared: framework, a **permutation** (do the plan and the alt together), no link, alt fails, case outweighs, and sometimes an impact turn. See [Answering a kritik](answering-kritiks.md). Aff teams can also build a [kritikal affirmative](kritikal-affirmatives.md) from these families.

## Ableism and disability critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that society treats some bodies and minds as "normal" and others as broken. They call this ableism. In their view, disability comes as much from how society is built as from any one body.

**Typical link.** The aff uses disability as a word for harm, such as "crippling." Or its impacts treat disability as close to death.

**Typical alternative.** Reject the ableist framing, and center the accounts of disabled people.

**Common aff answers.** No link: the word was not central. A perm: do the plan and drop the framing. The plan helps disabled people too.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because the framing shaped the plan's goals. The aff usually argues it works because the plan can stand without the framing.

## Anti-blackness critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that anti-Black racism is not a leftover from the past. They say it is built into society's structure and its ideas about who counts as fully human. Some versions doubt that reform can end it. Others draw hope from Black thought and resistance.

**Typical link.** The plan relies on the state to fix a harm that advocates say the state helps produce. Or the aff tells a story of steady progress.

**Typical alternative.** Some alts refuse the terms of the current order. Others center Black thought.

**Common aff answers.** A perm. The plan helps Black communities now. Past reforms show real progress.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because reform through the state repeats the harm. The aff usually argues it works because the plan's help does not block the alt.

## Biopolitics critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that modern governments do not only make laws. They also manage populations: they count and sort people, and decide whose lives are protected.

**Typical link.** The plan collects data on people or sorts them by risk. Or the aff frames its impact as the population's survival.

**Typical alternative.** Refuse the aff's management frame, and reject ranking whose lives matter more.

**Common aff answers.** Management saves lives. A perm with limits on how the plan sorts people. No link to this plan's design.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because any management of a population still sorts. The aff usually argues it works because limits keep the benefits without the sorting. The example below shows this fight.

## Capitalism critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that an economy built on profit is not neutral. It puts profit ahead of human need. They say market reforms fix one harm but keep the system that causes it.

**Typical link.** The plan uses market tools, such as tax credits. Or the aff treats economic growth as good.

**Typical alternative.** Reject market logic, and support groups that organize work around need.

**Common aff answers.** A perm. No link. Alt fails: no clear path to replace markets. Some affs impact turn: markets have reduced poverty.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because the plan makes the market system stronger. The aff usually argues it works because a plan and a broader movement can happen together. A full walkthrough is on [The cap K](example-cap-k.md).

## Feminist and gender critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that ideas about gender shape politics. Traits coded as masculine, such as strength and control, are treated as the mark of good policy, and care is treated as secondary.

**Typical link.** The aff frames the problem as a need for strength or protection. Or it ignores how the plan affects people differently by gender.

**Typical alternative.** A feminist analysis that puts gendered experience at the center, often with an ethic of care.

**Common aff answers.** A perm: do the plan with a gender analysis. No link. The plan helps women and gender minorities too.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because a logic of strength still drives the plan. The aff usually argues it works because a gender analysis can sit alongside the plan.

## Psychoanalytic critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that political choices are shaped by desires and fears that people do not fully see. People want a wholeness they can never reach, and politics turns that lack into a fantasy: defeat this one enemy, and all will be well.

**Typical link.** The aff promises a complete solution. Or it names a threat whose removal would bring safety.

**Typical alternative.** Accept that the lack cannot be filled, instead of chasing it. Debaters call this "traversing the fantasy."

**Common aff answers.** Psychoanalysis was built to treat individuals, not whole societies. A perm. The alt has no concrete result.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because the plan still acts out the fantasy. The aff usually argues it works because the plan can drop the promise and keep its modest benefits.

## Security critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that calling another country a threat is not a neutral report. Threat talk spreads fear, justifies military action, and can bring about the conflict it predicts.

**Typical link.** The aff says a rival country is a danger that must be contained, or a war that only the plan can stop.

**Typical alternative.** Reject the threat framing, and approach other countries without seeing threats first.

**Common aff answers.** Some threats are real. A perm: do the plan without the threat framing. The alt leaves real dangers unanswered.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because the plan needs the threat to make sense. The aff usually argues it works because the plan still solves without the threat framing. See the example on [Kritiks](kritiks.md).

## Settler colonialism critiques

**Core claim.** Advocates argue that in settler countries, land was taken from Indigenous peoples, and the settlers stayed. This is an ongoing structure: the settler state still claims the land. In this view, decolonization means returning land.

**Typical link.** The plan uses the settler government to manage land or people, and treats its authority as a given.

**Typical alternative.** Return of land and recognition of Indigenous **sovereignty** (the right of a people to govern itself).

**Common aff answers.** A perm. The plan helps people now, including Indigenous people. The alt does not say how land return happens.

**Judging note.** The neg usually argues the perm fails because a plan that keeps settler control cannot be combined with decolonization. The aff usually argues it works because the plan's help now does not stop land return later.

## Other families

Teams also run kritiks about ecology, religion, how the aff describes other regions, and debate itself. Some negs combine families. The same parts apply: find the link, the impact and the alt, and judge how well each was explained.

## How to judge any K family

1. **Decide framework first.** It tells you what counts.
2. **Ask for the link in this round.** What did this aff do or say?
3. **Ask what the alt does.** Use the neg's explanation, not your own.
4. **Check the perm.** Can the aff do both? If not, why not?
5. **Weigh.** Compare the K and its alt to the case.

You do not need to know the tradition behind a K.

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic biopolitics K against an aff that tracks health data to send care where it is needed. The block and 1AR are left out.

```
1NC                     2AC                     2NR                      2AR
K: Biopolitics
L: plan tracks health   1. No link: the data    CX: data is sorted by    Sorting sends care
   data to sort people     is anonymous            risk group; that is      where it is needed
   (Theorist 17)                                   the link
I: sorting decides      2. Case outweighs:      K turns case: groups     Case helps those
   whose life counts       program saves lives     marked low value get     groups first (1AC ev)
                                                   less care
Alt: refuse the aff's   3. Perm: do the plan    Perm still sorts;        Data limits stop the
   management frame        with data limits        links are DAs to it      sorting; perm solves
```

*How to read it:* row 1: the 1NC says the plan uses health data to sort people, and the 2AC says the data is anonymous. The 2NR uses a CX answer: the data is sorted by risk group. The 2AR says that sorting sends care where it is needed. Row 2: the 1NC says sorting decides whose life counts, and the 2AC says the case outweighs. The 2NR says the K turns the case, because groups marked low value get less care. The 2AR says the case helps those groups first. Row 3: the 1NC alt refuses the management frame, and the 2AC perms with data limits. The 2NR says the perm still sorts, and the 2AR says data limits stop the sorting. The round turns on the perm.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: reading a family's generic link to every aff.** Fix: apply it to this aff's words, plan or evidence.
- **Neg: assuming the judge knows the tradition.** Fix: explain the core claim in plain words.
- **Aff: answering the family, not the K that was read.** Fix: answer the link, impact and alt the neg read.
- **Aff: an impact turn that denies a harm is real.** Fix: dispute whether the alt or the K's theory helps, not whether people are hurt. Impact turns on the system, as in cap good, are a different thing.
- **Both: caricaturing the other side's view.** Fix: state its best version, then answer it.
- **Judges: deciding on your view of a family.** Fix: decide on the arguments made, and say which one decided it.
