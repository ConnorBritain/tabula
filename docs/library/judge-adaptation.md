# Adapting to your judge

> **Plain version.** Every judge decides a little differently. Some want slow, plain speeches. Some can follow very fast speech and know every argument type. Adapting means changing how you debate so this judge can follow you and see why you win. You change your speed, your words, how many arguments you run and how much you explain. You do not change what is true or fair.

## Why adaptation matters

A judge can only vote on what they understand. The best argument in the round does not help you if the judge did not catch it. Adapting is how you make sure your best arguments reach the judge.

It is also a real skill. Outside debate, you will need to persuade people with different backgrounds: a teacher, a boss, a city council. Debate gives you practice with many kinds of listeners.

## Step 1: Read the paradigm

A **paradigm** is a judge's short note on how they decide. Many judges post one online before the tournament. Read it before the round, or as soon as you know your judge. Look for:

- **speed:** how fast they can follow;
- **defaults:** what they do if no one argues a point, such as how to weigh impacts;
- **argument views:** how they feel about topicality, counterplans, kritiks and theory;
- **lines:** behavior they will punish, such as rudeness or stealing prep;
- **the email chain:** whether they want the speech docs.

See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md) for the common types, and [Writing a paradigm](writing-a-paradigm.md) for what judges are asked to include.

If there is no paradigm, ask your coach or teammates whether anyone has had this judge.

## Step 2: Ask a short question before the round

If the tournament allows it, ask one or two quick questions while you set up. Good questions are specific and easy to answer:

- "How fast can you follow?"
- "Do you want to be on the email chain?"
- "Is there anything you want us to know about how you decide?"

Do not quiz the judge or ask for a lecture. A new parent judge may not know the terms. Keep it simple and polite.

## Step 3: Adjust the parts you control

You can change many things without changing your arguments.

| What to adjust | For a judge who wants it plain | For a judge with a lot of experience |
|---|---|---|
| **Speed** | Normal talking pace | Faster, if they say so, but always clear |
| **Words** | Plain words; explain each term once | Shorthand terms are fine |
| **Number of arguments** | Fewer, stronger arguments | More, if you can cover them well |
| **Explanation** | Tell a simple story: problem, plan, result | Focus on the key clash and the warrants |
| **Weighing** | Say in plain words why your side matters more | Use impact calculus terms and compare directly |
| **Evidence** | Explain what each card proves in your own words | Point to key lines in the card |

Some things help with every judge: a clear roadmap, signposts ("On the disadvantage..."), and plain weighing in the last speeches. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

## Step 4: Choose arguments the judge can vote on

Adaptation also shapes which arguments you run and which you go for at the end.

- **A judge who is new to debate** may find a complex theory argument or kritik hard to follow. If you run one, explain it in plain words, and make sure you have a simpler path to win too.
- **A judge who values stock issues** wants to hear harms, inherency and solvency by name. See [Stock issues](stock-issues.md).
- **A judge who leans one way** on a big question, such as kritiks or conditionality, can often still be persuaded. But it takes more explaining. Plan for that, or choose a different argument.

Some judges say "I will vote on anything." Even then, an argument they understand well is safer than one they have to piece together.

## Step 5: Watch the judge during the round

Judges give signals. You can use them.

- **The judge stops writing.** You may be too fast, or repeating yourself. Slow down or move on.
- **The judge looks confused.** Explain the point again in simpler words.
- **The judge says "clear" or "slow."** Change at once. Many judges stop flowing if you do not.
- **If the judge shows reactions,** such as nodding or writing a lot on one argument, that argument may be landing. You may want to spend time on it in your rebuttal.

Do not read too much into one look. Some judges keep a neutral face on purpose.

## Panels: more than one judge

In some rounds, three or more judges decide together. Each one votes, and the team with more votes wins. With a panel:

- **Usually, speak so the least experienced judge can follow,** while still giving the experienced judges your warrants.
- **Explain your terms once,** briefly, then use them.
- **Make your weighing plain.** It has to work for every judge on the panel.

## Adapting without giving up your integrity

Adapting is not pretending. You should not:

- argue things you know are false;
- change your evidence or overstate what a card says;
- drop respect for the other team to please a judge.

You can still run the arguments you believe in. Adaptation is about how you present them, and which ones you choose to go for, not about hiding who you are. Many coaches say the best debaters can win the same argument in front of any judge, by changing how they explain it.

## Example in flow shorthand

The same 2AC answer to a spending disadvantage, given two ways. The first is for a judge who wants it plain. The second is for an experienced judge who can follow fast speech.

```
Plain-judge version                       Experienced-judge version
"Their argument says the plan costs       1. L/T: plan saves $ by yr 3
too much money. But our evidence shows       (Author 22)
the plan saves money by year three
(Author 22). So it does not take money
away from schools."
"Even if it did cost money, our plan      2. Case outweighs: lives now >
helps people right away, and that            budget risk later
matters more than a budget risk later."
```

*How to read it:* both versions make the same two answers. The first answer is a link turn: the plan saves money by year three, from the Author 22 card. The second answer is weighing: the case helps people now, which matters more than a later budget risk. The plain version uses full sentences and states the argument in plain words, without the label "link turn." The experienced version uses numbers and shorthand. The arguments do not change. Only the way they are said changes.

## Common mistakes

- **Not reading the paradigm.** Fix: read it before every round, even for a judge you have had before. Paradigms change.
- **Talking down to a lay judge.** Fix: be clear, not condescending. Explain once, briefly, and move on.
- **Ignoring "clear" or "slow."** Fix: change your pace right away.
- **Running too many arguments for the judge.** Fix: cut to the strongest few, and explain them well.
- **Changing your argument's substance to please the judge.** Fix: change how you present it, not what is true.
- **Adapting only in the first speech.** Fix: keep adapting in the rebuttals, where the judge decides. See [Rebuttal strategy](rebuttal-strategy.md).
