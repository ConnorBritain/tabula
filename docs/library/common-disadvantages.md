# Common disadvantage shapes

> **Plain version.** A disadvantage, or DA, says the plan causes something bad. The topic changes every year, but a few kinds of DA come back every year, because they fit almost any plan. Most plans cost money, use up political support, affect the economy, set an example, or change how other countries see us. Each of those is a common DA. If you know these shapes, you can follow most DA debates on any topic.

## Why the same DAs come back

A **disadvantage** has four parts: uniqueness (the harm is not happening now), link (the plan starts it), internal link (the steps from the plan to the harm) and impact (why the harm matters). See [Disadvantages](disadvantages.md) for the full chain.

Some DAs link to a feature that almost every plan has. Almost every plan costs money. Almost every plan needs political support to pass. Almost every plan changes something in the economy. So the neg can prepare these DAs once and use them against many plans. Debaters call these **generic** DAs, because the link is general, not about one plan.

Generic does not mean bad. A generic DA with a strong, plan-specific link explanation can win. But a generic link is also easier for the aff to answer with "no link." The best neg teams find a reason that *this* plan triggers the DA.

## The shapes at a glance

| Shape | The link in one line | The impact is often |
|---|---|---|
| **Politics** | The plan costs a leader political support | Another priority fails |
| **Economy** | The plan slows growth | Economic harm, or conflict |
| **Spending / trade-off** | The plan costs money or attention | Cuts to another program |
| **Spillover / precedent** | The plan sets an example that spreads | More actions of the same kind |
| **Relations** | The plan angers another country | Lost cooperation on other issues |
| **Federalism** | Federal action takes power from the states | States lose their role |

Each is described below. Three have full example pages.

## Politics

**What it says.** Leaders have limited **political capital**: the goodwill and favors they use to win votes. Pushing the plan spends some of it. Then the leader has too little left for another priority, and that priority fails.

**Usual answers.** The priority will fail or pass anyway (non-unique). Other fights already cost capital (a **thumper**, an event now that should already trigger the link). The plan is popular. Winning a hard fight builds capital ("winners win," a link turn). Capital does not decide votes. Fiat means the plan passes with no fight.

**Judging note.** Uniqueness changes fast, so the newest evidence often matters, and many judges ask whether capital really decides the priority. See [The politics DA](example-politics-da.md).

## Economy

**What it says.** The plan raises costs, adds rules or creates doubt for businesses. That slows growth. A weaker economy leads to harm: lost jobs, less money for public goods, or, in many versions, more conflict.

**Usual answers.** The economy is weak anyway (non-unique). The economy is too strong for this plan to move it (a link-size answer). The plan is too small to change the whole economy. The plan helps growth (link turn). The economy is resilient and recovers from shocks (no internal link). Economic decline does not cause conflict (impact defense). Some teams argue that growth itself has harms (an impact turn).

**Judging note.** The step from "growth slows" to the final impact is often long, so judges look hard at each internal link. See [The economy DA](example-economy-da.md).

## Spending / trade-off

**What it says.** The plan costs money, time or attention. If the budget or the agency is stretched, the plan's cost forces cuts to something else, and people who rely on that program are hurt. That harm to people is the **impact**: the reason the DA matters. See [Impacts](impacts.md).

**Usual answers.** Spending is rising anyway, and other costs did not cause cuts. The plan is cheap. The plan pays for itself (link turn). The government can borrow or ask for more money. "Normal means" (the usual way the government pays for new programs) gives the plan new money.

**Judging note.** The internal link is often the weak point: why must the cost come out of *this* program? See [The spending DA](example-spending-da.md). This DA is also a common **net benefit** to a counterplan that costs less: a reason the counterplan alone is better than doing both. See [Net benefits](net-benefits.md).

## Spillover / precedent

**What it says.** The plan does more than its own job. It sets a **precedent**: an example that others follow. Once the government acts this way here, it, or other actors, will act the same way in other areas. Those later actions cause the harm.

**Usual answers.** There is already a precedent like this, so the plan adds nothing new (non-unique). The plan is narrow and will not be copied. Future actors decide on their own, case by case (no internal link). The plan sets a good precedent instead (link turn).

**Judging note.** The neg must explain who copies the plan and why. A precedent claim with no actor and no reason is often weak, and judges notice.

## Relations

**What it says.** The plan changes how another country sees us. A rival or an ally reacts badly, so relations decline. Then the two sides cooperate less on another issue, and that loss leads to the impact.

**Usual answers.** Relations are already bad, or are strong enough to survive one dispute. The other country does not care about the plan, or likes it (link turn). Countries cooperate where their interests meet, whatever the mood (no internal link). The lost cooperation would not cause the impact.

**Judging note.** Relations evidence dates fast, like politics evidence. Judges often ask whether the other country would truly react to this plan.

## Federalism

**What it says.** Federal action in an area takes power from the states. States lose their role as places that try new ideas, or the balance between federal and state power shifts. That shift causes the harm.

**Usual answers.** The federal government already acts in this area (non-unique). The plan works with the states (no link). The balance is stable and one law will not shift it. The harm is small.

**Judging note.** This DA is most often the net benefit to the states counterplan, which avoids the federal action. See [The states counterplan](example-states-cp.md).

## How to judge any common DA

1. **Check the link to this plan.** Does the neg explain why this plan triggers the DA? Or only why plans in general do?
2. **Check the dates.** Politics, economy and relations evidence change fast. If a team points out the dates, weigh it.
3. **Follow each internal link step.** Many common DAs have long chains. See [Internal links](internal-links.md).
4. **Weigh the risk.** Many judges give a small risk some weight. Others treat a very small risk as near zero. Go by the weighing as argued. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic relations DA. The middle speeches are left out to save space.

```
   on joint program        together on shared      programs
                           interests anyway
I: joint program ends   4. Case outweighs       DA is faster than       Case is bigger
   -> harm                                         case                    and more likely
```

*How to read it:* on uniqueness, the 2AC says ties are already strained. The 2NR reads newer evidence that ties improved this year. The 2AR says that evidence is about one meeting. On the link, the 2AC reads a link turn: the ally wants the plan. The 2NR says the ally's leaders oppose it in public. The 2AR extends the link turn and says the ally's public wants it. On the internal link, the 2AC says the two sides cooperate on shared interests anyway. The 2NR says trust is key to joint programs, and the 2AR does not answer that. On the impact, the 2NR says the DA is faster, and the 2AR says the case is bigger and more likely. The judge must decide whether the ally opposes or supports the plan, and then weigh timing against size.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg: a generic link with no plan-specific story.** Fix: explain why this plan, not plans in general, triggers the DA.
- **Neg: old uniqueness on a fast-moving DA.** Fix: update politics, economy and relations evidence before each tournament.
- **Aff: answering every common DA with the same block.** Fix: find the weak step in this DA. It is often the internal link.
- **Aff: link turn plus impact turn on one DA.** Fix: that is a double turn. Pick one kind of turn per DA. See [Links and link turns](links.md).
- **Both: no weighing.** Fix: compare the DA's risk to the case in time, size and probability.
- **Judges: dismissing a DA because it is generic.** Fix: judge the link as argued. A generic DA that the aff does not answer well can still win.
