# Extinction vs structural violence: a weighing debate

> **Plain version.** Some rounds end with one team claiming a very large harm that may be unlikely, such as a war that could end human life, and the other team claiming a harm that is happening now, every day, to real people. The first is often called an extinction impact. The second is often called structural violence: harm built into how a society works. Each team argues that its harm should count more, and that the other team's estimate of size or chance is wrong. Neither kind wins by default. The judge decides based on the weighing the teams give.

## The two kinds of impact

An **impact** is the result at the end of an argument, the reason it matters. See [Impacts](impacts.md).

An **extinction impact** says the harm could end human life, or come close. Common generic shapes are a war between large powers, a disease that spreads worldwide, or the collapse of a system that people need to survive. These impacts usually sit at the end of a long chain of steps. For example: the plan causes an economic shock, the shock raises tension between rivals, and the tension leads to a war that escalates.

**Structural violence** is a term from the social sciences. It means harm that comes from how a society is set up, not from one event or one person's choice. Examples include poverty, unequal access to health care, or unequal treatment by institutions. People die younger or live with less freedom because of these conditions. The harm is often quiet. It does not make headlines, but it happens every day.

Either team can claim either kind. An aff advantage can be about reducing structural harm, and a neg disadvantage (DA) can end in extinction. The reverse also happens. Both kinds describe real human suffering. Good debaters, and good judges, treat both with care.

## The case for the extinction impact

Teams that weigh for extinction usually make these arguments.

- **Magnitude.** Extinction affects everyone alive and every person who would ever live. No other harm is that large.
- **Irreversibility.** Most harms can be fixed later, at least in part. Extinction cannot. There is no recovery and no second chance.
- **Risk times size.** Even a small chance of a huge harm can be worth more than a sure, smaller harm. Debaters often call this **expected value**: multiply the chance by the size.
- **It comes first in a sense.** If no one survives, no one can work on structural harms either. So preventing extinction protects the chance to fix everything else.
- **Speed.** Some extinction scenarios, such as a war that escalates, could happen fast once they start.
- **The plan must fix it.** A harm happening now still needs proof that this plan reduces it. If the plan's effect on the structural harm is small or uncertain, that impact shrinks too.
- **Root-cause claims are chains too.** "This harm causes conflict" has steps, and each step needs evidence, just like an extinction chain.

## The case for the structural impact

Teams that weigh for structural violence usually make these arguments.

- **Probability.** The harm is not a prediction. It is happening now, and the evidence describes it, not guesses at it.
- **Timeframe.** The harm is ongoing. Every day without action adds to it. An extinction scenario may be years away, if it comes at all.
- **Long chains are weak.** Each step in an extinction chain may say "could" or "might." Each weak step lowers the real chance. A four-step chain of "could" may leave a very small risk.
- **The harm adds up.** A structural harm hurts many people over a long time. Added together, the total can be very large.
- **Root cause.** Some teams argue that structural harms, such as poverty or unequal treatment, help cause the conflicts that the other team fears. If so, the structural impact may **turn** the extinction impact: fixing one helps prevent the other. See [Turns case](turns-case.md).
- **What we choose to see.** Some teams argue that always prioritizing rare, dramatic events teaches us to ignore harm that happens every day.

## How framework and ethics claims enter

Often the weighing debate is not only about numbers. Teams may argue about the rule for weighing itself. This is sometimes called **impact framing**. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

- **Probability first vs magnitude first.** One team argues that very small risks should count as almost zero. The other argues that any real risk of the worst harm must count.
- **Consequences vs other ethical views.** Many teams use a **utilitarian** frame: judge by results, and count all lives equally. Others argue for different ethical views. For example: we have duties that results do not override, or we should give priority to the people who are worst off. Each view can change which impact wins.
- **Kritiks.** A **kritik** (K) questions the ideas behind an argument. Some kritiks argue that extinction-style predictions are a way of thinking that justifies harm now in the name of avoiding a far-off disaster. The answering team often argues that predicting risks is how real policy choices get made. See [Kritiks](kritiks.md).
- **Framework and role of the ballot.** A team may argue that the judge's vote should first address harms happening in the present. The other team may argue that the judge should weigh all consequences of the plan. See [Framework](framework.md) and [Role of the ballot](role-of-the-ballot.md).

Each of these claims needs reasons. Saying "probability first" or "extinction outweighs" is not enough without saying why.

### Middle grounds

Many good rebuttals do not choose a side in the abstract. They compare the specific chains. How strong is each step? How certain is the structural harm in this case, and how much does the plan change it? A team can also say, "Even if you use their framing, we still win, because..." These **even-if** arguments often decide close rounds.

## How judges differ

Judges really do differ here. Many paradigms say which way they lean. See [Judge paradigms](judge-paradigms.md).

- **Some judges** weigh risk times size, and give large impacts a lot of weight even at low odds.
- **Some judges** discount long chains heavily and prefer impacts with strong evidence that they are happening.
- **Some judges** decide mainly by the framework or ethical argument that one team won.
- **Most judges** say they want the debaters to do the weighing, and they look first at what was argued.

If you are judging, use the weighing the teams argued, not your own preference. In your reason for decision (RFD), name the weighing argument that decided the round.

## Example in flow shorthand

The aff's advantage reduces unequal access to health care. The neg's DA says the plan's cost causes an economic shock that leads to a large war. The 2AC is the second aff speech; the 2NR and 2AR are the last neg and aff speeches. Key: Mag = magnitude, Prob = probability, FW = framework (here, the rule for weighing), Ext = extend, x = times, -> = leads to.

```
ROW 1: THE IMPACTS
2AC: Case: unequal care access
  -> preventable deaths now,
  daily
2NR: Mag: shock -> war ->
  extinction; ends all lives
  (Analyst 21)
2AR: Prob: 4 "could" steps;
  risk is near zero

ROW 2: MORE WEIGHING
2NR: Irreversible: no recovery
  after extinction
2AR: Ongoing: deaths add up
  every year; real now

ROW 3: THE FRAMING
2AC: FW: probability first;
  tiny risks are guesses
2NR: Low risk is not zero;
  risk x size still wins
2AR: Ext FW: a tiny risk is a
  guess; 2NR gave no number
  for the risk

ROW 4: CASE TURNS DA
2AC: Case turns DA: unequal care
  fuels unrest (Scholar 20)
2NR: Unrest card is about
  unrest, not war
2AR: Ext case turns DA (no
  answer to "not war")

ROW 5: DA TURNS CASE
2NR: Extinction turns case: no
  one is left to help
2AR: Their turn needs the DA: a
  near-zero risk turns nothing
```

*How to read it:* each side has a gap. The 2AR never answers that the unrest card is about unrest, not war, so the neg is ahead on the case turn. The 2NR never put a number on the risk, so the aff is ahead on the size of the risk. The framing decides the round: under probability first the aff is likely ahead, and under risk times size the neg is likely ahead.

## Common mistakes

- **Neg or aff: "extinction outweighs" and nothing more.** Fix: explain the chance of the impact, not only its size.
- **Neg or aff: "it is happening now" and nothing more.** Fix: explain how big the harm is and how much the plan changes it.
- **Both: weighing labels, not chains.** Fix: compare the actual steps and the strength of each card.
- **Both: a framing claim with no reason.** Fix: say why probability, or magnitude, should come first.
- **Both: no even-if arguments.** Fix: show that you still win under the other team's framing.
- **Judges: preferring one kind of impact by habit.** Fix: decide by the weighing the teams argued, and say so in your RFD.
