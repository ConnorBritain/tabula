# Impact calculus

> **Plain version.** Most rounds end with each team winning something. The judge then has to compare: is the aff's good result bigger than the neg's bad result? Impact calculus is how debaters help the judge compare. They ask how big each harm is, how likely it is, how soon it happens, and whether it can be undone. The team that explains the comparison best often wins, even if the other team won more arguments.

## Why it matters

Judges do not want to guess. If the aff says "the plan saves lives" and the neg says "the plan causes a recession," the judge needs a reason to prefer one. Impact calculus gives that reason. Without it, the judge must do the weighing alone, and either team might be unhappy with the result.

The last two speeches (2NR and 2AR) should spend real time on it.

## The main measures

| Measure | The question | Example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnitude** | How big is the harm? How many people, how badly? | "A war affects millions; the program cut affects thousands." |
| **Probability** | How likely is it to happen? | "The cuts are written into law; the war is only a risk." |
| **Timeframe** | How soon does it happen? | "The cuts start next year; the conflict is decades away." |
| **Reversibility** | Can it be undone? | "A budget can be fixed later; lives lost cannot." |
| **Scope** | Who is affected, and how widely? | "The harm falls on the whole country, not one town." |

Some debaters also use **severity** (how deep the harm is for each person) as part of magnitude.

### Magnitude

Magnitude is the size of the impact. Big impacts, like a large war or a collapse of the economy, can dominate a round. But a big impact with a weak chain of steps may have a small real risk.

### Probability

Probability is the chance the impact happens. A harm that is very likely can beat a bigger harm that is unlikely. Probability depends on the whole chain: uniqueness, link and internal links. Each weak step lowers it. See [Disadvantages](disadvantages.md).

### Timeframe

Timeframe is when the impact happens. A harm that comes first can matter more, especially if it would stop the other harm from ever happening, or make it worse.

### Reversibility

Some harms can be fixed later. Others cannot. A harm that cannot be undone often gets extra weight.

## Turns case

**Turns case** means your impact makes the other team's impact worse, or stops their plan from working. For example: "The recession from the DA would cut the very funding the plan needs. So the DA turns the case." If true, this is very strong: you win your impact *and* shrink theirs. See [Turns case](turns-case.md).

The other team answers with "case turns the DA" or "no turn": they show the link goes the other way, or not at all.

## Big and unlikely vs small and likely

This is the most common weighing debate. There is no single right answer. It depends on what the teams argue.

- **The case for the big impact:** even a small chance of a huge harm, like a large war, can be worth more than a sure small harm. Multiply size by chance, and the big impact can still be larger.
- **The case for the likely impact:** long chains of "could lead to" make big impacts far less likely than they sound. A harm we can see happening now is more certain. Some judges discount very small risks almost to zero.

Judges differ here, and many paradigms say which way they lean. A good debater makes the argument either way, with reasons, and does not just say "extinction outweighs."

## How to do impact calculus in a speech

1. **Name both impacts.** "Our advantage is X. Their DA is Y."
2. **Pick two or three measures** where you win. You do not need all of them.
3. **Compare directly.** "Even if their impact is bigger, ours is far more likely, because their link card only says 'might.'"
4. **Use "even if."** "Even if you think the DA happens, the case outweighs, because it comes first and cannot be undone."
5. **Tell the judge what to write.** "Vote aff because the case is more likely and comes first."

## Example in flow shorthand

Weighing at the end of a round. The aff's advantage helps a public health program. The neg's DA says the plan's cost causes a recession, and a recession raises the risk of conflict.

```
1AR                      2NR                        2AR
                         Mag: recession ->          Prob: 4-step chain; each
                            conflict, millions         step "could" -> tiny risk
                            at risk
                         TF: recession hits         TF: case saves lives now,
                            in 1 yr                    in yr 1
No turn: plan funded     DA turns case:             Ext set-aside: recession
   by a set-aside;          recession cuts             can't cut it. 2NR never
   recession can't cut      health budget ->           answered it
   it (Expert 22)           case fails
                         Even if risk is small,     Sure, real lives >
                            size wins                  speculative war
```

*How to read it:* the 1AR answers the turn with a set-aside card. The 2NR repeats the turn but never answers that card. In the last speeches, the neg weighs magnitude, timeframe and turns case. The aff weighs probability and timeframe, and extends the set-aside card. If the judge believes the set-aside card, the turn is gone, and the round comes down to big-but-unlikely against sure-but-smaller.

## How to judge weighing

1. **Find the weighing in the last speeches.** List each team's comparisons: magnitude, probability, timeframe, turns case.
2. **Check that each impact was won first.** An impact a team did not win has nothing to weigh.
3. **Resolve the turns.** Did a "turns case" argument survive the answers to it? If not, set it aside.
4. **Compare the comparisons.** If one team said why its measure matters more (for example, "probability first, because tiny risks are guesses"), and the other did not answer, use that.
5. **Only then add your own judgment,** if the teams left a gap. Say so in your RFD.

## Common mistakes

- **No weighing at all.** Fix: in the last speech, compare your impact to theirs out loud.
- **Saying "extinction outweighs" and stopping.** Fix: explain the risk, not just the size.
- **Using every measure.** Fix: pick the two or three where you really win, and go deep.
- **Ignoring the other team's weighing.** Fix: answer it with "even if" arguments.
- **Weighing impacts you have not won.** Fix: first win that your impact is real, then weigh it.
- **Judges: weighing for the debaters when they did it themselves.** Fix: use the comparisons that were argued, before your own.
