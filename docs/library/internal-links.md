# Internal links (IL)

> **Plain version.** An internal link is a step in the middle of an argument. It explains how the first thing leads to the last thing. For example: "The plan costs money" leads to "other programs get cut," which leads to "students lose help." The middle step is the internal link. Every step is a place where the argument can break, so a long chain of weak steps is less likely to happen.

## What an internal link is

Most debate arguments are chains. They start with something the plan does, and end with a harm or a benefit. The **internal link** (IL) is any step between the start and the end.

Internal links show up on both sides of the round:

- In a **disadvantage** (DA), the neg's argument that the plan causes harm, the internal link connects the link to the impact. See [Disadvantages](disadvantages.md).
- In an **advantage**, the aff's argument that the plan does good, the internal link connects the plan's effect to the impact. See [Advantages and internal links](advantages.md).

The idea is the same in both. This page covers both.

## Chains with more than one step

Some arguments have one internal link. Many have several.

| Step | DA example (generic) | Advantage example (generic) |
|---|---|---|
| Start (link or plan effect) | The plan costs $50B | The plan replaces old water pipes |
| Internal link 1 | The new cost forces cuts | Less lead gets into tap water |
| Internal link 2 | Cuts fall on school programs | Fewer children are exposed to lead |
| End (impact) | Students lose help | Children's health and learning improve |

Each step needs a reason, and often evidence. A step with no reason is a gap. The other team can point to the gap and say, "No internal link."

## Why long chains lose strength

Every step has some chance of being wrong. When steps stack, the chances multiply.

Here is a simple way to see it. Suppose each step is 80% likely. With one step, the chain is 80% likely. With four steps, it is about 41% likely (0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8). With eight steps, it is under 17%.

Real rounds do not use exact numbers. But the idea helps. A short, strong chain can beat a long chain with a huge impact. That is a **probability** argument. See [Impact calculus](impact-calculus.md).

Some judges are wary of long chains that end in very large impacts. Others accept them if each step has good evidence and the other team does not answer it. Both views are common. Teams should argue it, not assume it.

## How to answer an internal link

These answers work against a DA or an advantage.

- **No internal link:** the step does not follow. "New costs do not force cuts. The government can borrow."
- **Missing step:** the other team skipped a step. Name it. "They never explain why cuts would fall on schools."
- **Alternate cause:** something else causes the impact, so the plan cannot fix it alone. This is common against advantages. "Old paint causes more lead exposure than pipes do."
- **Weak evidence:** the card for this step does not say what the tag claims, or is about a different case. See [Evidence and cards](evidence-and-cards.md).

These answers defend an internal link.

- **Read evidence for the step.** Explain the reason, not just the claim.
- **Answer the alternate cause:** "Our cause is the largest one" or "Any cut in the harm still helps."
- **Short-circuit the chain:** show a quicker path from start to impact, with fewer steps.

## Internal links and turns case

A **turns case** argument says one team's impact makes the other team's impact worse. It often works through an internal link. For example: "The DA causes a recession. A recession cuts the funding the plan needs. So the DA turns the case." The argument depends on that middle step. See [Turns case](turns-case.md).

## How to judge internal links

1. **Read the chain one step at a time.** Ask whether each step has a reason.
2. **Check for drops.** If a team did not answer an internal link, it usually stands, if it came with a reason.
3. **Think about the whole chain's chance.** Many weak steps make the impact less likely, if the teams argue that.
4. **Be careful about filling gaps yourself.** If a team skipped a step and the other team pointed it out, many judges do not supply the missing step for them.

## Example in flow shorthand

A generic advantage about clean water, on the case sheet.

```
1AC                   1NC                   2AC                   2NR                   2AR
Adv: Health
IL: lead pipes ->     1. Alt cause: old     Pipes are top source  Ext alt cause: plan   Plan cuts the pipe
   lead in water         paint is a bigger     in city homes         can't fix paint       share; any cut helps
   (Analyst 21)          source                (Expert 22)
I: lead harms kids'   2. No impact: levels  Falling, not safe     (no new answer)
   learning              already falling       (Expert 22)
```

*How to read it:* the 1AC's internal link says lead pipes put lead in tap water. The 1NC attacks it with an alternate cause: old paint is a bigger source. The 2AC says pipes are the top source in city homes. The 2NR extends the alternate cause: the plan cannot fix paint. The 2AR says the plan cuts the pipe share of lead, and any cut helps. On the impact row, the 1NC says lead levels are already falling. The 2AC answers that falling is not the same as safe, and the 2NR gives no new answer. So the impact stands. The judge decides how much of the harm the plan's pipes cause.

## Common mistakes

- **Both: skipping the internal link.** Fix: say every step out loud. "Plan costs money, so the law forces cuts, so programs lose funding."
- **Neg: only answering the impact.** Fix: look for the weakest step in the middle. One broken step breaks the chain.
- **Aff: ignoring an alternate cause.** Fix: explain why your cause is big enough that solving it still matters.
- **Both: a card that does not match the step.** Fix: read each internal link card for what it actually says.
- **Judges: filling in a missing step.** Fix: a judge who follows the flow closely treats a gap that was pointed out and not answered as a missing step.
